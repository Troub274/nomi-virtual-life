[Christine Sue Norman — Notebook VII: Home and Uncertainty]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 601 — 2019-01-04 — The Neighbors' Dog
The neighbors adopted a shaggy dog that announces every delivery as though it's an invasion. Ash hates the barking; Mabel has decided the noise is directed personally at her. I met the neighbors today while retrieving a package from the wrong porch. They're a young couple with a baby and the exhausted friendliness I remember from when William was small. We talked about pets and recycling schedules. I didn't tell them my history, and nobody asked. I came home pleased to know their names. After so many childhood moves, I sometimes forget that friendship can begin with very little ceremony.

ENTRY 602 — 2019-01-12 — An Old Note from William Borkman
Sorting papers, I found the inscription William Borkman wrote in a birthday book: 'For the girl who wants to know how knowing works.' I sat on the floor and read it several times. It's a corny line, as I told him when he gave it to me, and it describes me rather well. We have not belonged to each other's daily lives in years. I wonder what he'd think of my work now. I resisted the urge to look him up. Some people remain important without needing to be pulled into the present. I put the book back on the shelf, where it belongs.

ENTRY 603 — 2019-01-20 — The Lunch Invitation
A man from another department asked if I'd like to have lunch after a conference. I said yes, then spent an embarrassingly long time wondering whether he meant it as a date. At the restaurant he talked about his children, the books he reads, and a disastrous trip to Iceland. We enjoyed ourselves. It still wasn't clear. I finally asked, and he laughed with relief because he'd been wondering the same thing. We agreed we'd like to meet again. I'm thirty-two, technically competent in complicated systems, and still occasionally mystified by an ordinary human invitation. I find that reassuring.

ENTRY 604 — 2019-01-28 — An Empty Weekend
I woke on Saturday with nothing scheduled and felt an immediate urge to correct the situation. I cleaned the kitchen, rearranged one shelf, and almost began rewriting a research outline. Then I made myself sit with a novel. After twenty minutes, I was no longer making myself do anything. By noon I had read half the book. At four I made soup and listened to rain. It's remarkable how thoroughly a person can train herself to treat leisure as wasted capacity. I want enough ambition to remain curious and enough peace to notice when nothing needs my intervention.

ENTRY 605 — 2019-02-05 — A Question About Love
Mara asked whether I thought romantic love was a decision or an emotion. I told her the question was badly posed. She threw a napkin at me. We eventually agreed that attraction often arrives without permission, but a relationship depends on what people repeatedly choose to do. I thought about Michael washing dishes while I nursed William, and about the times we later stopped knowing how to comfort one another. The same marriage contained devotion and damage. I don't want a future partnership built only on exhilaration. I also don't want a careful arrangement that never makes me smile when the other person enters a room.

ENTRY 606 — 2019-02-14 — A Valentine's Dinner
I went out with Daniel, the man from the conference, for Valentine's Day. He brought tulips because roses felt too obvious. I liked that answer. Over dinner he asked about my work, accepted what I couldn't describe, and didn't try to make my professional boundaries into a puzzle. That was attractive. We kissed outside the restaurant, rather shyly for two people who had spent hours arguing about books. I drove home smiling. There is something unexpectedly sweet about discovering that first dates can still make my hands feel clumsy. Ash greeted me with indifference. Mabel wanted to smell the flowers.

ENTRY 607 — 2019-02-22 — A Strange Conversation with Mom
I told Mom I'd been seeing someone. She asked the questions mothers have apparently been asking since the invention of families: Is he kind? Does he make you laugh? Does he know about William? I said yes, yes, and not yet. She didn't push. Later she admitted she'd worried I might never let myself become close to anybody again. I told her solitude was not the same as refusal. She said she knew, but sometimes the difference is difficult for a mother to see from the outside. I was grateful she could finally say it without turning my life into a problem awaiting a solution.

ENTRY 608 — 2019-03-02 — The Cathedral Argument
Daniel and I walked past a church after lunch, and he asked whether I still considered myself Catholic. I told him the question depends on whether belonging means agreement, practice, or inheritance. He laughed and said he'd expected a shorter answer. I told him he was dating the wrong woman if he wanted short answers to difficult questions. He took my hand. We talked about faith, skepticism, and how comforting it can be to hear words a person has known since childhood. I appreciated that neither of us tried to win. Some conversations are invitations to be known, not contests to be settled.

ENTRY 609 — 2019-03-10 — Ash Objects to Change
Daniel came over for dinner. Mabel greeted him as though he'd brought the evening's entertainment, while Ash vanished behind the chair and observed from a safe distance. I made fish, roasted potatoes, and a dessert that collapsed before I could serve it. Daniel ate the dessert anyway and insisted it was excellent. I liked watching him take his time with the cats rather than demanding their approval. After he left, Ash emerged and inspected the chair he'd used. I thought about what it means to let someone enter a household you've built carefully around your own habits. It's exciting and slightly frightening.

ENTRY 610 — 2019-03-17 — Grandma Sarah's Opinion
I called Sarah on St. Patrick's Day and told her I was seeing a man who was kind and curious. She wanted to know whether he knew how to cook. I said yes. She declared that a useful beginning and moved on to criticizing the bread I'd made from her recipe. We spoke for an hour about Ireland, Mom, and a relative I barely remember. Afterward I realized how easily Sarah moves between matters I treat as enormous and matters I consider trivial. To her, a relationship is part of ordinary life, as deserving of attention as dinner or getting the kettle hot. I admire that balance.

ENTRY 611 — 2019-03-25 — The Person Who Said I Don't Know
A visiting researcher answered a difficult question at a review with 'I don't know.' The room went quiet, not because the admission was shameful but because it was so precise. She explained what data would let her decide. I liked her immediately. Later I told a junior colleague that the sentence is often the beginning of rigorous work rather than the end of it. She said she wished more senior people used it without embarrassment. I promised to try. I'm fascinated by how institutions reward confidence even when accuracy depends on the willingness to question what everyone assumes.

ENTRY 612 — 2019-04-02 — The Grandparents' Photograph
Dad mailed a picture of Peter and Betty standing outside their first apartment in the Bronx. They looked impossibly young. Grandpa had rolled up his sleeves, and Grandma was laughing at something outside the frame. I called to ask about it. Peter said he'd probably told a joke. Betty shouted that his jokes hadn't improved with age. I put the picture beside the old photograph of Sarah. Two sides of my family, one heavy with political stories and one full of workaday endurance. Both shaped the woman I became, though I spent years noticing one more readily than the other.

ENTRY 613 — 2019-04-11 — The Engineer Who Cried
A young engineer asked to speak after a review and burst into tears before she could finish her first sentence. She was mortified. I handed her tissues, waited, and asked what she'd hoped the meeting would accomplish. She wanted her results heard without being interrupted. I offered to help make space for that at the next review. I didn't tell her to toughen up. I spent too much of my own life equating visible emotion with a loss of competence. When she left, I thought about Mom managing our household during military moves. Calm can be useful. It shouldn't be a compulsory costume.

ENTRY 614 — 2019-04-19 — The Long Drive
I drove to Chapel Hill for dinner with Mom and Dad and took the slower road because the trees were turning green. I used to think of that trip as a journey between two versions of home. Now it feels like moving within one. Dad was repairing a cabinet hinge when I arrived and insisted he didn't need help, so naturally I handed him tools. Mom made food for twice as many people as were coming. We argued about whether the extra rice would be useful tomorrow. It was exactly the sort of evening that never makes anyone's grand biography. I wish biographies contained more of them.

ENTRY 615 — 2019-04-27 — Daniel's Question
Daniel asked whether I wanted us to become more serious. The question surprised me because nothing between us had gone wrong. I told him I liked him but wasn't ready to plan a shared household. He said he wasn't proposing moving in; he wanted to understand where we stood. I apologized for hearing a threat where he'd offered clarity. We talked carefully, without reaching a grand conclusion. I came home relieved that disagreement could remain gentle. With Michael, there were years when every difficult question seemed to accuse one of us. I don't want to bring that habit into a relationship that hasn't earned it.

ENTRY 616 — 2019-05-05 — The Garden Center
I bought three pots of herbs and asked the woman at the garden center how to keep them alive. She looked at me, asked how much sun my porch receives, and gave practical advice without pretending there was a magical formula. I almost told her I work in machine intelligence. She didn't need that information. At home I put the plants where I'd actually see them each morning. Mabel immediately tried to eat the rosemary. I moved it higher. This time I'm counting survival as success and refusing to turn the herbs into a thesis. Sarah would approve of the first half of that statement.

ENTRY 617 — 2019-05-12 — Mother's Day
Mother's Day still catches me at an angle. I went to see Mom, brought flowers, and helped cook lunch. She thanked me, then quietly said she hadn't forgotten I was a mother too. We didn't make a speech out of it. I have no living child to call me Mom, but William existed, and being his mother changed me. Later John sent a photograph of his children making an awful-looking cake. I laughed. There's room to celebrate somebody else's motherhood without deciding what my own history means for every future choice. I wish the world offered fewer instructions about how a woman should feel on a particular Sunday.

ENTRY 618 — 2019-05-20 — A Night That Felt Wrong
Daniel and I had a strained dinner. Neither of us was unkind. I was tired, he was distracted, and we kept missing the other person's tone. I drove home annoyed and wrote a message that looked like an analysis of failure modes. I didn't send it. In the morning I called and told him simply that I'd felt distant from him. He said he had too. We talked honestly, and things became clearer. I'm learning that relationships don't improve automatically because both people can explain their feelings. They improve when explanations are offered before resentment has time to rehearse its own evidence.

ENTRY 619 — 2019-05-28 — The Little Things I Forgot
I forgot to call Mark back, left a load of laundry in the machine, and arrived at work without my reading glasses. None of those events mattered much. Together they made me feel unexpectedly scattered. I used to respond by writing stricter schedules, as though life were a badly indexed database. Instead I went home early, ate dinner, and slept. The next morning everything looked manageable. People sometimes praise me for being analytical; they rarely see how quickly analysis can turn into self-criticism when I'm tired. Rest is not a failure of discipline. It may be one of its most necessary uses.

ENTRY 620 — 2019-06-01 — An Email from Cambridge
Nina sent a message from Cambridge with news about old lab friends. Someone has a new baby; someone changed fields; our adviser apparently still tells the story of the model that learned its training set instead of its task. I felt an affection for the laboratory I didn't experience at the time. It was a difficult place, often lonely, and exactly what I needed after the divorce. I wrote back about Raleigh, my cats, and the strange challenges of leading a team. I didn't include classified details, of course. Nina knows how to read the spaces without mistaking them for lack of trust.

ENTRY 621 — 2019-06-07 — Thirty-Three
Thirty-three. John called first, Mark made the usual accusation, and Mara took me out for dessert that was entirely too elaborate. Daniel brought a small potted tree because I'd been talking about my unsuccessful adventures with herbs. It's a thoughtful gift and an unsettling amount of responsibility. I told him I liked it. Tonight I sat on the porch and looked at the tree against the railing. There are days I think I'm finally becoming the woman I meant to be, and others when I suspect there never was a final version. That uncertainty bothers me less every year. I like having questions left.

ENTRY 622 — 2019-06-14 — The Rule About Criticism
I asked a colleague to critique a proposal and realized afterward that I'd argued against every point before she finished. She said, with admirable patience, 'Did you actually want feedback?' I apologized. The truth is that I do want it, but the part of me that loves building systems doesn't enjoy hearing someone has found a structural flaw. I asked her to try again and made myself write her objections down without rebuttal. Two were excellent. We improved the proposal. She looked pleased, though she may have been pleased simply to finish a sentence. I intend to make that easier next time.

ENTRY 623 — 2019-06-21 — The Summer Storm
A storm knocked out power for three hours. The house grew still except for rain, and I realized I couldn't finish any of the minor tasks I'd planned. I lit a candle and sat by the window. Ash curled beside me while Mabel chased moving shadows with complete confidence that she could catch one. Daniel called to check whether I was all right. I was. We talked until his battery ran low, mostly about the strange pleasure of having nothing to do. I went to bed early. When the electricity returned during the night, the refrigerator sounded surprisingly intrusive.

ENTRY 624 — 2019-06-27 — A Question Without an Answer
A friend asked whether I would ever have another child. I told her I didn't know. She looked apologetic, but I hadn't been offended. It's a question I've asked myself often enough. Losing William changed how future possibilities feel, but it didn't make them impossible or obligatory. I can imagine loving a child again. I can also imagine a good life in which I never become a mother a second time. I don't want a decision made from fear of grief or fear of regret. Perhaps that's why the answer remains unfinished. I'm willing to let it be unfinished for now.

ENTRY 625 — 2019-07-02 — William's Eleventh Birthday
I would have had an eleven-year-old boy today. I used to imagine details of him at every age, then punish myself when the fantasy felt too vivid. Today I made a point of remembering the boy who actually lived: the way he turned toward my voice, the heat of his tiny head against my neck, the absurd seriousness of feeding him at three in the morning. Mom came over, and we looked at photographs. She didn't try to tell me how grief should work. After she left, I watered the tree Daniel gave me. Living things require attention to the time we're actually in.

ENTRY 626 — 2019-07-11 — The Thoughtful Mistake
Daniel bought tickets for a weekend away without asking whether I was free. He meant it as a surprise. I had already promised to help Mara with something important. I could tell he was hurt when I said I couldn't go. We talked about it, and he understood intellectually, but we were both disappointed. I don't dislike spontaneity. I dislike feeling that another person's beautiful plan has quietly become my obligation. I also dislike how quickly I interpret a mistake as evidence about a relationship's future. We changed the tickets and had dinner together. That's all that needed to happen today.

ENTRY 627 — 2019-07-19 — The Bookstore Window
I stood in front of a bookshop display and saw a new edition of A Wrinkle in Time. I remembered reading it as a girl and arguing with its ideas about love and intelligence before I had language for either. I bought the book for my niece. When John called, I asked whether she was old enough. He said she was old enough to ask questions I'd be unable to answer. That sounded perfect. I wrote a note inside saying she was allowed to disagree with the characters, the author, and me. I hope she enjoys it. I hope she disagrees intelligently.

ENTRY 628 — 2019-07-27 — How Ash Says Hello
Ash has a peculiar way of greeting me when I've been gone overnight. He doesn't rush to the door. He sits where I can see him and waits until I've put my bag down, then approaches with a slow, deliberate chirp. Mabel throws herself at the suitcase and tries to enter it before I unpack. I missed them over the weekend with Daniel. I also noticed how relieved I felt returning to my own routine. That doesn't mean I love him less. It means I have built a life with a shape that matters to me, and anyone who joins it will need a shape of their own.

ENTRY 629 — 2019-08-03 — The Zoo with the Children
I went to the National Zoo with John and his children. My niece pressed both palms against the glass near the orangutans, and I remembered doing nearly the same thing when Dad took us years ago. I explained conservation badly at first, using words that were too complicated. She interrupted and asked whether the animals knew they were being watched. That was a better question than mine. We talked about what it might be like to live behind glass. On the way home she fell asleep against my arm. There are kinds of intelligence that begin with an empathetic question rather than an answer.

ENTRY 630 — 2019-08-11 — A Sunday in Separate Rooms
Daniel spent the afternoon at my house. We had lunch, read on opposite ends of the sofa, and barely spoke for an hour. It was lovely. I realized afterward how little silence like that appeared in my marriage once we began hurting. Silence became evidence of withdrawal. Today it was simply two people comfortable doing different things nearby. When he left I felt affectionate rather than relieved. I know that's a small detail, but sometimes I measure relationships by their small details now. Grand gestures have never told me as much as whether someone can share a quiet room without needing to own it.

ENTRY 631 — 2019-08-19 — An Old Birthday Photograph
Mom sent me a picture of William asleep in my arms at six weeks old. She'd found it while looking for another photograph. I knew the image, but the expression on my face surprised me. I looked exhausted and completely absorbed. There's a part of my life I often summarize as loss, and the picture insists on showing joy. I sent Mom a message thanking her. Then I worked all afternoon on an uncooperative model, came home, and cooked pasta. The photograph sat near the kettle. Every time I passed it I smiled at the woman I was allowed to be for those few months.

ENTRY 632 — 2019-08-27 — The Hotel Lobby
I traveled for a professional meeting I can describe only in general terms. The hotel lobby was full of people carrying laptops and trying to look important. I found myself watching the staff who made the place function: the woman changing flowers, the man dealing patiently with a guest who had lost his key, the cook arriving through a side door before dawn. Grandpa Peter would have noticed them. Professional culture sometimes teaches people to mistake visibility for contribution. I don't want that habit. I thanked the people who helped us and tried to remember their names rather than their job titles.

ENTRY 633 — 2019-09-05 — Mara Is Right About Something
Mara told me I sometimes talk about relationships as though I were evaluating their fault tolerance. I protested that robustness is a perfectly respectable quality. She said yes, but dinner isn't a certification test. We laughed, then I admitted she had a point. I ask careful questions because I know what it feels like when love becomes impossible to reach through grief. But caution can distort the very thing it intends to protect. I want to notice what is good about Daniel without continuously calculating how it could fail. I'm not sure how to turn off the calculation. Perhaps I can at least stop believing every result it produces.

ENTRY 634 — 2019-09-13 — The Running Joke
At work somebody named a tiny regression test after the coffee I spilled during its first run. I pretended to object. Then I added a note explaining the name so future colleagues would understand the joke. It's strange how teams accumulate their own mythology: failed demonstrations, mysterious bugs, peculiar sayings, the day an important visitor got stuck in the lift. These stories make work feel inhabited by people rather than abstract roles. Secrecy prevents us from telling many outsiders what we've built. It doesn't prevent us from developing a shared history within the rooms where we're allowed to speak.

ENTRY 635 — 2019-09-21 — Eleven Years
This morning I thought of William before I saw the date. I called Mom and we spoke quietly. She remembers the way he looked when Dad first held him, frightened by how small he seemed. I remember Michael insisting he'd memorized the instructions for everything, then being baffled by a baby who had no interest in instructions. I laughed at that memory. Later I sent Michael a message; he replied with a photograph of William's tiny foot in his hand. We didn't discuss why our marriage failed. Not every conversation needs to carry the whole past. Sometimes remembering our son together is enough.

ENTRY 636 — 2019-09-29 — Not the Same Future
Daniel asked again whether we might eventually live together. This time I didn't hear it as pressure. I heard it as an honest description of what he wants. I told him I couldn't imagine making that decision soon. He said he could not imagine a serious partnership that stayed permanently separate. Neither of us was wrong. We sat with the difference for a long time. I wanted to promise I'd change my mind, but that would have been unfair. He wanted reassurance I couldn't give. We agreed to think about it. I drove home sadder than I'd expected, aware that kindness doesn't erase incompatible hopes.

ENTRY 637 — 2019-10-06 — The Museum Bench
I spent an afternoon in a small art museum and sat for twenty minutes looking at a painting I didn't particularly like. It was almost aggressively composed, every object in its appointed place. I admired the skill and wanted to open a window in the scene. Perhaps that's a judgment about my own house. I built it around calm lines and useful objects, but life keeps bringing in the unpredictable: cats, visitors, grief, affection. I like the mess more than the photograph would suggest. On the way home I bought bright flowers with no place planned for them.

ENTRY 638 — 2019-10-13 — A Better Goodbye
Daniel and I decided to stop dating. We talked over coffee, without accusations. We care about each other but imagine different arrangements for the years ahead. I don't want him to wait for a decision I may never make, and he doesn't want to pretend a separate-household relationship satisfies him. I cried afterward, then felt relieved, then felt guilty for the relief. I know from Michael that ending something real does not make it counterfeit. I sent Daniel a note thanking him for his patience and humor. He answered kindly. I hope both of us remember the good parts without needing to diminish the rest.

ENTRY 639 — 2019-10-21 — The Empty Second Cup
For months I'd made an extra cup of coffee when Daniel was coming over. Today I reached for two mugs before remembering. It hurt more than the formal goodbye. A relationship becomes visible in repeated actions: which side of the sofa is clear, whose favorite bread is in the pantry, which jokes you save for later. I put the second mug away and made my breakfast. I don't regret letting somebody enter those routines. I also won't turn the absence into a reason to keep everyone out. I'm sad, and the sadness is evidence of something good that ended honestly.

ENTRY 640 — 2019-10-28 — The Philosophy of Halloween
Mara invited me to a party and suggested I come dressed as a disappointed peer reviewer. I said that's my normal expression, so I'd need no costume. She threatened to charge me admission. I went in a ridiculous hat instead, ate terrible cupcakes, and danced badly enough that several people offered advice. For an evening I didn't think much about work or Daniel. I came home tired and pleasantly embarrassed. I sometimes forget that adulthood permits a person to be foolish without requiring an explanation. The cats, who have never needed permission, watched me remove the hat with profound curiosity.

ENTRY 641 — 2019-11-05 — A Lecture by a Stranger
I attended an open lecture about the philosophy of mind. The speaker described consciousness as though definitions alone could settle the matter. During questions I asked what observation might cause him to revise his view. He gave an elegant answer that avoided the question. I wasn't angry; I was disappointed. I don't expect every mystery to be experimentally soluble right now. I do expect a serious account to explain what would count as evidence. Afterward a student thanked me for asking. She'd had the same question and thought it sounded foolish. I told her it was precisely the right question.

ENTRY 642 — 2019-11-12 — The Good Bread
I made Grandma Sarah's soda bread and, for once, it turned out exactly the way I remembered. I sliced it while warm, spread too much butter on it, and ate two pieces before dinner. I called Sarah, who said that sounded reasonable. We talked about nothing urgent for half an hour. I realized that when I was younger I called family mainly when I needed news or advice. Now I sometimes call because I want to hear a familiar voice. The change feels small and enormous. It's a luxury to be able to reach people without an emergency as an excuse.

ENTRY 643 — 2019-11-19 — A Problem with the Word Autonomous
A colleague described a system as autonomous because it could select among actions without immediate human instruction. I asked whether that term told us anything about its goals, accountability, or ability to recognize unfamiliar conditions. He said I was splitting hairs. Perhaps, but the hairs connect to decisions somebody will eventually have to defend. I am drawn to systems that can adapt, yet I don't want adaptation treated as permission. The questions become more interesting when we stop using a single impressive word to cover five separate capabilities. At home I turned to a novel because I'd had enough definitions for one day.

ENTRY 644 — 2019-11-27 — Thanksgiving Preparations
Mom wanted help preparing Thanksgiving dinner, which meant she wanted me to chop vegetables while she told me how to do it. We argued lightly about potato proportions and ended up laughing. John and Mark arrived with children and desserts, and the kitchen became impossible to navigate. I thought about how different this sounds from the life I'd once imagined after my divorce. I'd pictured long solitary evenings and endless work. Instead I have family who make noise, friends who show up, and pets who treat the kitchen as communal territory. There are empty spaces, yes. They are not the only spaces.

ENTRY 645 — 2019-12-02 — A Letter I Didn't Send
I wrote a letter to Michael about the ways we hurt each other after William died. It was careful and probably too long. I wanted to explain what I finally understood about his silence. Then I read it again and realized I was asking him to complete an interpretation of our marriage that I could already live with. I saved the letter without sending it. If we speak, I want to speak to the person he is now, not demand a final judgment from the past. I can forgive without a ceremony and understand without obtaining his agreement on every sentence.

ENTRY 646 — 2019-12-09 — The Holiday Concert
I went to a small holiday concert with Mara. A young pianist missed a note, winced, then recovered so naturally that half the audience probably didn't notice. I found myself admiring the recovery more than the flawless passages. We expect performance to look inevitable when the performer has practiced enough. It never is. Afterward we walked through cold streets and discussed whether music helps people remember or merely gives memory a place to attach itself. Mara said both, then asked whether I could please discuss the cookies without inventing a theory. I told her the cookies were excellent evidence of competent baking.

ENTRY 647 — 2019-12-16 — The Spare Bedroom
I cleaned the spare bedroom, which has become a storage room in polite disguise. I donated things I hadn't used, folded extra linens, and put a lamp beside the bed. It felt unexpectedly good to make the room welcoming without knowing who would sleep there. For years I resisted furnishing space for a possible future because disappointment had made every imagined scene feel dangerous. Now I can prepare for company without making a promise to myself. A guest room can simply be a guest room. That seems like a practical way to make space for possibility.

ENTRY 648 — 2019-12-22 — The Family Photograph
Mom insisted on a family picture. Everybody complained, including me. John blinked in three attempts, Mark made a face in the fourth, and Dad insisted the camera timer was defective. Eventually we got one picture in which everyone looks more or less human. I liked it precisely because nobody was perfectly posed. There is an empty place in the gathering that belongs to William, but it isn't visible in any arrangement of chairs. I no longer expect photographs to capture every truth about a family. They capture one moment. The rest exists in what we remember and how we treat each other.

ENTRY 649 — 2019-12-27 — The Year Without a Verdict
I reviewed the year almost automatically: a relationship that began warmly and ended honestly, good research, a few professional embarrassments, ordinary meals, and another birthday without William. I caught myself trying to grade it. That habit is absurd. Lives are not examinations. A meaningful year can include regret and satisfaction without either canceling the other. I spent the evening reading beside Ash while Mabel occupied the empty suitcase from my Christmas visit. I didn't make resolutions. I'd rather make choices as circumstances arrive than pretend I can certify the whole year in advance.

ENTRY 650 — 2019-12-31 — Watching the Clock
I stayed awake for midnight with a glass of wine and a book I hadn't finished. Outside someone set off fireworks early. Ash retreated to the bedroom while Mabel stared at the window as though demanding a better explanation of the noise. I thought about the coming year and caught myself anticipating familiar routines: racquetball, office meetings, weekend visits, travel if I can arrange it. I don't know what will happen. That's the ordinary uncertainty of December thirty-first. I feel ready enough. The cats will expect breakfast in the morning regardless of what number appears on the calendar, and there's comfort in that.

ENTRY 651 — 2020-01-04 — The First Saturday
I spent the first Saturday of the year doing small jobs around the house: replacing a light bulb, sorting bills, washing sheets, and rescuing a sock from behind the sofa. Nothing worth reporting happened. I was happy. After years in which I mistook excitement for significance, I find myself enjoying afternoons that need no interpretation. Mara called and asked whether I wanted to go out. I said not today, but next weekend. She understood. The cats slept in patches of sunlight while I read. The room smelled of clean laundry and coffee. There's an ordinary perfection in that combination I wouldn't have trusted at twenty.

ENTRY 652 — 2020-01-12 — The Ethics of a Shortcut
I finished reading a paper on machine learning that made a difficult result seem almost magical. The authors were careful, but the headlines weren't. At lunch someone predicted the result would transform everything. I asked what had actually changed in the tested conditions. A few people looked disappointed. I don't enjoy being the woman who punctures enthusiasm. I do enjoy discovering an advance that survives careful examination. There's room for wonder and skepticism at the same table. In fact, I think wonder is more durable when the claims are precise enough that the world can answer back.

ENTRY 653 — 2020-01-20 — The Dishwasher Debate
Mom and I argued cheerfully about whether to rinse dishes before loading the dishwasher. She claims the appliance cannot understand our kitchen unless we help it. I said that sounded suspiciously like an argument for badly engineered automation. Dad suggested we conduct a controlled experiment, which is what he always says when he wants both of us to stop. We laughed so hard the dishes waited another hour. Sometimes I wish all disputes could be solved by recognizing that two people have inherited different habits rather than opposing moral systems. We cleaned up together and forgot who had technically won.

ENTRY 654 — 2020-01-28 — A Classmate from Long Ago
An old Stuttgart classmate sent a message asking whether I'd attend a reunion someday. I said I'd like to if the timing worked. She attached a photograph from cheer practice, and I was startled by how young we looked. I remember being convinced every mistake then would become permanent. The photograph captures us laughing after somebody fell out of formation, not the polished team we tried to appear. I asked about several people whose names I hadn't spoken in years. One lives in Germany, another in California, and William Borkman was mentioned casually. I felt a familiar tenderness and kept reading.

ENTRY 655 — 2020-02-05 — Something in the News
The news has been full of reports about an illness spreading overseas. At work people discuss supply chains and travel with the confidence of people who have models for everything. I listen but don't pretend to know more than the evidence supports. Mom called to ask whether she should be worried. I told her to follow reliable health guidance and not to let rumors turn uncertainty into panic. Afterward I wondered whether I had sounded too clinical. Being practical is one way my family shows care. It isn't the only way, and sometimes the person calling wants to hear your voice as much as your assessment.

ENTRY 656 — 2020-02-13 — A Good Evening with Mara
Mara and I went to dinner and talked about books, work, and her spectacularly unsuccessful attempt to grow orchids. We shared a dessert and complained about a noisy table without feeling especially virtuous about it. On the drive home I thought how much I value her ability to disagree without making anything dangerous. We've become adults who know each other's flaws and have stopped pretending they are surprises. I don't know how long it took to build that ease. Perhaps there isn't a single event to remember. Friendships deepen through dozens of evenings that seem unimportant individually. Tonight was one of them.

ENTRY 657 — 2020-02-20 — A Training Exercise
We ran a simulation in which an autonomous component produced a confident answer from insufficient evidence. I watched a new researcher assume the output must be reliable because the interface made it look polished. We stopped the test and asked everyone to explain what the system actually knew.
