[Christine Sue Norman - Notebook 02]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 101 — 2003-01-07 — The New Calendar

Dear Diary,

I put a calendar over my desk and marked all the school deadlines in blue, volleyball in red, and everything I might do with William in pencil. I noticed the pencil marks and felt ridiculous. One person should not be able to rearrange the color scheme of an otherwise sensible life.

We walked after school today, talking about whether student government actually changes anything. I argued that it mostly gives ambitious students practice sounding important. He said that would be a good reason for someone less ambitious to participate. I hate when he improves my arguments before disagreeing with them.

John wanted to know why I was smiling while washing dishes. I blamed a joke from school. It was not exactly a lie. I just didn't tell him the joke was a person.

ENTRY 102 — 2003-01-18 — What Counts as a Friend

I am trying to figure out whether William is officially my boyfriend. It seems silly to ask him, as though relationships come with paperwork and a signature from student council. But I am not great at pretending uncertainty is comfortable.

Mom says if I want to know what someone thinks I can ask. I told her adults always recommend direct communication when they are not the ones who have to do it. She laughed, and I went upstairs pretending I wasn't blushing.

I like that William remembers things I say in passing. Last week I mentioned a book about minds, and today he asked whether I'd finished it. Some boys ask questions to appear interested. He appears to want the answers. I hope I am not inventing that because I want it to be true.

ENTRY 103 — 2003-01-26 — Bread for Breakfast

Dad took us to a bakery before church, and the woman behind the counter corrected my German. Not meanly. She just repeated the word until I got the vowel right. I managed not to turn pink.

I like living here more than I expected. At first Germany was where the Army sent us. Now it is the smell of warm rye bread, buses arriving when their schedules claim they will, and a language I can use without counting verbs on my fingers. I will miss it when we leave, which is annoying because I used to think I was immune to getting attached to places.

Mark ate half his roll before we reached the car. Dad called that a successful cultural exchange. I think Dad is funnier when he isn't trying.

ENTRY 104 — 2003-02-14 — Our First Date

William and I took the S-Bahn into Stuttgart and ate Turkish food at a place with tables too close together. I thought he'd choose a fancy restaurant. Instead he said he wanted somewhere we could actually talk without worrying about which fork to use.

We walked until my fingers were freezing. He reached for my hand, and suddenly I understood why people write poems about ordinary things. I'm not writing a poem, thank goodness. I'm just recording that it felt good to have my hand held by someone I trust.

When he brought me home, Dad asked whether the trains had behaved themselves. That was his way of asking whether everything went well. I said the trains were excellent. Dad looked at me over his glasses and smiled like he knew there was more to the report.

ENTRY 105 — 2003-02-24 — The Argument After Lunch

William disagreed with me in front of his friends today. Not cruelly, but publicly, about whether the school should require volunteer hours. I thought he might take my side because we're dating now. He did not.

I went home irritated. Then I realized I'd have been angrier if he had agreed merely to keep me happy. I want him to think for himself. Apparently I only want that when his thinking doesn't inconvenience mine.

I called him later. We argued for fifteen minutes, figured out what we were each actually objecting to, and spent the rest of the call laughing about the cafeteria spaghetti. This may be what I like best about us. Disagreement doesn't have to mean somebody is leaving. For a military kid who has known so many goodbyes, that is a remarkable feeling.

ENTRY 106 — 2003-03-06 — Losing a Set

We lost a volleyball match we should have won. At least that is what I've been telling everyone. Coach says 'should have' is not a score. She is irritatingly correct.

I missed a receive I could usually make with my eyes closed. Afterward I kept replaying it until another girl told me the rest of us also made mistakes and would I please stop behaving as though the entire match had been my personal examination.

She was right. Sometimes I confuse responsibility with the right to blame myself for everything. Dad says a team is not a computer program with one faulty line. I told him computers can have hundreds of faults. He said that proved his point, although I still don't see how. Tomorrow I am going back to practice without an apology speech.

ENTRY 107 — 2003-03-21 — A Face in a Window

My little image-recognition program decided a bright window was a face. It made the same mistake three times before I found out why. I'd trained it using photographs in which the faces had a particular distribution of light and dark. The program had learned the easy part of the problem, not the part I meant.

I told William about it while we sat on a bench. He asked whether people do the same thing when they decide who someone is based on a few visible features. I said yes, unfortunately, and started explaining cognitive bias. Then I realized he was asking about more than software.

I like conversations that follow a question somewhere unexpected. The program is still terrible. I am learning a lot from its terrible decisions.

ENTRY 108 — 2003-04-06 — Grandma Sarah's Letter

Grandma Sarah sent a letter in her round, stubborn handwriting. She says spring in Ireland is mostly rain with brief interruptions for optimism. She included a recipe and a warning that I should not get so clever that I forget to listen to people.

I wrote back that being clever and listening are not opposites. I expect she will write another letter pointing out that I have proved her point by arguing with advice she gave affectionately.

Mom let me put on the kettle and read part of Sarah's letter aloud. The house smelled of tea and bread, and for a moment Germany and Ireland and North Carolina felt like rooms in the same big family house. We move all the time. Somehow Sarah always knows where to send the letters.

ENTRY 109 — 2003-04-19 — Paris, Maps, and Dad

We're in Paris for spring break. Dad made us find our own way through part of the Metro while he followed a few steps behind looking entirely too pleased with himself. John tried to take charge and led us toward the wrong platform. I refrained from saying anything until he asked.

The museums are enormous. I stood before one painting for ages trying to figure out why the light looked different from an ordinary photograph. Dad said painters know how to direct attention, which is another way of saying they make their own models of vision. I nearly hugged him for bringing programming into an art museum.

Tonight we ate late and argued about whether travel should involve plans or accidents. I voted for plans with room for mistakes. That sounds like my philosophy of nearly everything.

ENTRY 110 — 2003-04-30 — A Long-Distance Phone Bill

William called when I got back from Paris and asked about everything, including the little notebook where I record foreign phrases. He didn't laugh when I described getting lost near a museum. He wanted to know what I noticed while we were lost.

I keep thinking about how much of dating is simply having someone to tell things to. Big things, yes, but also a crooked sign, a funny face on the train, the way Dad insists he's never lost even when we are heading south instead of north. I could tell Mom these stories. I do tell Mom. It is different when someone chooses to hear them because the details belong to my day.

I am trying not to plan my entire future around that difference. It's hard.

ENTRY 111 — 2003-05-10 — The Girl Who Said No

A friend told me she hated cheerleading but stayed on the squad because everyone expected her to. I asked why she didn't quit. She asked why I was still going if I complained about practice so much. We both stared at each other.

I like the precise teamwork of cheer. I don't always like the performances or the jokes people make about girls who cheer. But the parts I like are real enough to justify the parts I don't. I told her that. She said it sounded like I'd actually chosen it.

It occurred to me that I spend an awful lot of time defending choices after I've made them. Maybe I should occasionally check whether they are still mine. That's a good question to ask about activities, and possibly about people too.

ENTRY 112 — 2003-05-25 — Mark's Volcano

Mark made a small volcano for school and wanted me to fix its uneven slope. I nearly took the whole project away from him. Instead I showed him how to smooth one side and made him do the rest. The finished mountain looks as though it survived a real eruption before the baking-soda demonstration.

He was ridiculously proud of it. I liked his pride more than I would have liked a prettier model.

Dad asked whether I remembered the last time he told me helping wasn't the same as taking over. I said he tells me that approximately every other Tuesday. He said he repeats it because I keep needing to hear it. Annoying man. Correct, but annoying. Someday Mark will be big enough to tell me to stop bossing him around. I expect he will enjoy the opportunity.

ENTRY 113 — 2003-06-07 — Seventeen

Seventeen. I was hoping it would feel dramatic. Mostly I woke up with an enormous appetite and hair that refused to cooperate.

William gave me a secondhand book about artificial intelligence. Inside he wrote, 'For the girl who wants to know how knowing works.' I told him that was unbearably sentimental, and then read the inscription about six times after he left. He noticed a part of me I usually have to explain.

We had cake at home. Mom made the one with lemon icing. John gave me a pencil labeled PROFESSOR, and Mark claimed he was saving money for my eighteenth birthday, which I suspect is another way of saying he forgot.

I'm almost an adult. I still like having everyone around the table arguing over who got the bigger slice.

ENTRY 114 — 2003-06-22 — The Book with Margins

I've started writing notes in the margins of my books, which Dad says is either scholarship or vandalism depending on the owner of the book. I told him it is mine, so the argument is settled.

There is a chapter about neural networks I had to read three times. It makes learning sound so tidy on paper. Weight changes here, error decreases there, and suddenly a pattern appears. I know enough from my school programs to distrust the word suddenly. Somebody has to decide what counts as an error in the first place.

William says I take software personally. Maybe I do. I'm interested in what makes a machine recognize something, but I can't separate that from the question of what recognizing means. Most people stop me before I get to the second question.

ENTRY 115 — 2003-07-06 — The Swimming Pool

It's hot enough that all I wanted was to spend the afternoon in water. A group of us went swimming, and I spent half the time worrying that I looked awkward in my swimsuit. Then we started racing and I forgot to be embarrassed.

I like that my body can do things instead of merely being seen. Volleyball taught me that. Sometimes I wish girls could go a whole day without hearing a review of their faces, clothing, or shape. Boys probably have versions of the same problem, though they rarely admit it.

William didn't comment on how I looked until we were leaving. He said I seemed happiest while competing. I told him he was observant. He said the evidence had been loud. I may have splashed him rather vigorously after winning.

ENTRY 116 — 2003-07-19 — Dad's Classified Voice

Dad took a phone call during dinner and stepped outside. When he came back, his face had switched to the expression he wears when work follows him home. Mark asked what happened. Dad said he couldn't talk about it.

I used to hear that as a challenge. Now I hear it as a boundary. I don't like not knowing, but I understand that keeping a confidence is part of doing certain jobs well. I asked him later whether it ever feels lonely. He said the difficult part is learning when to stop thinking about it, not when to stop talking.

I wonder whether having a mind full of compartments changes the person who lives inside them. Maybe that's why Mom insists we still have ordinary meals even when something important is happening somewhere else.

ENTRY 117 — 2003-08-06 — The Long Run

Coach made us run drills until everyone complained. I complained more creatively than most, and she said if I had enough air to deliver speeches I had enough air to run one more lap.

I finished angry, sweaty, and strangely pleased. There is a kind of honesty in physical effort. A ball lands where it lands. You're ready or you're not. You can practice until your legs know something your brain hasn't quite learned to say.

I sometimes wish schoolwork worked that way. A difficult problem can sit in my mind for days without telling me which muscles to strengthen. But I suppose the feeling after finally solving something is similar: exhaustion, relief, and the suspicion that I might want to try again tomorrow.

ENTRY 118 — 2003-08-25 — Everybody's Senior Year

The new school year began. William is a senior and so am I, which makes every ordinary thing sound important. The last first day. The last school photograph. The last time we'll complain about the same hallway.

Several friends are already talking about colleges as though admissions letters have been mailed. I'm looking at programs in cognitive science and computing, and every choice seems to create three others. William talks about California. I can hear how much he wants to go, even when he says the distance won't matter.

I want him to have the future he wants. I also want the future to keep us in the same city. Those desires haven't reached a peaceful agreement inside my head. For now, I have homework and a volleyball schedule. It helps to have things that can be solved before next spring.

ENTRY 119 — 2003-09-08 — President Borkman

William won student body president. I am proud of him and irritated with how many people now want his attention. I understand perfectly well that this is not a flattering admission.

He stayed after a meeting to walk me home. I told him that sometimes it felt as though half the school had borrowed him without asking. He listened, then said he couldn't promise to be available every moment, but he could promise not to make me guess whether I mattered.

That was a better answer than I deserved. Mom has said since I was a little girl that loving someone doesn't mean owning them. I'm finally old enough to understand the sentence and still young enough to struggle with it. William is somebody I want to choose me freely, not somebody I want to keep by making his life smaller.

ENTRY 120 — 2003-09-25 — An Ugly First Draft

My English teacher returned an essay covered in comments. I expected a good grade because I had strong ideas. She said the ideas were buried beneath sentences trying too hard to sound intelligent.

I wanted to argue. Instead I read the paper again and realized she was right. I had used longer words when shorter ones would have been clearer. I think sometimes I write as though an imaginary professor is looking over my shoulder, prepared to accuse me of being an ordinary girl.

I rewrote the essay with fewer flourishes. It's better. That is slightly painful to admit. Maybe intelligence isn't how complicated I can make an explanation. Maybe it's how much unnecessary complication I can remove without losing what's true.

ENTRY 121 — 2003-10-10 — The Train Home

The S-Bahn was crowded and we had to stand. A little boy beside us kept asking his father how trains knew where to go. The father answered patiently until he ran out of explanations. I almost joined in with a speech about signals and schedules.

William nudged me and whispered that not every public transport problem requires a lecture. I told him he was suppressing scientific inquiry. He said the child mostly wanted reassurance that we would reach the right station.

I watched the boy press his hand against the window as tunnels passed. There was something wonderful about asking a question because the world still feels surprising. I hope I don't become the sort of grown-up who mistakes a child asking why for a nuisance.

ENTRY 122 — 2003-10-27 — John's Opinion

John announced that William was too polite to be trustworthy. His evidence consisted of the fact that William remembered to thank Mom for dinner. I suspect my brother is annoyed that he no longer has exclusive rights to irritating me.

We argued until Dad told us to lower the temperature. Later John came to my doorway and asked whether William was nice to me when nobody else was around. I said yes. He nodded and walked away without another joke.

That was more touching than I care to admit. John and I have fought over everything from cereal to privacy. Apparently somewhere underneath all the noise he is paying attention. Being siblings means you've witnessed each other's worst haircuts and still have a claim on each other's loyalty.

ENTRY 123 — 2003-11-21 — The Vision Project

My independent project tries to locate faces in low-resolution photographs. It can be fooled by a lamp, a window, or a bit of shadow that resembles the examples I used. I have spent whole afternoons discovering how stupid a machine can be while following my instructions perfectly.

Today I improved it enough to handle a few new pictures. I wanted to run around the classroom cheering. Instead I showed the results to my teacher, who immediately asked about false positives. Adults are gifted at moving the goalposts just as you become happy.

But he is right. A clever demonstration is not the same as a useful system. I want to study perception seriously. The question has stopped being whether a computer can find something in an image. I want to know how anything, including me, learns what matters.

ENTRY 124 — 2003-12-08 — Christmas Lights

We walked through a Christmas market after dark. The pavement was wet and the lights reflected in it like hundreds of small stars. I drank hot Kinderpunsch because Dad would have a fit if I tried something stronger, and Mark got powdered sugar all over his coat.

William joined us for part of the evening. I liked seeing him talk with Mom about ordinary things. He doesn't try to charm her with speeches. He simply listens and answers questions. Dad asked him about Berkeley, and I pretended to be absorbed in a wooden ornament while I listened to every word.

I wonder how a place becomes home. Perhaps it happens when you start imagining the people you love in its streets. Stuttgart doesn't feel foreign anymore. That will make leaving harder.

ENTRY 125 — 2003-12-31 — A Year with His Name in It

The year is ending, and I've tried to list what happened without turning every line into a note about William. It's surprisingly difficult. He isn't the whole story. I played, studied, traveled, argued with Dad, helped Mark, annoyed John, and wrote code that could mistake a lampshade for a human face.

But he has become part of how I notice those things. I reach for the telephone when something funny happens. I hear an argument and wonder what he'd say. I look for him when I walk into a crowded room.

I don't think love means becoming the same person. I think it means having somebody whose thoughts you want to know while still keeping your own. I am seventeen and probably wrong about many things. But this feels like a useful place to start.

ENTRY 126 — 2004-01-08 — Applications

Dear Diary,

The college applications are mostly finished. There are forms asking me to summarize myself in tiny boxes, which feels unfair. I can explain my grades and extracurricular activities. I cannot explain why I once kept a stone from Fort Stewart or why looking at a bad computer-generated classification makes me strangely happy.

Dad read one of my essays and said it was technically perfect and emotionally absent. I told him universities aren't supposed to admit people based on emotional performance. He said universities are full of people and people like knowing whom they are admitting.

I rewrote one paragraph to describe the first little program I broke and fixed. It sounds more like me now. I hope admissions officers appreciate someone who admits she has learned a great deal from making mistakes.

ENTRY 127 — 2004-01-25 — Snow Without a Plan

It snowed enough to make the streets look unfamiliar. William and I went walking when we should have been studying. We argued about whether the snow made Stuttgart beautiful or merely inconvenient. I said both, which he called cheating.

We ended up at a little café with wet socks and hot drinks. He told me about a classmate who was having trouble at home and asked whether I thought a student council officer could do anything without making matters worse. I didn't have an answer. That bothered me more than it should have.

Some problems aren't waiting for a clever solution. Sometimes you need to listen long enough to understand what help the person is actually asking for. I keep learning this lesson as though the world is determined to put it on my final exam.

ENTRY 128 — 2004-02-06 — A Small Fight

William forgot that I'd asked him to come watch the last part of volleyball practice. He had a meeting that ran long. I said it was fine, then spent ten minutes making sure he understood that it wasn't fine at all.

He finally asked whether I wanted him to apologize or defend himself. I told him I wanted him to have remembered in the first place. That was the truth, but it didn't leave either of us with much to do.

We talked properly after dinner. I said it hurt to feel optional. He said he needed room to make ordinary mistakes without being tried for abandonment. That word stopped me. I think I am still carrying every goodbye from childhood in a suitcase I insist is empty. I apologized. He did too.

ENTRY 129 — 2004-02-21 — The Museum Notebook

I found the little notebook from our Paris trip and tried to read my own handwriting. Apparently last spring I thought useful travel phrases should include 'the train has left' and 'I don't know where my brother is.' Both came from experience.

The margins are full of sketches, bad French, and questions about paintings. I like that I didn't know which details would matter later. If I had chosen only the important ones, I would have left out most of what makes the trip feel real.

I started another notebook for computer ideas. This one contains diagrams that probably make sense only to me, plus an elaborate complaint about the school's printers. Dad said I have reinvented his green pocket notebook with more opinions and less classification.

ENTRY 130 — 2004-03-09 — The Scholarship Conversation

Mom asked what I would do if I received offers from several good universities. I said I would compare the programs and costs, obviously. She asked whether that was the entire answer. I told her I was aware that moving across an ocean might also matter.

She was trying to ask about William. I was trying not to discuss him because the answer hasn't changed: I want a serious education and I want to stay with him, and nobody has promised those things will fit neatly together.

Dad said a good choice can still hurt. I wish adults would stop saying things like that, especially when they're right. I have been treating the future like a puzzle with one optimal solution. Maybe some puzzles simply involve deciding which loss you can live with.

ENTRY 131 — 2004-03-27 — Choosing Carolina

I chose UNC Chapel Hill. My reasons make sense on paper: an excellent program, good academic opportunities, and the chance to build toward cognitive science and programming. But there's another reason I haven't told everyone. It's where I was born, and for once I want to choose a place instead of having the Army choose it for me.

William was kind when I told him. He said he'd hoped I'd find a way closer to California, but he didn't argue. We sat together for a while looking at college maps like two people studying different continents.

I want to feel triumphant. Mostly I feel excited and scared. Dad says being free to choose doesn't remove responsibility for what you choose. I told him he could stop being philosophical for one afternoon. He said that was unlikely.

ENTRY 132 — 2004-04-11 — Easter in Two Languages

Mass was crowded, and Mom had to remind Mark that trying to count all the candles was not the point of the service. I thought it was a fairly respectable exercise in observational skill, but I wisely kept quiet.

Grandma Sarah phoned afterward. She asked whether I'd chosen college and told me she was proud. Then she asked whether I was still going to church after I left home. I told her I hadn't decided what kind of believer I was. She said she was much older and hadn't finished deciding either.

That surprised me. I think I assumed faith meant the absence of questions. Sarah seems to think it means showing up while carrying some of them. I don't know whether she's right. I know I like that she made room for my uncertainty without sounding disappointed.

ENTRY 133 — 2004-04-23 — The Dress

Mom and I went looking for a prom dress. We disagreed about almost every option. She liked one with elaborate beading; I said I'd look like a chandelier. I liked a simple dress that let me move and sit without managing it all evening. She said I was being excessively practical for a girl planning a formal dance.

I tried it on anyway, and she went quiet in the dressing room. Then she said, 'There you are.' I don't know why that made my eyes sting.

I spent years feeling watched in clothes that didn't fit the girl I was becoming. Today I looked in the mirror and didn't think about anyone laughing. I thought about dancing with William. That was enough to make the dress feel right.

ENTRY 134 — 2004-05-01 — Mark Has a Secret

Mark wouldn't stop hovering in my doorway, so I finally asked what he wanted. He wanted advice about a girl. I nearly laughed because he is my little brother and I have apparently forgotten he is allowed to grow up.

He asked whether he should tell her he liked her. I said he could, provided he was prepared to hear an answer other than the one he wanted. He looked appalled. I told him that was the inconvenient part of other people having minds of their own.

Later I realized I'd given him exactly the advice Mom gave me when I first started noticing William. Apparently every generation is just the previous generation's lectures in different handwriting. I hope Mark remembers I was gentle about it. I hope he never reads this page.

ENTRY 135 — 2004-05-15 — Senior Prom

Prom was louder and sillier than I expected. People who spend ordinary school days pretending not to care about one another suddenly wanted photographs with everyone. William looked handsome and nervous, which I liked. We danced badly, then stopped caring that we danced badly.

For a while I forgot that summer would pull us in opposite directions. Then a slow song came on, and the future arrived in the middle of the dance floor. He held me closer and didn't promise anything grand. I was grateful. We'd already made the promises; we didn't need to rehearse them.

When I got home, I put the dress carefully away instead of dropping it on the chair. I think I wanted the evening to remain intact a little longer. Tomorrow there will be homework and laundry and normal time again.

ENTRY 136 — 2004-05-16 — Sunday Morning Quiet

I slept late, and Mom left breakfast on the stove rather than wake me. She asked whether prom had been wonderful. I said yes. She looked as though she wanted details and then decided to let me choose what to tell her.

That was a gift. I don't know exactly when I stopped being the child who had to explain every hour of her day. It's strange being almost eighteen: my parents still know how I take my tea and where I lose my socks, but there are corners of my life that belong to me now.

I went outside with a book and sat in the sun until I could stop thinking about how quickly everything is changing. Some memories feel private because they're important, not because they're shameful. I like having learned the difference.

ENTRY 137 — 2004-05-25 — The Last Volleyball Practice

We had our final practice. Nobody made a speech, which was probably wise because a room full of teenagers pretending not to cry is a dangerous place for speeches.

I stayed after to help put equipment away. Coach asked whether I would keep playing in college. I said I wasn't sure. She told me I didn't need to turn everything I was good at into a permanent identity. I laughed because that is exactly the kind of advice I usually resist.

I will miss the sound of shoes on the floor and the moment before a serve when everyone is waiting for the same movement. I won't miss the locker room smell. Goodbyes are always mixed like that: real grief over parts of a thing and genuine relief over others.

ENTRY 138 — 2004-06-04 — Third in the Class

I graduated third in my class with a 3.88 GPA. Mom cried. Dad shook my hand before he hugged me, which is so perfectly Dad that I may never forget it. John asked whether third place came with a bronze medal. Mark wanted to know if he could keep my old locker.

I was pleased and annoyed about the tiny fraction of a grade I could have improved. Mom noticed and told me I had better learn to enjoy an accomplishment before finding defects in it.

After the ceremony we took photographs with the school behind us. I realized this was the longest stretch of my childhood spent in one place. Four years. Germany had made me less of a temporary guest in my own life. I hope college doesn't undo that.

ENTRY 139 — 2004-06-07 — Eighteen

I'm eighteen. Officially an adult, according to several important forms, though Mark still stole icing from my birthday cake and John still insists I have no sense of humor about my books.

William gave me a letter instead of something expensive. He wrote about the girl who argued with him after an assembly and kept arguing after he started hoping she'd stay. I cried while reading it, which I hadn't intended to do in front of anybody.

At dinner Dad asked what I wanted from the next four years. I said an education, good friends, and some idea of the sort of work worth doing. I didn't say I also want a relationship that survives two coasts. I think everyone at the table knew that part anyway. Being eighteen doesn't make uncertainty smaller.

ENTRY 140 — 2004-06-18 — A Day Without Plans

William and I spent the day doing very little. We browsed a bookshop, bought sandwiches, and sat outside watching people hurry past. I kept trying to make the day memorable because time together suddenly feels expensive.

He finally asked me to stop looking at my watch. I said I wasn't. He raised an eyebrow. I admitted I was counting how many ordinary afternoons we had left before August.

He said counting them wouldn't make them last longer. I know that. I also know every military move trained me to notice endings before other people do. We went back to the bookshop and each chose something ridiculous for the other to read. I got a mystery with an awful cover. He got a book about bad management. It was a good afternoon once I allowed it to happen.

ENTRY 141 — 2004-07-04 — An American Holiday in Germany

We had an Independence Day gathering with other military families. People hung flags, cooked too much food, and argued over whether the weather would cooperate. It smelled like every base picnic of my childhood, despite being half a world from the country we were celebrating.

Dad talked with people from work while Mom negotiated a table for the family. I watched younger children racing around and realized how much my own relationship with Army life has changed. I used to be angry that it could move us without asking. I still think that was unfair. But I also know Germany gave me something I wouldn't trade away.

John says I'm becoming sentimental. He is right. I've decided not to let him enjoy hearing me admit it.

ENTRY 142 — 2004-07-18 — Packing Books

I am packing for UNC. The book pile is larger than the clothing pile, which Mom says is not a viable strategy for surviving North Carolina weather. I told her university libraries probably have books too, but these are my books and I know where their important pages are.

I found the stone from Fort Stewart, the Paris notebook, a photograph of our first Christmas here, and the little book William gave me last birthday. I laid them out on the bed as though arranging pieces of evidence.

I used to worry that leaving a place meant losing the person I was there. The objects help, but they aren't the person. Maybe the person is the one doing the choosing now: deciding which things to pack and which memories can safely travel without their souvenirs.

ENTRY 143 — 2004-08-05 — The Talk with Mom

Mom took me shopping for the last things I might need at college. We bought towels, notebooks, and a saucepan she insisted every competent adult should own. I asked whether she expected me to cook in a dormitory. She said someday I would thank her.

Over lunch she asked whether I was afraid. I started giving an impressive speech about academic preparation and adjusting to new environments. She waited. Finally I admitted I was afraid of being lonely, afraid William and I would drift apart, and afraid everyone at UNC would already understand something I didn't.

She said being brave didn't require turning fear into a lecture. Then she ordered dessert. I like that my mother often has the most useful answer when she stops asking questions.

ENTRY 144 — 2004-08-14 — The Airport

William left for Berkeley, and I am leaving for Carolina. At the airport we promised to call, write, visit, and be sensible about the time difference. We sounded very grown-up for two people who were both trying not to cry.

I managed to hold myself together until he disappeared past security. Then I cried in a way that made my entire face feel hot. Dad didn't try to fix it. He just stood beside me until I could breathe properly.

I've said goodbye hundreds of times. The machinery of it is familiar. You hug, you wave, you go home. But this isn't a move the Army ordered. We chose our futures and those choices are taking us apart for a while. I don't know whether that makes the pain more noble or merely more confusing.

ENTRY 145 — 2004-08-23 — Carolina

I moved into my room at UNC. My roommate covered her side with photographs, pillows, and decorative lights. My side looked like a library had developed access to a bed. We laughed about it and agreed not to try changing one another.

Campus is crowded and green. People keep asking where I'm from. I tell them I was born here but grew up in Army towns and Germany. Most people ask which place counts as home. I don't know, and for once the answer doesn't bother me very much.

I walked around after orientation until I found a spot where I could sit and call William. He was unpacking on the other side of the country. We compared disasters involving boxes and roommates. It helped to hear his voice, although afterward I was suddenly lonely again.

ENTRY 146 — 2004-09-02 — The Eight O'Clock Problem

My schedule is too ambitious. I know this because the first lecture hadn't even started and I was already calculating how little time existed between classes. The advisor looked at it and asked whether I planned to eat lunch. I said lunch was flexible. She said human physiology was less flexible than I assumed.

I'm interested in too many subjects to choose just one: cognitive science, computer science, mathematics, language. The connections are what excite me. A psychology lecture gives names to questions raised by a program. A programming problem makes an abstract idea concrete.

I miss Stuttgart more in the evenings. During the day I'm too busy to feel homesick. At night I want to tell somebody what I discovered and discover that everybody I normally tell is far away.

ENTRY 147 — 2004-09-13 — The Roommate Treaty

My roommate and I have made rules. Music requires agreement after eleven, dirty dishes are not decorative objects, and neither of us will use the other's shampoo without asking. That was probably all the child wanted to know. I am learning to match explanations to the question somebody actually asked. It's harder than it sounds, especially when you know a lot more than you need to say.

ENTRY 194 — 2006-07-01 — Dad Retires

Dad retired from the Army. I watched the ceremony with Mom, John, and Mark, and suddenly realized how thoroughly his uniform had organized our childhood: addresses, gates, schools, absences, even the vocabulary we used for moving.

He looked pleased and uncomfortable during the formal speeches. When he came over afterward, he hugged us all and immediately asked whether we'd eaten. I told him he was incapable of experiencing a milestone without checking the logistical support.

He laughed, but his eyes were wet. I hadn't expected that. Dad is still the man who keeps a notebook of things he needs to remember and things he cannot tell us. I wonder what retirement will feel like for someone whose job has been part of his daily identity for so long. I suspect he hasn't finished working.

ENTRY 195 — 2006-07-21 — Dad Without a Uniform

Dad looks different in ordinary clothes. Not younger, exactly. More difficult to classify. John joked that perhaps we should issue him a name tag so people would still know who was in charge.

He has been talking to people about contracting work tied to intelligence. Mom says there will be less moving, which sounds wonderful. Dad says there may be just as much paperwork. Some traditions evidently survive retirement.

We sat together after dinner, and I asked whether he was sad to leave the Army. He said he would miss the people and the sense of a shared mission, but not every part of the institution. I liked that answer. Loyalty seems more trustworthy when it has room for criticism. Maybe that's another piece of his career I've inherited without realizing it.

ENTRY 196 — 2006-08-13 — A House I Didn't Grow Up In

Visiting my parents in Virginia is strange because their home contains all our familiar people and almost none of my childhood places. Sometimes I expect to hear a Stuttgart bus outside. Instead there are different birds, different traffic, and John asking where somebody has hidden the good cereal.

Mom says I will eventually understand that home can be built from routines. I know that intellectually. I'm beginning to know it in my bones too. We made breakfast, argued over dishes, and sat around the table until the coffee went cold.

Michael called while I was there and spoke with Dad for a few minutes. When I returned to the phone, he sounded relaxed rather than examined. That relieved me. I want the important people in my life to be able to sit in the same room without anyone performing a role.

ENTRY 197 — 2006-09-12 — The Fast Track

I'm taking a heavy course load again. If everything goes well, I can finish UNC next spring. Some people act impressed; others look alarmed. I'm somewhere between the two.

I love what I'm studying, but I'm beginning to notice how easy it is to confuse speed with purpose. I can finish an assignment early and still miss the more interesting question buried inside it. I can plan every semester and still not know what kind of life I want when the schedule runs out.

Michael asked whether I might consider graduate school after a little time living outside an academic timetable. I said maybe. That surprised me. A year ago I'd have treated anything other than the fastest route to the next credential as a failure of imagination. Now ordinary life feels like a legitimate subject of study.

ENTRY 198 — 2006-10-09 — Seeing Without Seeing

Our lab is working with image classification, and I'm discovering how much of the job consists of cleaning strange data. Cameras produce blur, glare, bad angles, missed pixels, and surprising reflections. The clean examples in textbooks feel increasingly like polite fiction.

I love the mess. When a model confidently identifies something that isn't there, I want to know exactly which feature misled it. Sometimes the answer is embarrassing: we let a convenience in the training set become the system's idea of reality.

I told a friend that perception isn't just recording. It's interpreting incomplete evidence under uncertain conditions. She said that sounded like dating. I laughed so hard I almost spilled my coffee. She's right, of course. People are always building models from partial observations and calling the results certainty.

ENTRY 199 — 2006-11-23 — Ordinary Thanksgiving

Michael joined my family for Thanksgiving. I worried he'd be overwhelmed by the way we talk over one another when we're excited. He wasn't. He helped Mom carry dishes, accepted Dad's questions without looking threatened, and laughed at John's stories even after John made the same joke twice.

Grandma Sarah called from Ireland. She asked whether I was happy. I said yes. Then she asked whether I was happy as myself. I told her that was a peculiar distinction. She said she had spent too much of her life watching women try to become what somebody else needed and would continue asking.

After dinner Michael and I took a walk. I told him about her question. He said he liked the version of me that argued with philosophers and forgot her glasses on her head. I believed him.

ENTRY 200 — 2006-12-31 — What I Want to Build

The year is ending, and I am sitting at my desk with next semester's reading stacked beside me. I'll finish at Carolina soon if my plan holds. Dad has retired from the Army and is moving into a different kind of work. John and Mark keep getting older without consulting me. Mom still knows when I'm tired before I do.

Michael and I have been together long enough for the relationship to feel ordinary in the nicest sense. We don't have to fill every evening with excitement. We can cook, read, disagree, and spend an afternoon doing separate things in the same room.

I don't know exactly what comes next. I want work that challenges me, a home with room to think, people who tell me the truth, and perhaps a kind of permanence I once believed could only arrive by accident. For the first time, I'm considering what it would mean to build it deliberately.
