[Christine Sue Norman - Notebook 01]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 001 — 1996-06-07 — Ten! Finally!

Dear Diary,

I am TEN. Two whole numbers in my age. Mom says it means I get more responsibility, which sounds suspiciously like the same chores with a better title. John put a paper crown on my head at breakfast and Mark tried to eat one of the candles before the cake was even made.

Mom gave me this notebook with a cloth cover and said I could keep things in it that were just mine. I asked whether that meant even she wouldn't read it. She said yes, unless she had a reason to think I was in danger. I wish she hadn't added the last part, but I suppose mothers are like that.

I think I'll write everything interesting. Not just birthdays. If we move again, I want proof of who I was before the move. I already have my smooth stone from Fort Stewart. Maybe this diary can be another kind of stone.

Chris

ENTRY 002 — 1996-06-13 — John and the Rules

John said a diary isn't fair unless he can keep one too. I told him notebooks are sold to everybody, which made him throw a sock at me. Then Mark copied us, except his sock was wet and nobody knows why.

At dinner Dad asked us to explain the argument without accusing one another first. I thought that was impossible. John said he only wanted to know why I had suddenly become secretive. I said wanting a room inside my own head wasn't the same as hiding something bad.

Dad nodded like I'd said something clever. Mom said privacy is a privilege, but trust is a responsibility. This house is full of sentences that sound like homework.

I let John borrow my spare notebook. He drew a tank on the first page, so I don't think he will become a great diarist.

ENTRY 003 — 1996-06-28 — A Stone from Georgia

We were cleaning the closet and I found the box with the stone I took from Fort Stewart when I was four. It is gray and ordinary and probably nobody else would even notice it. I washed it anyway.

Mark asked why I kept a rock. When I said it was from our old home he asked if I remembered the house. I don't remember very much. I remember Mom calling us in, a truck, and being angry that someone could take my bedroom away. Some of that might be remembering the stories people told afterward.

I don't know where memory stops and stories begin. Maybe that's why I want to write things down now while they are still happening. The rock doesn't prove much, but when I hold it I know I was a little girl there once.

ENTRY 004 — 1996-07-12 — The Library is Mine

I got three books from the library and finished two of them before dinner. The librarian told me I should try something harder, so I borrowed a book with a grown-up cover mostly to prove I could.

I didn't understand the first chapter and had to look up four words. That made me furious until Dad reminded me that a word I don't know today can be a word I know tomorrow. He said nobody is born with a vocabulary. I asked if he was certain, and he told me I was being difficult.

I like libraries because nobody asks whether your family is staying in town. They hand you a card and let you belong. When we moved from Fort Gordon and Fort Benning, I lost friends, rooms, and roads I knew. But there always seemed to be a library waiting for me.

ENTRY 005 — 1996-07-27 — The Dugout

I still think about Peter Michaels sometimes. He kissed me behind the dugout in May and afterward we both acted as though we'd committed a major international secret.

The actual kiss was quick. The strange thing was knowing he wanted to do it and that I had agreed. I keep going over whether that makes him my boyfriend. I don't think so. He hasn't written anything, and we didn't promise anything. Maybe some things can happen and then be over without being bad.

I would rather Mom never find this page. Not because it was terrible. Because she'll look at me in that soft, knowing way grown-ups do, and then the whole thing will belong to her instead of me.

ENTRY 006 — 1996-08-24 — John's Wrist

John fell from a climbing frame today and broke his wrist. I heard him shout and ran so fast I almost tripped over Mark. At the hospital I kept telling the doctor what had happened. Mom finally put her hand on my shoulder and said, "Chris, John can answer."

I felt awful. I thought I was helping, and it turned out I was talking right over him. John was pale and scared but he wanted to explain it himself. Later he let me draw a tiny rocket on his cast.

Dad said being the oldest doesn't make me the owner of my brothers. He says that a lot. I know he is right, but when they're hurt my brain goes straight into boss mode before I can stop it. I have to learn to be beside them, not in front of them.

ENTRY 007 — 1996-09-05 — The New School Year

School started, and for once I already knew some people. I didn't have to stand in a classroom while a teacher announced me as the new girl. That alone made today wonderful.

A girl in my class cried after lunch because her family had just moved here. Somebody said she'd get over it. I remembered standing at the fence in Georgia wishing I knew even one person. So I asked her if she wanted to sit with us. She said yes so quietly I almost missed it.

I don't know if we'll become best friends. It doesn't matter. You don't have to promise somebody forever just to make the next hour kinder. I wish I'd understood that the first time we moved.

ENTRY 008 — 1996-09-19 — The Class Election

Our class elected a representative. I wanted to run, then decided I hated having people look at me. I told myself I didn't care. That was a lie. I spent the whole afternoon thinking of things I would have said.

Mom asked whether I was afraid of losing. I said no. Then she asked if I was afraid of trying where everyone could see. That was closer.

John says I am bossy enough already and no election is necessary. He may be right, but that doesn't mean I wouldn't like to try something. Next time there is a job I want, I am going to put my hand up before I talk myself out of it. That's an official diary promise, which ought to count for something.

ENTRY 009 — 1996-10-04 — The Field Trip Disaster

We went to a museum, and I got so excited about the old instruments that I nearly missed the time we were supposed to meet our group. The teacher looked worried when I came around the corner. I felt awful for making everyone wait.

One boy whispered that I thought I was too smart to follow directions. That stung because I hadn't meant to ignore anything. I had simply been looking so carefully at something that I stopped watching the clock.

Mom says attention is a gift, but you have to decide where to put it. I like noticing details other people walk past. I don't like making somebody else frightened because I wasn't listening. Next time I'm wearing a watch and making myself check it, even if the museum has something wonderful.

ENTRY 010 — 1996-10-26 — Halloween Costume Engineering

Mark wanted to be a knight, but his helmet fell over his eyes every time he ran. I made him a new one from cardboard, tape, and the foil Dad uses for baking potatoes. John said it looked like a satellite dish. It did.

I liked figuring out how to make the helmet stay on more than choosing my own costume. Mom warned me that helping Mark didn't mean deciding everything for him. So I asked whether he wanted the silver feather or the red one. He chose both, naturally.

We tried the whole thing in the hallway. Mark charged at the couch, the helmet stayed on, and he announced that I was the court wizard. I think I can live with being the wizard.

ENTRY 011 — 1996-11-11 — Grandpa Peter's Hands

Dad called Grandpa Peter in New York for Veterans Day. Afterward we talked about his old job on the docks in the Bronx. I remember his hands best: enormous, with lines that look like someone drew roads across them.

Grandpa says a job is important even when it doesn't make anyone famous. He tells Dad that people who build and move things should never be treated as invisible. Dad says discipline runs in the family. I told him stubbornness does too, and he gave me that look that means I am correct but shouldn't celebrate.

I know more stories about Grandma Sarah and Ireland than about Dad's side. I want to ask Grandpa Peter about the docks next time we visit. Ordinary lives ought to get stories too.

ENTRY 012 — 1996-12-08 — The Christmas Choir

At church rehearsal Sister Margaret moved me from the front row because I kept correcting the timing of the other children. I was furious. I was also right about the timing.

Mom says being correct is not the same as being useful. Sister Margaret let me help the younger kids learn their parts if I promised not to sound like a tiny drill sergeant. I tried. Once I started laughing with them, they actually sang better.

I don't understand why some adults act as if questions are disrespectful. But I am beginning to understand why nobody likes being corrected in front of everybody else. Perhaps there is a way to tell the truth that doesn't make it feel like a slap.

ENTRY 013 — 1996-12-27 — Sarah and the Dough

Grandma Sarah came after Christmas and made soda bread without measuring much of anything. I asked how many cups of flour and she said, "Enough." This is not a real answer.

She pushed my hands into the dough and told me to notice how it changed when I added milk. It was sticky, then soft, then somehow right. I still wish she'd used a measuring cup, but her loaf came out beautifully.

She talked about Ireland while we worked. Some stories were funny, and some turned quiet halfway through. I asked about Patrick, her husband who died before I was born. She said she loved him, but that loving somebody doesn't turn every choice they made into a good choice. I think I understood about half of that. I wrote it down because I may understand the other half later.

ENTRY 014 — 1996-12-31 — What Ten Was Like

If I had to give this year a grade, maybe B plus. I hated the school button incident. John broke his wrist. Mark is still extremely noisy. But I have my own diary and I read more books than last year.

Dad says I should write what I want for next year. I want to learn more about computers, be less frightened of looking foolish, and find out why adults never agree about politics but always say children are too young to discuss it.

I also want to stop treating every new place as a temporary waiting room. We have lived in Virginia for a while now. Maybe I can just be here instead of worrying about the next Army orders. We will see.

ENTRY 015 — 1997-01-11 — The Pentium

Dad brought home an old Pentium computer that his office didn't need. He says I can use it if I promise not to treat the inside like a science experiment without asking. He knows me too well.

It has DOS, and Dad showed me how to move between directories. I typed a command wrong and made the screen show an error. Instead of getting angry he showed me how to read what it said. Errors are apparently instructions if you are patient enough.

John thinks the interesting thing is games. I think the interesting thing is telling the machine what to do and seeing it actually do it. It listens more literally than my brothers. If I write something badly, the computer doesn't guess what I meant. That might be annoying, but it also seems fair.

ENTRY 016 — 1997-01-23 — A Broken Program

I made a little program do something silly today by changing one number. Then I changed another number and it stopped doing anything at all. For a minute I thought I'd broken the computer.

Dad explained that software isn't a fragile object like a vase. Usually you can find the mistake and try again. He made me write down the changes one at a time. It was slower but it worked.

I think I like this because the computer has no idea whether I'm good at sports or popular at school. It doesn't care what I look like. The answer depends on the rules I give it. That feels like having a room where I can be judged only by what I figure out.

ENTRY 017 — 1997-02-14 — Valentine Cards

Everybody had to give cards to the whole class so nobody would get left out. I liked the idea until I realized how ridiculous it was to give a card with hearts to somebody who'd been mean to me all week.

Mom said being polite doesn't mean pretending to feel something. I wrote "Happy Valentine's Day" on his card and left off the extra hearts. That seemed honest enough.

I don't have a boyfriend. Some girls act as though this is an emergency. I remember Peter behind the dugout and think I'd rather wait until I actually like somebody than pick a boy because February has decided I should.

ENTRY 018 — 1997-03-03 — The Metro Map

Dad took us into Washington and let me work out the Metro route. I love the colored lines. Everything looks tangled until you understand where to change trains, and then it becomes a puzzle with an answer.

John kept suggesting we could go somewhere entirely different just to annoy me. I drew two alternative routes to show him we could, which only made him laugh harder.

On the ride home I wondered whether people could make maps of things besides trains. Maybe thoughts have stations and connections too. I didn't tell Dad because I was afraid he'd make it into a lesson. Sometimes I just want to keep a question in my head for a while and see what happens.

ENTRY 019 — 1997-04-13 — The Church Question

I asked Sister Margaret why God would give people brains and then get angry when they asked questions. The room went so quiet I thought I was going to be sent out.

Instead she said faith without questions might be only obedience. I liked that answer. Mom was less impressed when she heard I had interrupted the lesson. Grandma Sarah laughed and told me to keep asking, but also to learn when other people needed their turn.

I don't know exactly what I believe. I do know I don't want to say something just because everyone else is saying it. If a thing is true, questions should help us find it. I wish adults acted as if they believed that more often.

ENTRY 020 — 1997-05-02 — The Ball That Broke Nothing

John and I were playing catch indoors even though we aren't supposed to. We knocked over Mom's vase. It didn't break, but the water soaked a stack of papers on the table. John wanted to put everything back and pretend nothing happened.

I almost agreed. Then I remembered the time we broke a window and thought of blaming Mark. That memory still makes my stomach tight.

We told Mom. She made us dry the table and replace the papers neatly. She was cross but not nearly as angry as she would have been if she'd discovered we lied. John said confessing is unfair because you get punished either way. I said at least we get to choose the kind of people we are while we're being punished. He said that sounded like Dad.

ENTRY 021 — 1997-06-07 — Eleven

I am eleven today, which is less exciting than ten. Dad gave me a computer manual. John gave me a pencil that says WORLD'S MOST ANNOYING SISTER. I intend to use it for all my important work.

Mom invited two friends over and we made our own pizzas. Mine had too many mushrooms. Mark ate the olives off everyone's plates.

I sometimes feel older than my brothers and sometimes exactly their age. I like being responsible when it means people trust me. I hate it when it means I get blamed for whatever Mark does with the toothpaste. Maybe growing up is partly discovering that authority is not the same as freedom.

ENTRY 022 — 1997-07-08 — Jane Eyre

I finished Jane Eyre. I had to ask Mom about some words, and she warned me parts would be difficult. She was right. Jane is stubborn and doesn't always make the choices I'd make, but I like that she refuses to become less of herself just because she loves someone.

I argued with Dad about whether Jane was being proud or brave. He said those can look alike from the outside. I hate answers that make the question harder, but I think this is one of them.

I want people to like me. That isn't a crime. I just don't want to agree with something wrong because I'm frightened they'll stop. It's easier to write that here than to do it at school, where being laughed at feels like the end of the world.

ENTRY 023 — 1997-08-18 — The End of Summer

The Army hasn't moved us, and the same school is waiting next week. That sounds boring when I say it, but I am secretly delighted. I know the roads and which neighbors have dogs and which library shelf has the mysteries.

We visited family and I heard adults talk about orders and promotions again. I tried not to listen, but I listened anyway. Dad noticed and told me no new move was planned. I asked him to promise. He said he could promise to tell me the truth, not to control the Army.

I suppose that's fair. It isn't especially comforting. But I think I'd rather have a real promise than a pretty one that somebody can't keep.

ENTRY 024 — 1997-09-07 — The First Day Back

I didn't know anyone who was new until I saw a boy standing by the door with a schedule in his hand. He looked exactly how I used to feel: as though there was a secret everyone else had been taught.

I showed him the classrooms and the water fountain that works. When he thanked me I said it wasn't a big deal. But it was a big deal when someone did it for me.

Later a girl asked why I always collect lost people. I laughed, but I thought about it all evening. I don't want to be the girl who needs a new friend to feel important. I just know how much a small kindness can matter when you've arrived in the middle of a story.

ENTRY 025 — 1997-09-26 — Computers and Boys

A boy told me computers were mostly for boys. I asked whether the machine knew the difference. He said that wasn't the point. I told him it was exactly the point.

Then I came home and spent an hour trying to figure out a problem from Dad's manual. I couldn't solve it and got so angry I snapped my pencil. Mom gave me another pencil and told me not to make one stupid boy responsible for my whole afternoon.

She was right. I don't have to prove everything the minute someone doubts me. Tomorrow I'll try the program again because I want to understand it, not because he needs correcting. Though correcting him would be satisfying.

ENTRY 026 — 1997-10-11 — Grandma's Rosary

Grandma Sarah told me about her old wooden rosary while we were talking on the phone. I can picture the beads because I've seen her carry them for as long as I can remember.

She says prayer can help people keep hold of themselves even when it doesn't change the world. I asked whether she was ever angry at God. She said certainly, and then asked why I thought loving someone meant never being angry with them.

That surprised me. Most grown-ups don't like difficult questions about church. Sarah seems to think questions are part of faith. I am going to remember that the next time someone tells me doubt is a bad thing.

ENTRY 027 — 1997-11-15 — Mark's Volcano

Mark wanted me to build his model volcano because mine would look better. I nearly did it. Measuring and making things are fun, and I could tell exactly how to stop the cardboard from leaning sideways.

Dad caught me with the glue and asked whose science project it was. I said Mark's. He asked why my hands were doing the work.

I helped Mark mix the paste and showed him how to shape it. His mountain leaned badly to one side, but when the foam spilled out he jumped up and down like he'd made a real volcano. I almost fixed the crooked part afterward. I didn't. Being smart enough to do something doesn't mean it belongs to me to do.

ENTRY 028 — 1997-12-05 — A Book about Robots

I found a book about robots and have been bothering everyone with questions. Some machines can move their arms, some can follow instructions, and some can notice things with cameras. But what does it mean to notice?

Dad says a camera receives light, and a computer has to decide what the patterns mean. I asked how a person decides. He said that was an excellent question and went back to his newspaper. Typical.

Sometimes I think grown-ups enjoy naming the parts of a problem so they don't have to admit the mystery. I want to know how a machine could see a chair and know it is a chair even when it's dark or sideways or somebody has put a coat over it.

ENTRY 029 — 1997-12-24 — Christmas Eve

Mom made us dress properly for Mass and I complained about the shoes. Grandma Sarah would've told me to stop fussing and remember why we were there.

The church smelled like candles and wet coats. I knew all the responses, which made it feel like a place that didn't move even when our family did. Mark got sleepy and leaned against me. I thought about how irritating he can be and how frightened I would be if anything happened to him.

I don't know if that's what prayer is supposed to feel like. I didn't ask for anything. I just held his hand through the last song and was glad he was there.

ENTRY 030 — 1997-12-31 — Year of Questions

I'm eleven and I have more questions than I did last January. This is good, unless I ask them at the wrong time, which apparently happens often.

I can write simple programs now. I learned that I like difficult books even when they annoy me. John and Mark still make our house feel like an animal enclosure with a roof.

For next year I want to build something that sees pictures instead of just numbers. I also want to stop feeling embarrassed whenever I make a mistake in front of people. These might be related, because I will probably make a lot of mistakes if I try the first thing.

I am keeping the diary. It feels like somebody is saving the days before they run away.

ENTRY 031 — 1998-01-12 — A Camera Doesn't Know

Dad let me use an old camera to take pictures around the house. I photographed a chair, the same chair with a coat on it, and the chair turned so the legs looked strange. To me they were all obviously the same chair.

I made a little chart and tried to explain to John why a computer might not know that. He said the computer ought to get glasses. I told him that was not helpful. Then I laughed because maybe it was a little helpful.

I don't know how to make a machine understand any of this yet. My programs are mostly numbers and clumsy shapes. But there's something wonderful about a problem that doesn't become easy just because I want it to. I might be thinking about chairs for a very long time.

ENTRY 032 — 1998-02-08 — Snow Day Argument

School was closed because of the weather, which should have been wonderful. Instead John and I argued all morning about who got the computer. Mom drew a schedule on a piece of paper and taped it to the monitor.

I tried to convince her I needed more time because my work was educational. John said his game taught reflexes and strategy. Mom said we'd both become lawyers if we didn't stop talking.

When my time finally arrived, the program I'd saved wouldn't run. I spent most of my turn finding a missing symbol. At first I felt cheated. Then it worked, and I felt so pleased that I almost didn't mind John getting the machine next. Almost.

ENTRY 033 — 1998-03-06 — Science Fair

I built a program that tries to find squares and circles in a tiny black-and-white picture grid. It doesn't work as well as I'd hoped. If the picture is crooked or messy, the program gets confused.

At the fair a judge asked why it made mistakes. I told him exactly what I'd learned, including all the things I hadn't fixed. He stayed a long time and asked whether I thought people recognized shapes the same way. I said probably not, because people could guess from half a drawing.

I didn't win first prize. I was disappointed until I found "KEEP GOING" written across the bottom of my score sheet. I pinned it above my desk. I think the interesting part of this project is still ahead of me.

ENTRY 034 — 1998-03-21 — The Questions After

A girl asked why I entered the science fair if my program didn't work properly. I told her science is what people do before they know the answer. She said that sounded like something from a poster.

I was angry for about an hour. Then I looked at my project and realized she had asked the question I was secretly asking myself: should I have waited until it was perfect?

Dad says if researchers waited for perfect answers, they'd never show their work to anyone. I don't want to become the sort of person who hides every unfinished thing. I also don't want to stand there while people point out mistakes. Perhaps courage is doing both and keeping your hands steady.

ENTRY 035 — 1998-04-12 — Easter at Home

We went to church for Easter and Grandma Sarah called afterward. She asked what the sermon was about. I tried to tell her, but Mark kept interrupting with the story of how he nearly dropped his egg basket.

Sarah said a family is like a conversation where everybody talks at once and somehow you still know who belongs. I liked that, though I suspect she only said it because Mark was shouting into the phone.

I have questions about the Resurrection that I don't know how to ask without sounding rude. Maybe I'll write them out first. Sister Margaret likes questions better when they are clear. I'm discovering that good questions take almost as much work as answers.

ENTRY 036 — 1998-05-19 — John Says I'm Serious

John told me I never do anything silly unless I can explain why it makes sense first. That is not true. I have done lots of silly things. I simply dislike having an audience when they fail.

So I agreed to play basketball in the driveway even though I am terrible at it. Mark kept stealing the ball, and John pretended to give a sports commentary whenever I missed. I laughed so hard I could barely throw.

I think I understand what he meant. I like being good at things because it feels safe. But sometimes it's fun to be bad at something and let everybody know. I hope I remember that when school gets harder.

ENTRY 037 — 1998-06-07 — Twelve

Twelve is a strange age. Mom says I am still a child, and some days I agree with her. Then a teacher asks us to behave like young adults and I want somebody to explain when the rules changed.

For my birthday I asked for books and computer parts, which Dad called the most expensive inexpensive list he'd ever seen. John gave me a hand-drawn picture of our family as robots. Mine had glasses and a big red button labeled CORRECT EVERYONE. I laughed, but I'm keeping it.

I've been writing in this diary for two years. Sometimes I read my first pages and want to tell that little girl to calm down. Then I remember she was me, and I decide not to be mean to her.

ENTRY 038 — 1998-07-04 — Fireworks and Grandpa

Dad talked about Independence Day with Grandpa Peter on the phone. Grandpa said freedom was important but people used the word as an excuse to avoid doing their share. Dad said that sounded exactly like him.

At night we watched fireworks. Mark covered his ears and John tried to guess how high they went. I wondered who invented all the colors. Mom told me I was allowed to enjoy a thing before researching it.

I tried. For almost five minutes I just watched. The green sparks looked like something from a computer game, except nobody had put them inside a screen. I think the world is full of problems worth solving and things worth simply seeing. I am trying to learn both.

ENTRY 039 — 1998-08-22 — The Last Weekend

School starts again next week. I finished the summer reading list and then read two more books because there was nothing on television. Mom says I sound eighty when I complain about television.

A friend invited me over, and we spent most of the afternoon talking about how unfair our parents were. Then her mother brought us lemonade, and we thanked her politely, so clearly we weren't suffering too much.

I like having a friend who doesn't ask me to be the smart one all the time. We can disagree about music, clothes, and everything else without one of us having to win. It feels different from school competitions. I want more friendships like that.

ENTRY 040 — 1998-09-18 — Spanish Words

We started Spanish, and I like discovering how sentences fit together. I keep wanting to translate everything exactly and the teacher keeps saying languages don't work like that.

Dad knows enough German to make himself understood, but he says German isn't always offered in our schools. I asked for a German book anyway. The words look as though they've been built by joining smaller words into machines.

Spanish sounds completely different. I like the way both languages force me to notice things English lets me take for granted. It makes me wonder whether thoughts are different if the words around them change. I wrote that in the margin of my homework, and the teacher put a question mark beside it.

ENTRY 041 — 1998-10-16 — The Button Again

A button on my blouse came loose in class today. I know this sounds terribly familiar. I thought I'd outgrown the whole embarrassing-button problem, but apparently not.

A couple of boys laughed, and I could feel myself turning red before I even knew what was wrong. The teacher handled it kindly. At home Mom and I looked at my clothes, and she helped me choose a few dresses that actually fit.

I don't want my wardrobe decided by a stupid moment at school. The thing is, I really do like the dresses. They're comfortable, and I don't spend the whole day worrying about pulling fabric back into place. It's funny how something you choose to avoid embarrassment can become something you choose because you like it.

ENTRY 042 — 1998-11-11 — A Picture of Patrick

Grandma Sarah sent an old family photograph. Patrick O'Connor looks serious, like somebody who'd rather not be photographed. I stared at his face for a long time trying to see something familiar.

Mom said he became involved in the Irish struggle and died young, long before I was born. I asked if that meant he was a hero. Sarah said people weren't that simple. She told me to care about Ireland's history without making our family into the whole history of Ireland.

I think I wanted a story with an easy answer. A brave grandfather would be simpler than a man who made choices other people suffered for. But Sarah loved him, and she knows more than I do. I suppose I'll have to learn to hold both facts at once.

ENTRY 043 — 1998-12-03 — A Little Bit of Perfume

Aunt Maureen sent me a tiny bottle of perfume. It smells like orange peel and wood. I expected something sweet, like the kind of flowers on birthday cards, but I liked this better.

I wore one drop to school and kept sniffing my wrist, which probably looked ridiculous. One girl asked why I smelled like a forest. I said I thought forests were lovely. She didn't know what to say to that.

I'm discovering that it's possible to like dresses, computers, rough sneakers, books, perfume, and climbing trees without having to choose which things belong together. I wish people didn't keep sorting everything into what girls or boys are supposed to enjoy.

ENTRY 044 — 1998-12-24 — Candles

Christmas Eve Mass was crowded. A little girl in the pew ahead of us kept turning around to look at me, and I made silly faces when her mother wasn't watching. She almost burst out laughing during a quiet part.

Mom didn't see. I think.

When the candles were lit, I thought of Grandma Sarah, and Patrick, and people I know only from stories. I don't believe every answer adults give about heaven. But I like the thought that all those lives aren't simply erased because their people aren't here anymore.

I wrote their names in the back of this diary. Nobody asked me to. It just seemed like something worth doing.

ENTRY 045 — 1998-12-31 — A Year of Being Twelve

I am twelve and a half, and I have decided that growing up is mostly being told you are too young for some things and too old for others.

The science fair was my favorite thing this year, even though my program made foolish mistakes. I still have the judge's note. I also have more dresses than button-up shirts now, which is something I never predicted.

I think I am getting better at saying what I really think without making it sound as if everybody else is stupid. Not perfect, obviously. John will confirm that.

Next year I'd like to learn more programming and read something that makes me uncomfortable in a useful way. Dad says I always want the hardest possible book. He is probably right.

ENTRY 046 — 1999-01-30 — Dune

Dad gave me Dune. It is huge and complicated and sometimes I have to read a page twice. I love that the planet's water and plants and politics all affect one another.

I also find it frightening how easily people begin to treat a leader as though he couldn't possibly be wrong. I asked Dad how you know when someone deserves your loyalty. He said you start by seeing whether they can tolerate disagreement.

I had thought the book would be mostly about sandworms. There are sandworms, which are excellent. But the most interesting part may be how people convince one another to believe things. I didn't expect a science-fiction book to make me think about church and governments at the same time.

ENTRY 047 — 1999-02-17 — The Argument at Dinner

Dad and Mom disagreed about something on the news tonight. They weren't angry, exactly, but both sounded very certain. When I asked which of them was right, they gave me two long explanations that didn't really answer.

I asked whether two intelligent people could look at the same facts and reach different conclusions. Dad said all the time. Mom said facts were rarely the whole story.

That is extremely inconvenient. I like puzzles where there is an answer on the last page. But perhaps people aren't a math problem and even a correct answer can leave something out. I keep thinking about Sarah's stories of Ireland. Everybody seems to inherit a history and then argue from inside it.

ENTRY 048 — 1999-03-27 — Mark's Backpack

Mark came home upset because somebody had been pushing him around at school. My first thought was to find the boy and tell him exactly what I thought of him. Dad stopped me before I reached the door.

He made Mark explain what happened and decide what help he wanted. I thought Dad was being too slow. Later he went to the school with Mark, and the trouble stopped.

I hate seeing my little brother scared. I hate that I can't protect him from everything. But when Dad asked how I'd like it if someone always spoke for me, I remembered John's broken wrist and felt embarrassed. I am still learning that loving people doesn't make them mine to manage.

ENTRY 049 — 1999-04-11 — The Science Museum

We went to a museum in Washington and I spent so long at the robotics exhibit that my family nearly left without me. I watched a machine perform the same task over and over. It was impressive and also a little silly.

I asked Dad whether you could build something that didn't just follow instructions but figured out what mattered in a room. He asked how I'd tell it what mattered. That seems to be the terrible part of my favorite questions: every answer creates another question.

John said we should build a robot to do our chores. I said a robot would need to recognize the difference between dirty and clean dishes. He said that sounded like my problem.

ENTRY 100 — 2002-12-31 — The Year Before Something

I am sixteen and a half. I can speak German without rehearsing everything. I play sports, work on programs that still make stupid mistakes, and argue with people about questions nobody can answer simply.

I also keep thinking about William Borkman. There. I've written his name. I might as well admit it to a notebook that has kept worse secrets.

Four years ago I was the girl who felt publicly humiliated by a broken button. Six years ago I was ten and delighted to own a diary. I recognize those girls, but I wouldn't quite know how to be them again.

Next year might be different. Not because I have a grand plan. Because there is someone at school whose questions make me want to keep talking after the argument is over. I haven't felt that way before, and I don't want to pretend I haven't noticed.
