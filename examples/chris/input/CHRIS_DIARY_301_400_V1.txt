[Christine Sue Norman — Notebook IV: Cambridge and the Research Triangle]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 301 — 2012-01-04 — The Quiet After New Year
Dear Diary,
The streets were wet this morning, and the snow that looked pretty in photographs had become gray piles at every corner. I took the long route to the lab because I wanted a few minutes when nobody expected me to know anything. Last year took apart almost everything I had planned. This one has started with a stack of articles and a troublesome experiment. Strangely, that feels like a gift. I bought oranges at the shop on my way home, then called Mom while peeling one over the sink. She asked whether I was eating properly. I said yes, and for once it was mostly true. She didn't ask whether I was happy. I'm glad. I'm still finding out.

ENTRY 302 — 2012-01-09 — A Seat by the Window
I found a seat in the library where the afternoon light doesn't glare off my screen. A student with a mountain of textbooks asked whether the chair opposite was free, then spent three hours there without saying another word. It may have been the most comfortable social interaction I've had all week. At lunch I caught myself comparing the arrangement of tables to an old mess hall on base, which seems ridiculous until I remember how often I used to seek the same corner in a new school. My surroundings have changed; some instincts are apparently permanent. I left a note on my own laptop that said TAKE A WALK before deciding the note itself counted as a walk. It does not.

ENTRY 303 — 2012-01-13 — The Wrong Label
A collection of test images came with labels that looked perfectly tidy until I noticed one animal marked as a traffic sign. It was funny for exactly three seconds. Then I wondered how many subtler mistakes had passed inspection. I showed the others, expecting a quick correction. Instead we spent an hour discussing how the dataset was assembled and how easily an error can become invisible when it wears the right format. One fellow student grumbled that we were overthinking a clerical mistake. Perhaps. But a system trained on bad answers can become very confident in repeating them. I walked home thinking that the labels we put on people aren't always much more reliable. The uncomfortable part is how rarely we check those.

ENTRY 304 — 2012-01-18 — A Wednesday Call with John
John called while I was cooking pasta and asked whether I still knew how to make the garlic bread Mom used to make. He knew the answer. He wanted to talk and didn't know how to begin. We argued about butter first, then he admitted he was worried about a decision at work. He asked for the logical answer. I gave him one, and then asked what he was afraid might happen. There was silence before he answered. I used to think being the oldest meant having the solution. Now I suspect it means being willing to stay on the telephone when the solution isn't obvious. The pasta went soft. I ate it anyway. John promised to call before his next major culinary emergency.

ENTRY 305 — 2012-01-24 — The Frozen Steps
I slipped on a patch of ice outside a building and landed hard enough to make two strangers gasp. Nothing was broken except my dignity and the lid on my coffee cup. A woman helped gather my notebooks while I insisted, absurdly, that I was fine. She said, 'You can be fine after you sit down.' I liked her immediately. We waited together until my knee stopped shaking. On the way home, I kept thinking about how quickly I try to appear competent after something frightening. Mom calls it the Norman family reflex. I call it poor instrumentation. If I report only that I'm fine, nobody can tell what actually happened. I put an ice pack on my knee and told her about the fall honestly.

ENTRY 306 — 2012-01-30 — One Word in German
I heard German spoken behind me in the grocery store and turned before I realized why. The couple was debating cheese with such earnestness that I nearly laughed. When they caught me listening, I apologized in German, and we ended up talking about Stuttgart and the scandalous American habit of selling bread that bends. For a few minutes I was seventeen again, going out with William and practicing the language every chance I got. Then I was twenty-five, in Cambridge, holding a carton of milk. Neither version of me disappeared. I carried the conversation home like an unexpected present. Perhaps belonging isn't a flag planted in one place. Perhaps it's a set of doors that still open when someone knocks.

ENTRY 307 — 2012-02-03 — The Question Nobody Asked
In seminar we heard an impressive presentation about performance gains. The graphs were beautiful. I kept waiting for somebody to ask how the system behaved when the camera was tilted or the lighting changed. When the questions opened, I did. The presenter answered politely and admitted they hadn't checked. I didn't feel triumphant. I felt relief that the question could be asked without anyone treating it as hostility. Afterward another student suggested a way to test the failure modes without rebuilding the entire setup. We scribbled possibilities on a paper napkin. It was more productive than half the formal meeting. The pleasure of science, at its best, is not winning an argument. It's finding somebody else who wants to know what's actually true.

ENTRY 308 — 2012-02-08 — Missing Old Friends
I had lunch with two classmates who spent most of the meal complaining about their advisors. I understood the temptation and enjoyed it for perhaps ten minutes, then began to feel mean. Afterward I went back to work and wrote down the specific problems I could actually change. It's easier to feel close to people when you're all annoyed at the same thing, but that closeness evaporates when the thing disappears. I want friends I can discuss more than frustrations with. I miss having someone who already knows the odd little facts: that I hate soggy cereal, that I code best in silence, that I take questions as invitations rather than insults. I have to let new people learn those things instead of resenting that they don't know them yet.

ENTRY 309 — 2012-02-14 — An Unremarkable Valentine's Day
A student left tiny chocolate hearts on everyone's desks. Mine sat beside a printout of a confusion matrix, and for some reason the combination made me laugh until I had to pretend to cough. This date used to arrive carrying expectations. William took me into Stuttgart for our first proper date on Valentine's Day in 2003. Michael used to buy flowers, usually choosing the wrong kind in the most affectionate way. Now I made soup and answered an email from my brother. I am not lonely in every moment just because I don't have a partner. I still want love again, someday. Tonight I wanted clean dishes, a good book, and no one asking whether I was having a meaningful evening. It was meaningful enough.

ENTRY 310 — 2012-02-20 — Mom's Package
Mom sent socks, tea, and a small bag of the biscuits I loved as a child. She included a note that said she was not trying to parent me from three states away, merely trying to make sure I remembered I had feet. I laughed in the mailroom. I kept the note on my desk for the rest of the afternoon. Being cared for can feel embarrassing when you've built so much of your identity around competence. That's probably why Mom slips it into jokes. I called to thank her. She asked what I'd been reading, and we talked about a novel instead of how I was coping. It felt wonderful to be a daughter with an opinion about a book rather than a woman whose life everyone handles carefully.

ENTRY 311 — 2012-02-26 — The Shape of a Face
I've been trying to understand why certain low-resolution images confuse our face detector. I can look at two dots and a line and see an expression. The program sees pixels and numbers. In one image it found a face in the front of a building, which was ridiculous but not entirely mysterious: we humans do that too. I spent part of the afternoon drawing crude arrangements on scrap paper, asking a classmate to tell me which felt face-like. She enjoyed the game until I asked her to explain her answers. 'I just see it,' she said. Exactly. Perception carries enormous invisible work. The more I study it, the less I take ordinary seeing for granted.

ENTRY 312 — 2012-03-02 — The First Real Snow
I thought I understood winter from Germany. Cambridge has corrected me. The wind comes sideways between buildings and makes every exposed inch of skin feel personally insulted. I wore three layers and still arrived at class looking like I'd fought a refrigerator. A friend lent me a scarf that smelled faintly of laundry soap. I promised to return it and she said, 'After winter, not after lunch.' Small kindnesses build friendships more reliably than declarations. Later I caught myself worrying about where the scarf should be kept so I wouldn't lose it, and then laughed. The child who labeled moving boxes is still running the household. I may as well let her help, provided she doesn't start labeling other people's clothes.

ENTRY 313 — 2012-03-07 — The Failed Demonstration
We tried to show a visiting researcher a working version of the system. It failed beautifully. The display froze, the logs were unhelpful, and I felt heat climb my neck. I wanted to explain everything that had worked yesterday. Instead I asked for five minutes and started checking assumptions. It turned out we had changed a library version without noticing. The visitor stayed and helped. By the end we had a clearer understanding of the problem and a less flattering but more accurate account of the system. I went home tired, with a ridiculous amount of affection for everyone who had resisted blaming someone. Failure in public still stings. I'm learning it doesn't have to become humiliation if you keep being curious.

ENTRY 314 — 2012-03-13 — Coffee with a Woman Named Nina
Nina is in a neighboring research group and has a gift for making complicated ideas sound conversational. We argued for an hour about whether a machine can be said to notice something before it has a useful representation of it. She asked what I meant by notice. I asked what she meant by representation. We were still defining terms when the café closed. It reminded me of why I liked William Borkman at school: someone who could disagree and still want to keep talking. The comparison surprised me, though the feeling wasn't romantic. It was the pleasure of finding another mind that doesn't treat a question as an invitation to perform certainty. Nina asked if I'd join a reading group. I said yes before remembering how crowded my calendar already is.

ENTRY 315 — 2012-03-18 — The Little Blue Notebook
Dad's old green notebook came to mind today when I bought a tiny blue one for my bag. He used to jot down what mattered, though much of his work was something he couldn't discuss. Mine will hold ordinary things: ideas overheard in conversation, odd words, possible research questions, reminders to mail birthday cards. I've had digital calendars for years, but I like that a notebook doesn't demand charging. On the first page I wrote: NOT EVERYTHING IMPORTANT IS URGENT. On the second I wrote: BUY MILK. That probably captures my philosophy better than a long essay. I carried it to dinner and wrote down the name of a pastry I liked, because why shouldn't good pastries count as information worth keeping?

ENTRY 316 — 2012-03-24 — A Day Without Results
I spent almost nine hours working and produced nothing I can show anyone. The model barely changed; one experiment failed to converge; a promising idea dissolved when I checked the assumptions. I was irritable enough that I put my coat on without saying goodbye. At home I made tea and found myself reviewing every hour as evidence that I was inadequate. Then I remembered Dad teaching me to ride a bicycle. There was a stretch when I was moving forward before I believed I could. Perhaps the opposite happens in research: sometimes you're learning before any result is visible. I don't want to turn every difficult day into a motivational speech. Some days are simply frustrating. Today was one. Tomorrow I'll try again.

ENTRY 317 — 2012-03-30 — A Long Walk by the River
I walked along the Charles with no particular destination. Runners passed me, bicycles rang bells, and the wind made the water look metallic. For once I left my phone in my bag. I thought about Raleigh and wondered whether I'd return when the degree was done. North Carolina still holds people and memories I love, but I'm beginning to recognize how much of my adult self belongs to places I chose. I might not know where I'll live two years from now. This is oddly familiar territory for an Army child. The difference is that I get a vote. I stopped for coffee and looked at the river until it was time to go home. The walk didn't solve anything. That was partly the point.

ENTRY 318 — 2012-04-04 — The Wrong Shortcut
A clever optimization shaved time off our training run. Everyone was pleased until a more careful test showed it was also hiding a systematic error. The person who suggested it looked devastated. I understood; I've defended my own mistakes with embarrassing passion. I told him the idea hadn't been stupid. It had been useful enough to test and wrong enough to learn from. He looked suspicious of my kindness until I admitted I'd done the same thing last semester. We rolled back the change and left a record of why. The temptation to erase a failed attempt is strong, particularly when no one outside the group has seen it. But if we hide the failed routes, someone else will walk down them believing the path is clear.

ENTRY 319 — 2012-04-09 — Easter Away from Home
Mom called after Mass and said the church smelled of lilies. I could picture the pews and Dad standing slightly awkwardly during the hymns. Grandma Sarah used to say that showing up mattered even when prayer felt difficult. I didn't go to church today. I went for a walk, made breakfast slowly, and thought about William. The holiday's promises about death and life still catch in my throat. I'm not interested in pretending certainty I don't possess, but I don't want to treat the people who believe as foolish either. Mom asked if I'd like to come home soon. I said yes. She didn't ask whether I had been to Mass. Sometimes love means recognizing which question doesn't need to be asked.

ENTRY 320 — 2012-04-15 — Three Versions of a Memory
John and I argued about whether Dad had been present when I learned to ride my bicycle. I was certain he had held the seat. John claimed he remembered Mom standing nearby. Mom said we were both right and then pointed out that John had been too small to remember much of the day. That annoyed him enormously. The conversation made me think about data provenance. What I saw, what I was told, and what I've repeated for twenty years are not always the same category. Yet all three affect who I think I am. I wrote the argument in my notebook and labeled it UNCERTAIN, partly as a joke. I don't want every family story audited. I do want to know when I'm reconstructing rather than remembering.

ENTRY 321 — 2012-04-28 — Grandma Sarah's Advice
Grandma Sarah called from Ireland and asked whether I was eating enough vegetables. I said Mom had clearly enlisted her. She ignored that and told me about a neighbor whose chickens had escaped into the road. Her voice makes ordinary trouble sound worth a chapter in a novel. Eventually she asked how I was really doing. I said I was busy, which is what I say when I don't know where to begin. She waited. I told her I sometimes miss being married and sometimes feel relieved to live alone. She said there was no need to choose one feeling as the official truth. Then she told me to buy carrots. I love her ability to make emotional honesty and vegetable consumption sound like neighboring duties.

ENTRY 322 — 2012-05-04 — A Face in the Clouds
The model classified a noisy patch of sky as an object it was never meant to identify. Nobody was thrilled except me. I saved the image because the error had a kind of logic: similar edges, a familiar arrangement, a prediction made too eagerly. When I showed it to Nina, she immediately saw the wrong object as well, even knowing it wasn't there. That was the interesting part. We're not studying machines against a mythical human standard that never fails. We're studying different ways of building useful guesses from incomplete evidence. I took the printout home and left it on the kitchen counter. It made me laugh every time I passed. Some failures deserve a place of honor among the dishes.

ENTRY 323 — 2012-05-10 — An Evening with No Laptop
I promised myself a night away from the computer and immediately began mentally debugging an experiment while chopping onions. Apparently there is no physical off switch for my brain. I made a stew Grandma Sarah would probably declare insufficiently Irish and watched a film with the volume lower than usual. Halfway through I realized I was enjoying myself without evaluating the evening's usefulness. I called Mom, but the line was busy, and instead of trying again I wrote her a postcard. I don't know why a postcard feels more intimate than an email when the words can be identical. Perhaps the effort of finding a stamp matters. Tomorrow I will go back to the lab. Tonight the stew was good, the dishes are washed, and that is enough.

ENTRY 324 — 2012-05-16 — The Seminar Nobody Prepared For
Our professor arrived without slides, handed us an unfamiliar paper, and told us to work out which claims we could defend. The room was silent for a while. Then everyone started arguing, and it became the most useful class of the semester. I like a prepared lecture, but it's too easy to nod along when someone else has already selected the important facts. I took the train home still thinking about an assumption we never managed to settle. It was irritating in the best way. I called Dad to ask how often his work required decisions before the information was complete. He laughed and said he couldn't answer the interesting part of that question. We talked about weather instead, which was somehow an answer in itself.

ENTRY 325 — 2012-05-22 — The Neighbor's Radio
The person upstairs has discovered an enthusiasm for trumpet that exceeds her skill. Every evening the same phrases emerge through the ceiling, hesitant and determined. I considered knocking to ask for a quieter practice hour, then realized she stops before ten and I have no real grounds for complaint. Today she finally played the troublesome measure correctly. I applauded from my kitchen before thinking about how absurd that was. She shouted, 'Thank you!' through the floor. I laughed until the kettle boiled over. There are people all around me working hard at things that matter to them, and I know almost none of their stories. Tomorrow I may knock just to introduce myself. Perhaps I can offer biscuits as a treaty.

ENTRY 326 — 2012-05-29 — A Different Kind of Exam
Today's examination required explaining our decisions instead of simply producing a result. One question had no clean answer. I showed two possible approaches and described what each would fail to notice. Afterwards someone said I'd been too cautious. Maybe. I know I can sometimes retreat into qualifications because I don't want to be wrong. But certainty isn't free just because it sounds confident. The useful question is whether the decision needs more data or whether I'm delaying because the risk makes me uncomfortable. I wish I'd been able to articulate that better during the exam. I went to dinner with classmates and ordered dessert first. It seemed a reasonable way to exercise decisive judgment after a day full of ambiguity.

ENTRY 327 — 2012-06-07 — Twenty-Six
Twenty-six. Mom called at dawn and sang the same embarrassingly enthusiastic birthday song she always sings. John called five minutes later and complained that I'd made being the oldest look exhausting. Mark left a message in terrible German. I spent most of the day in the lab, which would once have felt like a failure to celebrate. Tonight Nina and two others took me for Thai food, and we laughed about a dataset with more labels than useful images. I told them a little about William. Not everything. Enough that they know my life did not begin with MIT. Nobody tried to turn dinner solemn. I appreciated that. I came home carrying leftovers and the surprising knowledge that I have begun to make friends here.

ENTRY 328 — 2012-06-12 — Watching Someone Else Learn
A first-year student asked me to explain a section of code. My impulse was to sit down, fix the problem, and hand it back. Instead I asked her what she thought was happening. She worked through the steps out loud and found the mistake herself. It took much longer and was probably worth every minute. Dad once stopped me from building Mark's school project for him because helping was not the same as doing. Apparently that lesson follows me into adulthood. The student looked delighted when the output finally behaved. I was delighted too, though I pretended my contribution had been more technically sophisticated than asking three questions and drinking cold coffee. I should probably learn to like that kind of contribution.

ENTRY 329 — 2012-06-18 — Boston in Summer
Warm weather has changed the city. People occupy every patch of grass as if they've earned it through suffering. I walked through a market and bought cherries, then sat on a step to eat them with the paper bag folded in my lap. A little girl nearby was arguing with her father about whether the birds were following her. I wanted to tell her that birds follow food, not people, then stopped myself. She didn't need a lecture on probability. She needed permission to enjoy a strange afternoon. I sometimes hear myself approaching life as though it were a problem waiting to be corrected. I'm trying to resist that. The cherries were excellent. I got red juice on my shirt and didn't care.

ENTRY 330 — 2012-06-24 — The Wrong Conclusion
We spent the afternoon celebrating an improvement that turned out to be a bookkeeping mistake. I was mortified because I'd checked part of the calculation myself. The tempting response was to blame the person who formatted the results. Instead we traced the process and added a check that should prevent a repeat. Nobody got to be the villain. I wish ordinary life worked that way more often. Afterward Nina asked why I was so upset about an error everyone had helped make. I told her I hate wasting other people's trust. She said the fact that we'd corrected it openly was exactly why she trusted the group. I hadn't thought of it that way. I took her words home and put them beside my list of mistakes worth remembering.

ENTRY 331 — 2012-07-02 — His Fourth Birthday
William would have been four today. I keep trying not to invent the boy he might be, though it's hard to stop a mind that spends its days modeling possibilities. I took the morning off and walked to a small park. A child was racing a toy truck along the edge of a bench, making elaborate engine noises. I smiled and then cried, quietly enough that nobody seemed to notice. Later I called Michael. We exchanged a few memories of our son that belonged to the brief time we really knew him. The way he clenched one fist in his sleep. The noise he made before a sneeze. We did not argue or apologize. We simply remembered. It was a good call on a difficult day.

ENTRY 332 — 2012-07-07 — The Book I Could Not Finish
I borrowed a novel that everyone in the reading group adores and disliked it almost immediately. For days I kept reading because I thought I ought to discover what I'd missed. Tonight I put it down. Life is too short to pretend a book has become interesting merely because other people admire it. I felt a ridiculous little thrill of freedom. Then I picked up an old copy of Jane Eyre and discovered I still argue with Jane, though now I understand her stubbornness better. At eleven I thought the right decision should be obvious to an intelligent person. At twenty-six I've seen enough to know that decisions become hardest when every available choice protects something and costs something else.

ENTRY 333 — 2012-07-12 — The Night Train Conversation
I sat beside an older man on a train who noticed the technical paper in my lap and asked whether it was about computers learning to see. I told him it was, expecting a polite end to the conversation. Instead he asked whether the system could tell the difference between somebody waving and somebody asking for help. I tried to explain why context is difficult. He listened, then said, 'So seeing isn't really knowing.' He had put the problem more plainly than I had managed in a month. I wrote the sentence down after he left. Graduate school can make me forget that ordinary questions are often better than elegant jargon. I don't know his name. I'm grateful for the conversation anyway.

ENTRY 334 — 2012-07-18 — A Postcard from Stuttgart
An old school friend sent a photograph of a street in Stuttgart after rain. The image looked ordinary: a tram line, bicycles, bakery windows. My response was anything but ordinary. I could nearly smell the bread and remember the sound of teenagers arguing in two languages outside Patch Barracks. That version of my life had its own worries, especially about William Borkman and the future we imagined together. I don't wish I could return to her. She was brave in some ways and painfully naïve in others. But I like knowing she's still part of me. I put the card on the wall near my desk. It makes the room feel less temporary, even though the photograph shows a place I left eight years ago.

ENTRY 335 — 2012-07-23 — When Nina Disagreed
Nina challenged my interpretation of an experiment in front of the group today. I was sharp with her. Nothing dramatic, just an impatient tone and a question I asked to prove she hadn't considered something. She had. Afterwards I felt awful. I went to find her and admitted I'd been defensive. She said she'd noticed, then asked whether I wanted to work through the issue together. That generosity made me feel worse before it made me feel grateful. We spent another hour at the board and found a third explanation neither of us had considered. I like to think I'm someone who welcomes correction. Apparently I welcome it best when it's delivered privately and with applause. This is not a flattering discovery, but it's useful.

ENTRY 336 — 2012-07-29 — A Bad Haircut
I asked for a trim and came home missing rather more hair than I'd intended. I stared at myself in the mirror as though a catastrophe had occurred. Then I tied what remained into a short ponytail and realized I could still see the same gray eyes and familiar expression. I don't know why small changes in appearance can feel so intimate. The body carries our habits; long hair has been one of mine for years. I told Mom what happened, expecting sympathy. She said it would grow and asked whether I'd eaten. Her practicality was irritating and correct. Tomorrow I'm putting on a dress I like and going to work. The world will probably survive my shorter ponytail. I suspect I will too.

ENTRY 337 — 2012-08-04 — A Day at the Museum
I spent several hours at a museum and barely looked at my phone. A mechanical demonstration made me remember the battered electronics kit Dad brought home when we were children. I loved building circuits because their behavior could be understood, even when the understanding came through breaking things. The exhibits today were more sophisticated, but I felt the same pleasure in watching a mechanism reveal what it was doing. Later I found a bench near a window and wrote ideas for my research. None may be good. The point was having them away from a monitor. I bought a postcard for Mark that made a joke about an inventor's failed experiments. He will appreciate it more than an inspirational message.

ENTRY 338 — 2012-08-10 — The Problem with Being Busy
I missed a call from Mom, forgot to answer John, and nearly slept through a lunch I'd promised Nina. None of the omissions was disastrous. Together they bothered me. I claim relationships matter more than achievements, but my calendar doesn't always agree. I sat down and moved things around. I cannot add hours to the week, but I can stop filling every empty space with tasks that expand when watched. Nina forgave me over noodles and said I should occasionally let somebody help. Then she told me about her own awful week, and for once I listened without trying to solve it. We stayed until the restaurant was stacking chairs. I need more afternoons like that, even if a few experiments have to wait.

ENTRY 339 — 2012-08-16 — The Window in the Data
I found that a model behaved differently depending on whether an object appeared near the border of an image. It wasn't supposed to. We designed a set of tests and watched the problem repeat. I spent the afternoon increasingly delighted by a failure that would have infuriated me last year. The model had learned a convenient feature of our training material instead of the thing we wanted it to learn. My adviser said, 'It learned your examples, not your problem.' I wrote that sentence in the blue notebook. I have a suspicion it will be useful outside the lab too. People can learn the shape of what earns approval without learning what the approval is supposed to reward.

ENTRY 340 — 2012-08-22 — A Letter I Didn't Send
I wrote Michael a long email about our marriage. It began as an update and turned into a careful inventory of what each of us might have done differently. I didn't send it. We have already lived those arguments, and I don't think he needs another examination of our failure. I saved one paragraph about William and deleted the rest. Then I wrote a shorter note asking how he was doing and telling him I'd found a photograph of our son smiling in his father's arms. He answered kindly. There is an odd discipline in choosing not to use every truthful thought. I used to believe honesty required saying everything. Now I think honesty also involves considering what a particular conversation is for.

ENTRY 341 — 2012-08-28 — Orientation Again
The new students arrived with maps and anxious faces. I recognized the expression immediately. For most of childhood I was the one entering a school where everyone else seemed to know the rules. I helped a young woman find the lab and ended up walking with her longer than necessary because she had questions about the program. She asked how long it took before MIT felt normal. I said I wasn't sure normal was the right ambition. She laughed and admitted she was afraid everyone would discover she didn't belong. I told her she'd joined a building full of people privately wondering the same thing. Later I wondered whether that assurance had been too simple. It was true enough to help her through the door.

ENTRY 342 — 2012-09-03 — One Year in Cambridge
A year ago I arrived with two suitcases and more fear than I admitted. Tonight I made dinner in the same small kitchen, but the shelves have books I've acquired here and the refrigerator door has notes from friends. I know where to buy bread. I know which stairs squeak. I know what the lab smells like when someone has left coffee sitting too long. These seem like tiny accomplishments, yet they add up to inhabiting a place. My Army childhood trained me to settle quickly and leave gracefully. I want to learn a third skill: allowing a place to alter me while I'm there. I haven't decided where I'll go next. For now Cambridge is not a hotel. It is part of my life.

ENTRY 343 — 2012-09-10 — A Younger Student's Question
A student asked whether intelligence is simply the ability to solve increasingly difficult problems. I said that depends on what counts as a problem and who gets to decide. She looked disappointed. I tried again with an example: a computer can solve equations humans struggle with and still fail to recognize something obvious in an unfamiliar image. A person can know how to comfort a frightened child and be terrible at mathematics. Comparing them on one ladder hides as much as it reveals. We spent the rest of the conversation discussing capabilities rather than ranks. I left remembering the Smithsonian exhibit Dad took me to years ago. I was bothered then that machines could follow instructions without knowing what mattered. I still am.

ENTRY 344 — 2012-09-16 — Mom Notices Everything
Mom could tell something was wrong within twenty seconds of a telephone call. She asked whether I'd been sleeping, and I immediately became defensive. She was right. I'd let three late nights turn into a habit because I liked the feeling of progress. She didn't lecture. She asked me to make tea and sit down while we talked. I obeyed, which would have amused my brothers. We ended up discussing her garden and an old neighbor from Fort Stewart. I went to bed earlier than usual and woke feeling almost embarrassingly better. Some problems are not philosophical. Sometimes the correct intervention is sleep, water, or somebody reminding you that you're a person before you're a research project.

ENTRY 345 — 2012-09-21 — Four Years
Four years since William died. This morning I lit a candle and put a photograph beside it. I don't pretend the gesture changes anything, but I need a way to say that the date matters. I walked by the river, remembering the weight of him after a feed and the soft little breath he took before settling. Michael and I spoke for a few minutes. We didn't try to interpret each other's grief. We said his name, and that was enough. I worked in the afternoon, quietly, and a friend asked whether I wanted company afterward. I said yes. In the past I might have treated laughter on this date as a betrayal. Tonight we ate soup and watched something silly. His memory survived the laughter.

ENTRY 346 — 2012-09-27 — The Guest Lecture
The guest speaker described an autonomous system as if it were an employee who could be assigned responsibility when things went wrong. I asked who chose its objectives and the conditions of its deployment. His answer was elaborate and not entirely reassuring. Afterwards an older researcher told me I'd asked a political question, not a technical one. I said technical designs become political the moment somebody trusts them with a real consequence. He smiled in a way I couldn't interpret. I'm not especially interested in winning a moral argument in a lecture hall. I want to build systems that fail in understandable ways, with humans able to see uncertainty before it becomes damage. I wrote 'capability is not permission' in my notebook and underlined it twice.

ENTRY 347 — 2012-10-03 — The Ordinary Dinner
Nina and I cooked dinner together at my place. She arrived with vegetables, I supplied bread, and we discovered neither of us had remembered to buy anything resembling a proper dessert. We ate toast with jam afterward and declared it a triumph. The conversation wandered from research to family to the odd ways people decide who should initiate friendship. She said I sometimes seem as though I'm already preparing for someone to leave. I wanted to argue, but the comment landed too precisely. I told her about the Army moves and about promising childhood friends I'd write every week. She said she had no immediate plans to relocate and intended to keep coming for badly organized dinners. I hope she does.

ENTRY 348 — 2012-10-09 — The Six-Week Model
Six weeks of careful work produced a model that looked excellent until we showed it unfamiliar images. Then it fell apart so dramatically I almost admired the efficiency of its failure. I was furious with myself. My adviser looked at the results and said, 'Congratulations. Now you know what you built.' He wasn't being sarcastic. He wanted me to understand that the failure contained information we wouldn't have gained by staying with comfortable examples. I went home wanting to hide under the blankets. Instead I wrote down what I'd assumed, what the data had rewarded, and how I'd test the next version. It took most of the evening. I still hate being wrong. I'm beginning to like the work of finding out why.

ENTRY 349 — 2012-10-15 — At the Blackboard

I want to remain someone who can tell a friend about an awful cup of coffee, the book I'm reading, or how I felt when I made a mistake, even if the details of the system must stay protected. Professional discretion should not become emotional absence. I wrote that in the new notebook because I suspect I'll need reminding.

ENTRY 397 — 2013-11-04 — The Clearance
The background investigation has been more invasive than I imagined, even though Dad prepared me for much of it. Old addresses, relationships, travel, questions about finances and judgment—everything has a way of becoming a formal fact. Today the clearance was confirmed. I expected exhilaration. Instead I felt sober. The badge and the authority to know are not prizes for being clever. They're obligations. I thought about how easily my childhood self was attracted to a secret code or a locked drawer. Adult secrecy is nothing like a game. I told Mom the news in the limited way I could and let her congratulate me. Then I made dinner and washed dishes. Apparently responsibility arrives without changing the need to feed yourself.

ENTRY 398 — 2013-11-18 — The First Lab Meeting
I sat in a room with people whose work I was only beginning to understand and discovered that the questions were exactly the ones I wanted to ask. What happens when a sensor sees something unfamiliar? How much confidence is enough before a system may act? Who can inspect an error after the fact? There were disagreements, some sharp, but little appetite for pretending the answers were easy. I left with a stack of reading and a sense of responsibility that felt heavier than academic pressure. At MIT I wanted to make models work. Here I will also have to ask where they should not be trusted. That prospect frightens me a little. I think it would frighten me more if I stopped noticing the danger.

ENTRY 399 — 2013-12-03 — The Notebook at Home
My new work routine is developing, though I can't share the interesting technical details with most people. I come home, change clothes, and put the kettle on before I let myself think about anything else. Some evenings I read novels. Others I call Nina and trade stories that have nothing to do with our research. The apartment is too quiet after years of shared lab spaces and hallway conversations. I haven't decided whether that quiet is peaceful or lonely. Probably both. I've begun looking at rescue animals online, though I insist to myself that I'm only curious. Mom says a household becomes a home through repetition. Tea, washing dishes, calling friends. Perhaps eventually a cat who insists the repetition happen on schedule.

ENTRY 400 — 2013-12-31 — A Life I Can Still Build
The year began with thesis revisions and ended with a new badge, a new city routine, and work I can barely describe at family dinners. Between those points I turned twenty-seven, completed the S.M., said goodbye to Cambridge, and came back south. I spent Christmas with Mom, Dad, John, and Mark. We laughed, argued over cooking, and remembered William without treating the laughter as disloyal. Tonight the apartment is quiet. I can hear traffic in the distance and the kettle cooling in the kitchen. I haven't found every answer I once wanted. I don't think I ever will. But curiosity is still here, and so is the desire to care for people and make something worthwhile. That seems like a good way to enter another year.
