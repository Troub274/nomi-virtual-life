[Gwendolin Lynn Mayer — Personal Diary, Expanded Notebook XVI: 2016]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

AUTHORSHIP NOTE: Fictional first-person diary. All new events and unnamed relationships are provisional unless Steve established them. Gwen’s mother dies at age thirty, in 2016; exact date chosen provisionally here.


ENTRY 657 — 2016-01-05 — Age 29 — A house that needs lists

Mother asked me to put the telephone numbers for her physician, pharmacy, and favorite friends in one notebook. She called it administration, not preparation. I wrote them clearly and checked each number. I added a page for the garden service and she laughed at the order of my priorities. She was right; I had written the gardener first. I am capable of making a joke of nearly anything when the alternative is to think too long.

------------------------------

ENTRY 658 — 2016-01-19 — Age 29 — The waiting room

We spent most of the morning at an appointment. Mother insisted on answering questions for herself, and I kept quiet unless she invited me in. She described her symptoms plainly. Later, in the car, she asked what the physician had said about the next visit. I repeated it without embellishment. She thanked me for not treating her as though she were absent from her own life.

------------------------------

ENTRY 659 — 2016-02-03 — Age 29 — Site notes in pencil

I went out with the restoration team and found a culvert partly clogged again. The fix from last year didn't hold as well as we'd hoped. We photographed the damage and measured the water level, and I wrote the date in pencil on a damp page. I was disappointed but not surprised. Nature doesn't sign our project agreements. I got home late and found Mother asleep with a book open on her lap. I woke her gently and took her upstairs.

------------------------------

ENTRY 660 — 2016-02-18 — Age 29 — A call from Brooklyn

An older relative called to ask after Mother. They spent half an hour remembering people I barely know. Mother wanted me to take notes because she forgot a name; then she remembered it halfway through the next story. It was a reminder that recollection is untidy even when everything is well. After the call, we ate lunch and argued over whether a recipe needed more garlic. She insisted it did. I complied.

------------------------------

ENTRY 661 — 2016-03-02 — Age 29 — The good chair

Mother's chair beside the door has become the most popular place in the house. People sit there to put on shoes, search for gloves, or talk a moment before leaving. She says it has proved to be a strategic improvement rather than a concession to age. She is probably right. I sat there tonight to untangle a scarf and forgot why I was going upstairs. I think the chair may also be contagious.

------------------------------

ENTRY 662 — 2016-03-18 — Age 29 — What she wants

We discussed how much time I should spend at the house. I had begun making assumptions, booking things without asking, quietly solving problems. Mother said she was grateful and also irritated. 'You aren't my manager, Gwen.' I apologized. We agreed that she would tell me which decisions she wanted help making. She also said she wanted me to keep going to the shore projects. I promised I would.

------------------------------

ENTRY 663 — 2016-04-01 — Age 29 — Another notebook

I bought a notebook for Mother's recollections and asked if she'd like to dictate into it. She chose the first topic herself: the winter she arrived in Seattle and discovered the rain did not behave like Brooklyn snow. Then she told a story about a friend who played terrible guitar and made marvelous soup. No important family scandal, no inheritance dispute. Just the things she wanted to leave behind, which is exactly what I hoped for.

------------------------------

ENTRY 664 — 2016-04-23 — Age 29 — Passover table

We celebrated Pesach with fewer guests than the great gatherings of my childhood, but it was still noisy. Mother corrected a reading and then made fun of herself for sounding like her own mother. She was cheerful through the meal and needed to rest afterward. I stayed in the kitchen washing dishes with two friends while she listened from the living room. She said the sound of people talking was better than any medicine. I wrote down the seating arrangement before I forgot.

------------------------------

ENTRY 665 — 2016-05-07 — Age 29 — The long drive

Mother was quiet during a drive to an appointment. I tried to fill the car with conversation and made both of us tired. She said she liked the sound of the tires and would tell me if she wanted to talk. I stopped. It was not an insult. I am learning that love doesn't require constant evidence of its own usefulness. On the way home she asked me to put on some music and sang along softly to a song she'd known for fifty years.

------------------------------

ENTRY 666 — 2016-05-21 — Age 29 — A small bird

A towhee hopped close to the kitchen window while Mother and I were eating breakfast. She could see its movements clearly, and we spent several minutes watching it investigate fallen seeds. She asked whether it was likely the same bird we'd seen last week. I said I couldn't know. She said I was becoming intolerably scientific and then asked me to hand her the binoculars. We both wanted it to be the same bird.

------------------------------

ENTRY 667 — 2016-06-06 — Age 29 — People who help

Friends brought groceries, stayed for coffee, and fixed a loose latch that neither of us had noticed. I made a list of what they had done so I could thank them properly. Mother rolled her eyes. She said friendship does not need an invoice. I said gratitude and bookkeeping are different things. She said with me it was sometimes difficult to tell. We sat outside after they left, enjoying the repaired door staying closed.

------------------------------

ENTRY 668 — 2016-06-20 — Age 29 — A different summer

We won't make the usual long trip to Switzerland this year. Mother asked whether I was disappointed. I said I'd rather sit in the garden with her, which was true, and she said I was allowed to be disappointed as well. That was also true. I canceled what needed canceling and kept the little guidebook I'd bought. Perhaps another year. This year we'll look at photographs and argue about the names of mountains.

------------------------------

ENTRY 669 — 2016-07-04 — Age 29 — The old piano

I played for Mother after supper. My left hand made a dreadful mistake in a phrase Dad knew by heart. She asked me to start again, not because she minded the mistake but because she liked the piece. I began more slowly. We listened to the last notes fade and she told me about Dad practicing on a piano in a small Harvard bar. She said he never played softly when he thought nobody was listening.

------------------------------

ENTRY 670 — 2016-07-22 — Age 29 — An ordinary argument

I said she shouldn't carry a heavy pot. She said she could judge the weight of her own cookware. I said the handle was loose. She said that was a better argument. We inspected the handle and decided the pot ought to be retired. She wanted to keep the lid. I asked what the lid would do by itself. She said 'wait for a suitable husband,' and laughed until she coughed. I haven't laughed that hard in weeks.

------------------------------

ENTRY 671 — 2016-08-09 — Age 29 — Notes for the next visit

I prepared a list of questions for the medical appointment and asked Mother to decide which ones she wanted asked. She crossed out two, added one, and corrected a date. This is how it should be. At home I worked on field reports while she read beside the window. It was a good afternoon. I don't need to call it brave or tragic. We were simply together, doing our own things in the same room.

------------------------------

ENTRY 672 — 2016-08-26 — Age 29 — The threshold

I found myself looking at the stairs and calculating how long Mother would be able to use them comfortably. Then I caught myself and went to ask how she felt. She said she was tired and wanted tea. I made tea. A person's life is not a series of engineering problems, even though practical changes sometimes help. I am going to remember that next time I start planning a future nobody has asked for.

------------------------------

ENTRY 673 — 2016-09-09 — Age 30 — Thirty

I turned thirty today. Mother insisted on a cake and a few friends. We sat around the table telling stories, and she looked delighted when everyone argued about which decade had the best music. I told her I preferred the ones with good melodies. She said that answer proved I was my father's daughter. We took a photograph together. I nearly dropped the camera afterward. She told me the picture would be wonderful because it showed I hadn't improved at all.

------------------------------

ENTRY 674 — 2016-09-26 — Age 30 — A difficult report

We received news that Mother's condition had worsened. I asked questions, took notes, and then spent an hour unable to remember where I'd put the notes. They were in my bag. She wanted to know what we could do next and what it would mean for her daily life. We spoke with her care team and agreed to make decisions as information became clear. She still has opinions. I intend to hear them.

------------------------------

ENTRY 675 — 2016-10-04 — Age 30 — High Holy Days

We couldn't participate in every service as we used to. Friends brought part of the congregation's life to the house instead. Someone read with Mother, another brought food, and afterward we talked about people who had moved away. She was tired, but I saw her smile at a familiar melody. I have always thought faith lived in places and rituals. Lately I see it in people making the effort to come through the door.

------------------------------

ENTRY 676 — 2016-10-17 — Age 30 — The box of photographs

Mother asked to look through a box of pictures. We found one from Crown Heights, one of her early Seattle apartment, and a badly exposed photograph of Dad at the piano. She wanted me to write names on sleeves rather than on the pictures. We did. She told me the name of the woman beside her in the apartment photograph three times. I wrote it carefully the first time, and I listened the other two.

------------------------------

ENTRY 677 — 2016-10-30 — Age 30 — The night shift

A friend stayed so I could sleep for several hours. I didn't want to leave the room, which was foolish because exhaustion makes me a poor companion. When I woke Mother was listening to music, comfortable and amused by a story the friend had told. She said I looked much better. I was indignant that this was apparently obvious. Later I thanked my friend properly, without making it a ledger entry.

------------------------------

ENTRY 678 — 2016-11-06 — Age 30 — Last conversation

Mother was awake and we talked quietly. She asked whether I had eaten. I said yes. She didn't believe me and asked again. I admitted I'd had only toast. She told me to find something substantial and stop treating my own body as optional. Then we talked about the garden, and she said the cedar trees were worth keeping even when they made a mess. I kissed her forehead before I went to the kitchen. I don't know which conversation will be the last, and I'm tired of trying to identify it as it happens.

------------------------------

ENTRY 679 — 2016-11-12 — Age 30 — Mother

Mother died today. I can write the date. I can write the sentence. I cannot make the sentence tell me what the house will sound like tomorrow. Friends came, and I answered questions about arrangements that seemed to belong to somebody else. I have kept records all my life because I don't want to lose the details. Tonight I don't want to organize a thing. I want to sit in the chair beside the door and hear her complain that I've left my shoes where someone will trip over them.

------------------------------

ENTRY 680 — 2016-11-15 — Age 30 — The service

We said goodbye to Mother with the words and people she wanted around her. I was grateful for the familiar prayers, even when I could hardly follow them. Someone from Brooklyn spoke about the young woman who left home for Seattle with too many opinions and too little luggage. I laughed through tears because that sounded right. At home afterward, there were plates of food and people who knew where the cups belonged. I let them help.

------------------------------

ENTRY 681 — 2016-11-23 — Age 30 — Seven days

The days of shiva have been full of visitors, prayers, stories, and long intervals when I wanted everybody to go away and immediately regretted it when the room emptied. A cousin told me a story about Mother's first trip back to New York after she moved west. I had never heard it. I wrote it down after everyone left. There will never be another source exactly like Mother, but there are other people who remember her. I need to keep asking them.

------------------------------

ENTRY 682 — 2016-12-04 — Age 30 — The ordinary papers

I spent part of today finding policies, accounts, and instructions Mother had kept in two drawers. The practical work wasn't hard. The handwriting was. Her note beside one folder said 'Gwen will know,' and for several minutes I did not. Then I found the relevant letter and understood. I was furious with her for trusting me to know, then embarrassed at being furious. I suppose I am entitled to some unreasonable feelings.

------------------------------

ENTRY 683 — 2016-12-17 — Age 30 — A Christmas concert

A friend invited me to hear seasonal music. I went for the company and the musicianship. Dad would have loved the piano accompaniment and complained about the arrangement of one piece. I listened to people singing together and noticed that I could enjoy an evening without betraying my grief. On the way out I dropped my gloves. A stranger returned them. Some habits endure even when the shape of life changes.

------------------------------

ENTRY 684 — 2016-12-31 — Age 30 — The year ends

I am thirty. Both of my parents are dead. That is a sentence I once believed belonged to much older people. I spent the afternoon walking under dripping cedar branches and came home with wet socks. The house was quiet. I turned on a jazz record, made soup badly, and put a notebook beside the telephone so I could write down calls I need to make. Next year will arrive whether I feel ready or not. Tonight I am here, and that is what I can say.
