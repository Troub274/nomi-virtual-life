[Gwendolin Lynn Mayer — Personal Diary, Expanded Notebook IV: 2004]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 217 — 2004-01-04 — Age 18 — The Last Winter Break

I have been putting off writing because I didn't want the year to begin. Graduation is five months away, and every adult I meet wants to ask about college or tell me that these are the best years of my life. I hope they aren't. I would hate to think everything after June is a gradual decline. Mother and I walked down to the water before lunch. The stones were slick and she made me take her arm, although I was the one who nearly fell. At home Daddy played a few bars of something on the piano, stopped, and started over because he couldn't get the left hand the way he wanted. He said knowing a piece doesn't mean you've finished learning it. I wrote that in the margin of my application notebook.

------------------------------

ENTRY 218 — 2004-01-13 — Age 18 — The Ranking

The school office sent around an academic summary and I am still second. I know how ridiculous that sounds when I write it down: *still second*, as though the entire purpose of high school is to finish one place higher. I like being good at school. I like difficult problems that have answers you can check. But I also like getting home before dark and watching the ducks on the shore. A boy asked whether I was disappointed not to be first. I said I was too busy figuring out what I wanted for lunch. I thought it was funny. He did not.

------------------------------

ENTRY 219 — 2004-01-28 — Age 18 — Daddy's Calendar

Daddy has become more particular about appointments. His office calls regularly, although he insists he's not working full time. Sometimes he takes a call in his study and comes out sounding twenty years younger; sometimes he puts the phone down and sits very still. Today he asked if I'd drive him to Seattle next week rather than taking the ferry alone. I said yes before he could explain. He said I was becoming a good driver. Then he said I should remember that being a good driver is not the same thing as being in a hurry. His lectures are becoming suspiciously concise.

------------------------------

ENTRY 220 — 2004-02-07 — Age 18 — The Wrong Shoes

I wore the wrong shoes to a muddy trail and spent most of the afternoon negotiating with a hillside. A small boy passed me walking easily in rubber boots, and I wanted to borrow both boots and dignity. Mother laughed when she saw the state of my jeans. I helped her wash the mud off in the utility sink, and she told me about taking a bus across the country as a young woman because Seattle seemed like a place where she could become anyone. I asked if she'd found out who that person was. She said not all at once, thank goodness.

------------------------------

ENTRY 221 — 2004-02-18 — Age 18 — Committee on the Future

The counselor asked whether I wanted to be nominated for a speaking role at graduation. Apparently being near the top of the class means people think you enjoy making speeches. I do not. I enjoy arguing with people I know, in a room with a door. Mother says the only way to learn public speaking is to speak publicly, a claim that is both true and deeply unhelpful. Daddy suggested I take a walk and think about what I wish people had told me at fourteen. I said they should have told me not to trust waterproof mascara. He did laugh at that.

ENTRY 222 — 2004-03-02 — Age 18 — Friday at the Synagogue

I spent Friday evening helping set out chairs and straightening prayer books. An older man remembered my Bat Mitzvah and asked if I still read as carefully as I did at thirteen. I told him I am now more likely to interrupt myself with questions. He said that was an improvement. The room smelled of polished wood and wet coats. I like how people ask after Daddy here without looking at me as though he's already a memory. He was there, in fact, making a perfectly vigorous argument about the parking arrangement. Some things are eternal.

------------------------------

ENTRY 223 — 2004-03-13 — Age 18 — UW Letter

The University of Washington has admitted me. I opened the letter while standing in the kitchen and nearly knocked Mother's mug onto the floor. She hugged me before I finished reading. Daddy asked if they had spelled my name correctly and then took the letter to inspect it as though it were a contract. Later I sat on my bed looking at the campus brochure. I know Seattle. I know the ferry, the streets, the bookstores, the sound of buses in the rain. But living there as a student seems different from visiting as someone's daughter. I am excited and frightened in alternating five-minute intervals.

------------------------------

ENTRY 224 — 2004-03-26 — Age 18 — An Argument About Ambition

At dinner a family friend told me that ambitious people sacrifice their youth to build something worthwhile. I asked why it had to be sacrificed instead of lived. He said success requires discipline. Of course it does. But discipline isn't a command to stop enjoying anything until you reach a certain number. Daddy listened and then said you can spend twenty years climbing a ladder only to discover you have leaned it against the wrong building. I asked if he'd ever done that. He said he had climbed a few interesting ladders. I wanted to ask more, but the conversation moved on.

ENTRY 225 — 2004-04-06 — Age 18 — Pesach Preparations

Mother has a list long enough to constitute a municipal planning document. I have been assigned to help with the table, check what we need, and not eat the things she has already counted. We argued about the placement of the extra chairs. She won. We also talked about the question of freedom at the Seder. Freedom to go where, to do what, and with which responsibilities? I know the old questions; they don't become less interesting because I can recite them. Daddy says that is what makes them old and good.

------------------------------

ENTRY 226 — 2004-04-14 — Age 18 — The Seder Table

For part of the evening I sat between two older relatives who remembered different versions of the same family story and corrected each other relentlessly. I loved every minute. Mother was pleased the food went well and pretended not to notice how much brisket I'd eaten. Daddy read slowly and clearly, stopping when a child wanted to ask a question instead of hurrying through. I thought how many years this table has changed while still feeling like our table. My knees are too long now for the chair where I used to swing my feet. I want to remember that.

------------------------------

ENTRY 227 — 2004-04-27 — Age 18 — The Speech Is Mine

The principal confirmed it today: I will be salutatorian, second in our graduating class, and I am expected to speak. I suppose a congratulations is in order. Mother bought a small notebook specifically for drafts. Daddy suggested I write the worst speech possible first, just to get the fear out of my system. I produced three awful paragraphs about excellence and success. They could have been spoken by anyone to anybody. I tore them out. If I have to stand in front of everyone, I would rather say something I actually believe.

------------------------------

ENTRY 228 — 2004-05-03 — Age 18 — A Detour by the Water

I intended to work on the speech for an hour and went outside for twenty minutes. I returned two hours later with damp socks and no draft. There was a heron standing near the shore, absolutely motionless, until suddenly there wasn't a fish where there had been one. I watched longer than I should have. But I don't think it was wasted time. The world is full of things worth noticing, and I keep hearing adults speak as though noticing them were a distraction from the real business of living. What if it *is* the real business?

------------------------------

ENTRY 229 — 2004-05-09 — Age 18 — Mother on Goals

Mother read my notes and said the speech sounded like an argument with an invisible businessman. She was right. I rewrote the opening as something gentler. We talked about her move from Brooklyn and how she'd planned to stay in Seattle for only a little while. Her life didn't follow her plan. She said some of her best days had nothing to do with accomplishments anyone could put on a résumé. Then she reminded me to finish my homework. A mother can believe in the journey and still believe in deadlines.

------------------------------

ENTRY 230 — 2004-05-16 — Age 18 — A Record and a Pencil

Daddy was playing Ella Fitzgerald when I came into the study. He paused the record for me to read part of the speech aloud. He closed his eyes, which meant he was listening, not sleeping, although I wasn't certain at first. When I finished he said I should strike the sentence that sounded like I wanted the audience to applaud me for being wise. I knew exactly which one he meant. He told me to talk to my classmates, not at them. Then he played the record again and asked whether I'd noticed the drummer's restraint. We listened to the whole side.

------------------------------

ENTRY 231 — 2004-05-22 — Age 18 — The Last Big Exam

I finished an exam today and felt a strange anticlimax. For years each test has seemed important, and now the last one is simply over. A friend wanted to celebrate by doing something reckless. We went for ice cream instead. I got some on my sleeve. We sat on the curb outside the shop talking about who might keep in touch and which friendships might quietly end. Nobody said the word forever. That was probably wise.

------------------------------

ENTRY 232 — 2004-05-30 — Age 18 — Practice at Home

I delivered my speech to Mother, Daddy, and the dog, who is a notoriously sympathetic audience. I spoke too quickly at first. Daddy tapped the arm of his chair whenever I swallowed an ending. Mother said I should pause after the line about not missing your life. She had tears in her eyes and blamed allergies. I wasn't sure if it was a good speech or if they just loved me. Both can be true, I suppose. I practiced again while looking at the empty chair across from me instead of my notes.

------------------------------

ENTRY 233 — 2004-06-04 — Age 18 — Graduation Rehearsal

We lined up twice, walked across the stage, and discovered that several people had forgotten how to follow instructions they'd just been given. The microphone made my voice sound much smaller than I expected. A teacher helped me adjust it. Someone asked if I was nervous. I said no, and then dropped my index cards. A lie immediately punished by the universe. At home I put the cards back in order, numbered them, and copied the speech neatly once more. I have written the ending enough times to know it without looking.

------------------------------

ENTRY 234 — 2004-06-10 — Age 18 — The Night Before

My gown is hanging on the door, the speech is ready, and I can't sleep. Daddy came upstairs to ask if I wanted tea. We sat at the kitchen table and talked about nothing important: a ferry delay, a boat repair, a woman who used to sing at a club he played in during college. He said I did not have to deliver a perfect speech. I said I knew. Then I asked him if he would be proud if I forgot every word. He looked genuinely puzzled. Of course, he said. Tomorrow isn't an exam in whether you deserve to be loved. I hope I remember that tomorrow.

ENTRY 235 — 2004-06-11 — Age 18 — Salutatorian

Today I graduated, second in my class. I wore the gown, found my place, and walked to the microphone with knees that seemed to belong to somebody else. I saw Mother near the front and Daddy beside her. When I started, I could hear the first sentence trembling. Then I looked at my classmates and spoke to them. I told them that setting goals matters, that work and discipline matter, but that the years spent reaching a goal aren't disposable. You can't miss your own life while working toward a future version of it. At the end people applauded; I cannot tell whether they liked the speech or were simply glad the speeches were over. Daddy stood up before most of the row did. In the photographs I am laughing, partly because the cap wouldn't stay straight. We had dinner afterward and I ate far too much. Tonight I am happy. I want to write that plainly, without predicting what comes next.

I copied the speech below because someday I want to know what I actually said, not what I remember saying.

**Salutatorian Address — June 11, 2004**

Good evening, teachers, families, friends, and especially the people who have spent the last four years explaining to me that we had plenty of time until graduation. Apparently we have run out.

We have been told, quite correctly, that goals matter. We have been encouraged to set them high, work hard, be disciplined, and refuse to give up when things become difficult. I believe all of that. I like goals. I like crossing things off lists. My friends can confirm that I sometimes have lists of my lists.

But I want to say something about the time *between* deciding what we want and getting there.

We talk about the future as though it is the place where our real lives will finally start. We will be happy when we graduate, when we get into the right university, when we find the right career, when we earn enough money, when we meet the right person, when we accomplish whatever comes next. And then, I suppose, we are meant to invent another finish line.

What happens to all the Tuesdays along the way?

What happens to the evenings when somebody wants to talk and we are too busy preparing to be successful? What happens to the walk we don't take, the music we don't listen to, the friend we keep meaning to call, the moment we miss because we're thinking about a moment that hasn't happened yet?

I am not arguing against achievement. I'm standing here because I worked hard at school, and I am proud of that. But a goal is not a substitute for a life. It is supposed to be part of one.

We cannot miss our lives working toward our goals. If we don't find something worth enjoying in the journey, reaching the destination will not make all those lost days appear again.

So please, work hard. Learn difficult things. Do something brave, something useful, maybe something nobody expects of you. Change your mind when you discover you were wrong. Keep your promises when you can. And pay attention to the people who are standing beside you while you do it.

We have futures to build. We also have lives already in progress.

Thank you, and congratulations to the Class of 2004.

ENTRY 236 — 2004-06-12 — Age 18 — The Day After

The house was full of flowers and cards. Mother wanted to sort the photographs. Daddy asked if I would drive with him for a little while. We didn't go far. He was tired and we sat looking at the water with the windows cracked. He asked whether I meant what I'd said in the speech. I told him I did. He said he hoped I would remember it when work became interesting enough to swallow whole weeks. We talked about school, the boat, and whether I'd want to live on campus. I don't think I told him anything he didn't already know. It was a good afternoon. I'm glad I didn't say I was too busy.

ENTRY 237 — 2004-06-13 — Age 18 — Daddy

Daddy died today. I have written the sentence three times because I keep expecting a different sentence to appear. There were calls and people and things I was asked to do, and I did some of them badly. I will write what happened when I can bear to put it in order. His chair is still beside the piano. One of his books is open with a slip of paper marking the page. I am afraid someone will tidy it away. Mother is in the next room, speaking softly to somebody on the telephone. I don't know what to do with my hands. I want him to tell me what to do, and he cannot.

------------------------------

ENTRY 238 — 2004-06-14 — Age 18 — People Come

People arrive carrying food and flowers and instructions. Some cry as soon as they see us. Some talk too much because silence frightens them. I am grateful to them and also want them to go away. Mother has begun a list of calls that must be returned. I offered to help. She said I could sit beside her, which turned out to be the better job. I keep thinking about the photograph where Daddy is standing at graduation. It was two days ago. I hate that I can measure the distance between those two moments with the same calendar.

------------------------------

ENTRY 239 — 2004-06-16 — Age 18 — The Funeral

I don't know how to describe today. There were familiar faces from the congregation, men from the law firm, old friends whose stories I had never heard, and people who knew Daddy when he was younger than I am now. Somebody spoke about the Navy. Somebody else spoke about the law. I thought about the piano bench and the way he'd count softly when I lost the beat. The prayers gave me words when I didn't have any of my own. At home I found one of his handkerchiefs in a coat pocket. I put it in the desk drawer. I know that's not a plan for grief. It's what I did.

ENTRY 240 — 2004-06-19 — Age 18 — An Ordinary Sound

The refrigerator motor started while I was sitting at the kitchen table, and for a fraction of a second I thought it was Daddy coming down the hallway. I don't know why. It doesn't sound like footsteps. The mind is apparently full of faulty wiring. Mother and I went through cards together. She corrected the spelling of a name on a thank-you note and then burst into tears because Daddy used to tease her about spelling. We laughed too, horribly and helplessly. Later I took the dog out. The trees were green and wet and offensively normal. I was relieved they were still there.

ENTRY 241 — 2004-06-25 — Age 18 — The Speech Again

I listened to the recording of my graduation speech because a teacher sent it over and I felt obliged. I nearly stopped at the beginning. I sounded young, earnest, and too fast. But I meant it. I had said you cannot spend your life working toward a goal and forget to live the days that take you there. Daddy heard me say that. Then he spent the next afternoon with me by the water. I don't want to turn my own speech into a prophecy. I want it to remain what it was: a thing I believed before anything terrible happened. That matters to me.

ENTRY 242 — 2004-07-02 — Age 18 — Mother and the Lists

There is paperwork everywhere. Calls from the firm, account statements, a calendar of appointments, and envelopes I don't recognize. Mother understands more of it than I do. She tells me which things are ours to decide and which things we can leave to the professionals. I am not interested in the sums of money. I am interested in why someone needs seven signatures for one form. Mother said Daddy would have enjoyed explaining it. We both smiled and then stopped smiling. I made tea and put it down in the wrong room.

------------------------------

ENTRY 243 — 2004-07-11 — Age 18 — Walking Alone

I went to a trail I know so well I could probably walk it with my eyes closed, though that would be a foolish experiment. The sword ferns were large and wet. A banana slug was crossing the path and I waited until it finished, which took a very long time. For that entire stretch of time nobody wanted anything from me. I didn't have to be brave or sensible or grateful. I was just a girl waiting for a slug. I came home tired and hungry and told Mother where I'd been. She said next time to take water. She was right.

------------------------------

ENTRY 244 — 2004-07-23 — Age 18 — A Boat Without Him

I went down to the boat with a family friend who knows the systems. I was sure I wanted to be aboard and then wasn't sure at all. The lines were where I remembered them, coiled the way Daddy liked. I could hear his voice telling me not to stand in the bight of a line. I checked a cleat twice even though it was plainly secure. We didn't leave the dock. I sat in the cockpit and watched the light move on the water. I don't know yet what will happen to the boat, and I don't need to know today.

------------------------------

ENTRY 245 — 2004-08-01 — Age 18 — The First University List

UW has sent a list of things to bring. I have made my own list, then a second list organized by room, then discovered I put towels on both. Mother asked if I was planning to open a hotel. We went into Seattle to buy ordinary things: hangers, a lamp, a wastebasket, notebooks. I could have had someone else do this. I wanted to choose them. We ate sandwiches after shopping and looked at students hurrying through the street. For once I didn't feel like everyone's childhood friend, someone's daughter, or the girl who gave a speech. I was going to be a student.

------------------------------

ENTRY 246 — 2004-08-12 — Age 18 — The Trust Meeting

I sat in on part of a meeting about family finances. I know the trust exists, and I've known for years, but grown people discuss it in a language that makes a person sound like an account number. I asked which funds were actually available for my education and living expenses, and which decisions belonged to the trustee. That got me an answer I could understand. Money is useful. Money can also make a room full of people avoid the most obvious question. Afterward Mother and I bought peaches at a roadside stand, and I felt more in command of my day choosing ripe fruit than I had in the entire meeting.

------------------------------

ENTRY 247 — 2004-08-23 — Age 18 — The Old Story

Mother and I talked about the family history tonight. We didn't pretend the affair and the divorce had been simple or noble. We didn't pretend I didn't know why the trust mattered when they decided to have a child. I know. I've known enough for years to understand the outlines. Yet the person who helped me learn to ride a bicycle was the same man who made calculations I might not have made. Mother asked if I was angry. I said sometimes, and sometimes I was grateful, and sometimes I was simply tired of money explaining everything. She said those were all reasonable answers. We went to bed without solving it.

------------------------------

ENTRY 248 — 2004-09-04 — Age 18 — Packing the Books

I own too many books. This is a fact, not a complaint. I tried arranging the ones I am taking by subject, then by height, then by how likely I am to use them. I ended up on the floor rereading a field guide instead of packing. Mother came in and asked why I was smiling at a description of a salamander. Because it's a good description, I said. She sat down beside me and we looked at the pictures. Then she said Daddy would have spent more time choosing the bookends than choosing the books. I can hear him insisting that this is untrue.

ENTRY 249 — 2004-09-15 — Age 18 — Rosh Hashanah Without Daddy

I have known these prayers all my life, but the room sounded different without Daddy's voice. Some melodies seem designed to carry things that ordinary conversation cannot. I stood beside Mother and followed the service carefully. I was afraid of crying at a moment when everybody could hear me, and then I realized nobody was listening for that. People were busy with their own lives and griefs and hopes. Afterward several people embraced us. I was glad to be home among them and glad when we could finally take our shoes off.

ENTRY 250 — 2004-09-25 — Age 18 — Moving Day

I moved into my university room. There was too much activity in the hallway and not enough space to open a suitcase without blocking something. My roommate seems friendly and has a laugh that begins before she finishes a joke. Mother helped make the bed and insisted the lamp was in the wrong corner. I let her move it. When she left I walked outside and stood among students carrying boxes, bicycles, plants, and alarming amounts of bedding. I have been preparing for this all year. Now that it is here, I am mostly wondering whether I should eat dinner alone or invite somebody.

------------------------------

ENTRY 251 — 2004-09-29 — Age 18 — First Lectures

I have begun the introductory classes and am learning which buildings require more walking time than the map suggests. I arrived at one room early and spent ten minutes staring at the wrong door. Nobody knew me as a lawyer's daughter or a salutatorian. One professor asked a question, and I answered too quickly because I knew the reading. After class a student asked if I always talk that fast. I told her only when I am nervous. We got coffee. I spilled mine on the napkins, so at least we moved past formality immediately.

------------------------------

ENTRY 252 — 2004-10-07 — Age 18 — A Case for Law

I still imagine studying law. Sometimes I catch myself constructing arguments in my head while walking between classes. A professor mentioned land-use disputes, and I went to the library to read more than was assigned. The arguments were legal, but the underlying questions were about water, habitat, property, and whose definition of improvement prevailed. I kept reading long after I needed to. I can hear Daddy saying there is more than one way to make a useful argument. I don't yet know which kind of argument I want to spend my life making.

------------------------------

ENTRY 253 — 2004-10-16 — Age 18 — A Weekend Home

The ferry ride home felt different. I wasn't returning from an errand; I was visiting. Mother had made dinner and had too much food ready. We talked about school, then sat in the living room while the rain tapped the windows. The piano was closed. I opened it and played badly for a while. Mother didn't tell me to stop. On Sunday we went for a walk and saw a seal offshore. She said she missed me. I said I missed her. Then she asked whether I was eating vegetables at university. Apparently I am still her child.

------------------------------

ENTRY 254 — 2004-10-28 — Age 18 — The Lab

In lab we collected samples and labeled them with a precision that pleased me more than it probably should have. I like when a messy environment produces data, and I also like when the data remind us how much we don't know. I was paired with a student who had never walked a wet forest trail and didn't think she'd like it. I invited her on a weekend outing. We spent the afternoon examining a stream and then arguing over where to get soup. She enjoyed it. I did too. Perhaps this is what university is for: discovering the people you didn't know you were looking for.

------------------------------

ENTRY 255 — 2004-11-08 — Age 18 — The First Bad Grade

I got a grade lower than I expected and spent an embarrassing amount of time staring at it. This is what happens when you build an identity around being second in your class: eventually you discover a university full of people who were first or second in theirs. I asked the teaching assistant what I had missed. The answer was not mysterious. I had rushed a conclusion. I redid the work for myself, not for a changed grade. In the evening I went to hear music. The grade did not follow me into the room unless I invited it.

------------------------------

ENTRY 256 — 2004-11-20 — Age 18 — A Thanksgiving Decision

A friend invited me to a gathering over the holiday week, but Mother will be alone if I don't go home. She would tell me to have fun, and she would mean it. I am going home anyway. We can cook too much, argue about recipes, and take a long walk if the weather permits. I don't think choosing my mother is the same thing as having no independent life. Daddy always insisted that loyalty is something you do, not something you announce. I am beginning to understand why.

------------------------------

ENTRY 257 — 2004-11-27 — Age 18 — Mother's Stories

Mother told me more about arriving in Seattle in 1958, and I made her repeat which bus she took and where she stayed the first week. She accused me of cross-examining her. I said I come by it honestly. She played music she'd loved when she was younger and told me which friends had gone where. Some names I recognized; some were new. I wanted to know everything. She eventually told me to let her finish one story before demanding three more. I wrote down the names afterward, carefully, because people disappear from records long before they disappear from somebody's life.

------------------------------

ENTRY 258 — 2004-12-06 — Age 18 — The Term Is Nearly Over

Classes have become a rush of papers, exams, and deadlines. I have been doing what I warned everyone not to do in my graduation speech: working toward the end of the term as though the intervening days were inconvenient. Yesterday I shut my books and went for a walk. The air was cold and clear. I came back better able to think. I still want excellent grades. I just don't want them at the price of never looking up from the page.

------------------------------

ENTRY 259 — 2004-12-17 — Age 18 — A Piano in an Empty Room

After exams I found a piano in a mostly empty practice room and played for twenty minutes. I started with something simple, stumbled, recovered, then tried a little improvisation that Daddy might have laughed at. I kept hearing his criticism in my head, especially about the left hand. It wasn't a ghost or a sign; it was years of sitting beside him while he played. I am grateful for that. I played until someone knocked and asked if the room was booked. It wasn't. I invited her in and she listened for a minute. I think Daddy would have liked that.

------------------------------

ENTRY 260 — 2004-12-25 — Age 18 — A Quiet December

Mother and I spent the day at home, not doing anything significant enough for a photograph. I helped clear the pantry, took the dog out, read a book, and made an exceptionally poor attempt at repairing a drawer handle. Mother had to show me the right screwdriver. We talked about whether to travel during winter break and decided neither of us felt like making plans yet. I used to think the holidays required an itinerary. Today it was enough to have nowhere to be. I don't want to forget that.

------------------------------

ENTRY 261 — 2004-12-31 — Age 18 — The Year That Doesn't Fit

I can't summarize this year. I graduated. I was salutatorian. I made a speech about living the journey and not merely chasing the goal. Daddy was there. Two days later he died. I began university and discovered I can be happy for an hour without betraying him. I have made new friends, missed old ones, learned things, and made foolish mistakes. Mother is still Mother, especially when she reminds me to wear a coat. I don't know what 2005 will bring. I know I want to read more, walk more, become useful, and keep writing. I want to remember that a life is made from all of its days, including the ordinary ones. I meant that in June. I mean it now.
