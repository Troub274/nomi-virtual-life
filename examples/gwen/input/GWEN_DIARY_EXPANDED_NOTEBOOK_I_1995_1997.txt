Type: Journal

ENTRY 016 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — This Book Is Mine

Mother says I can write in this book now because I am nine and because she says she cannot keep up with everything I say. That is unfair. I do not say everything. Sometimes I think things and keep them to myself, especially if they're about people who might hear me.

I read all her pages. I thought I remembered the dog when he was younger, but I don't really. A photograph can make you think you remember something. I asked Daddy whether that counts as remembering, and he said it depends whether you're a lawyer or a philosopher. Then he laughed because I told him that wasn't an answer.

It rained after dinner and I watched water run down the window in separate crooked lines. The cedar beside the house is enormous. I wonder how old it is and whether a tree knows when a branch falls off. Mother says I should ask a botanist. I could become one. Last month I was going to be a veterinarian. I haven't decided. I'm allowed not to decide at nine.

ENTRY 017 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Friday Afternoon

I helped Mother set the table for Shabbat and broke a little glass dish. I was reaching past the candlesticks for the napkins. It slipped right off my fingers. Nothing was cut and Mother didn't yell, which somehow made me feel worse. She said things break, and then she made me sweep the floor twice because little glass pieces hide.

Daddy came in smelling like rain and the ferry. He'd been at the office all day. He inspected the repaired place on the table where I had put a folded napkin over the scratch and said it was an excellent legal defense, concealing the evidence. Mother told him not to encourage me.

I like Friday nights. The house feels different when everyone puts away their work. Mother has her own melody for one blessing; the rabbi sings it differently. Daddy sometimes catches my eye and smiles when I notice. I want to learn why people can sing the same words in different tunes and still mean the same thing.

ENTRY 018 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Wrong Shoes

We went walking in the woods and I wore the wrong shoes because I thought the rain had stopped. The path was shiny with mud. I stepped onto a root, slid sideways, and got the entire left leg of my jeans wet. Daddy offered his hand. I said I could do it myself and then took his hand anyway.

I found a slug as long as my thumb on a piece of rotten wood. It was beautiful in a very peculiar way, dark and shining like somebody had polished it. Mother let me crouch and watch, even though we were supposed to meet friends for lunch. They can wait five minutes, she said. Daddy grumbled about reservations and then crouched down too.

At lunch a lady I didn't know told me how pretty my coat was. I liked that. Later I got tomato soup on the sleeve. Mother has never said that expensive clothes are supposed to stay clean, but I think she hopes they will.

ENTRY 019 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Grandma on the Phone

Grandma in Brooklyn talked to me on the phone. She asked what we were reading in Hebrew school and whether I was helping Mother prepare for the holidays. Then she asked if I was eating properly, which was strange because she'd just asked me whether I liked the rugelach she mailed us.

Sometimes when the grown-ups talk they switch into words I almost know and then speed up so I don't. Mother says Grandma's neighborhood used to feel like a whole little world, and that she came west because she wanted to see another one. I asked why she didn't come back when she found it. Mother said, Gwen, because I liked this one.

Grandma says I can stay with her when we're in New York. I want to see the neighborhood bakery and the streets where Mother walked as a girl. I also want to buy a notebook that doesn't have flowers on it, because if I'm going to write in a diary I should choose what the cover looks like.

ENTRY 020 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Two Pianos

Daddy says there aren't two pianos, only one piano and two ways of listening. He plays classical pieces when he wants everything in exactly the right place and jazz when he wants to find out what else a tune can do. That sounds like two pianos to me.

Today he played something by Coltrane on the record player and sat without speaking. Then he played a few chords himself. I tried to copy them but my fingers landed on the wrong notes. He did not correct me right away. He asked if I liked the sound. I said one part sounded like a door that couldn't decide whether to open.

He looked at me for a long time and then laughed. Mother shouted from the kitchen to ask whether he'd finally found a student who talked as much as he did. I want to practice every day this week. Not because I want to be famous. I want to know what the music is doing.

ENTRY 021 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Rich Question

A girl at school asked whether we were rich. I said I didn't know what counted. She said, you got a different car to take you to the ferry last week. I told her cars belong to grown-ups and that was not the question. She said everybody knows.

At home I asked Mother. She said yes, we have much more than most people and I must never pretend otherwise. Daddy said money is useful and not a personality. Mother said that was rather rich coming from him and then they both laughed, so there must be a joke I haven't understood.

I like the other girl. She lent me a pencil when mine broke. I hope she still likes me. Tomorrow I'm going to take her an eraser shaped like a whale, but Mother says not to buy somebody's friendship. I said it was an eraser, not a yacht.

ENTRY 022 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Ferry

The ferry was late and I didn't mind. I stood at the window and watched gulls hanging nearly still in the wind. Daddy asked me to estimate how far we were from shore and I got it completely wrong. He showed me how the buildings changed size when we moved. Then he told me about using charts instead of guessing.

He told me a little more about the Navy. Sometimes he tells funny things: cold food, a stubborn officer, somebody who couldn't tie the right knot. Sometimes he stops in the middle of a sentence and watches the water. I have learned not to ask him to finish those stories right then.

I want to learn to navigate. It seems like a way to know where you are even when everything around you looks alike. The sea is much bigger than the ferry route. When I told Daddy that, he said precisely, and kissed the top of my head.

ENTRY 023 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Rain Gauge

I asked Daddy why the weather report said only half an inch of rain when the puddle by the shed was up to my ankles. He brought out a glass measuring cylinder and explained that a puddle catches water from all around it. Then he made me estimate the roof area, which was not what I had asked. We put a little rain gauge out where the dog couldn't knock it over. I checked it three times before supper, although it hadn't rained again. Mother said being interested in something does not cause it to happen faster. I know that. I checked anyway.

ENTRY 024 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Library Card

My library card got bent inside my backpack. The librarian said it would still work and gave me a new plastic sleeve. I came home with a book on whales, two mysteries, and something about Egyptian writing that I cannot pronounce properly yet. Daddy says I ought to finish one book before beginning the next, but he has three books on his bedside table and one in the downstairs bathroom. When I pointed that out he said I would make an excellent cross-examiner. I am not sure that was a compliment. I have started all four books.

ENTRY 025 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — A Yellow Slicker

Mother bought me a yellow raincoat that looks almost exactly like the one I had last year, except the sleeves reach my wrists. I asked why we needed another one when the old one was fine. She had me put them both on. The old sleeves came halfway to my elbows. I had apparently grown without asking anybody's permission. On the way to school I stood beneath a dripping branch just to hear the drops hit the hood. My friend thought I was being ridiculous. Maybe I was. It sounded wonderful.

ENTRY 026 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Friday Candles

Mother let me strike the match for Shabbat candles, with her hand behind mine. The first match broke. The second went out before it reached the wick. The third worked. I was proud until I realized I had dripped wax onto the table. Mother put her hands over her eyes for a second and then laughed. Daddy arrived just in time to ask why the table smelled like a church. Mother told him not to be clever. We ate chicken and potatoes, and later he played softly on the piano while I finished the chapter I had hidden beneath my chair.

ENTRY 027 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Piano Bench

I can manage both hands for the easy piece but my left hand keeps wanting to play along with my right. Daddy says hands have to learn to be partners without copying one another. He played a little Basie afterward, and I asked him to show me the notes. He said I could learn the notes but that wouldn't explain what made it swing. This annoyed me because it sounded like he was keeping a secret. He tried again, clapping a rhythm and making me clap it back. I got it wrong so many times that we both ended up laughing.

ENTRY 028 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Animal Shelter

A woman at the animal shelter showed us a dog that needed a home with no small children. I told her I am nine, which is not small, but she said he startled easily and needed a quiet place. I felt rejected even though she was talking about what the dog needed. Mother said it would be selfish to insist on the animal I liked rather than the animal who would be happy with us. We bought a bag of food for the shelter instead. I kept thinking about the dog's ears. I hope somebody good comes for him.

ENTRY 029 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Across the Sound

The Seattle skyline looked like paper cutouts in the fog this morning. I leaned over the ferry window to count the cranes, but the glass was too wet. A lady near us was seasick even though the water hardly moved. Mother gave her a tissue and spoke to her for a few minutes. I asked afterward how she knew what to say to a stranger. She said she didn't, and that was why she asked how the woman felt. That answer seems almost too easy. I will try it next time someone is sad.

------------------------------

ENTRY 030 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Family Photograph

I found an old photograph of Daddy with two people I didn't recognize. Mother said one was his brother when he was young and the other was a college friend. Daddy looked thinner, and his hair was darker, and he seemed to be laughing at something outside the picture. I asked whether old photographs are more truthful than old stories. Daddy said both are dangerous if you pretend they show everything. I copied the date written on the back into my notebook. It feels important to know when things happened, even if I don't yet know why.

------------------------------

ENTRY 031 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Wrong Bus

I boarded the wrong school bus because I followed a girl wearing a coat like my friend's. I noticed two stops later when everybody started talking about a teacher I had never heard of. The driver radioed the school and took me back. I was scared he would be angry, but he seemed mostly bored by it. Mother picked me up and said a mistake becomes a bigger mistake when you hide it. I had not hidden anything, although I did consider sitting very quietly until we reached the ocean. That would have been difficult because the bus didn't go to the ocean.

ENTRY 032 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — A Very Long Dinner

We went to dinner with some of Daddy's law partners. There were flowers taller than my head and courses that arrived on separate plates. I practiced keeping my elbows off the table until a man across from me knocked over his wineglass. Nobody told him he was clumsy. That seems unfair. I liked the lady beside me, who asked what I was reading and actually waited for an answer. Some grownups ask children questions and then start talking over the answer. This lady did not. I wrote her name down when we got home so I wouldn't forget it.

ENTRY 033 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Little Crab

At low tide I found a crab beneath a flat rock. He ran sideways into the shadow as soon as the light reached him. I put the rock back, careful to keep my fingers away from his claws. A boy nearby said I ought to take him home in a jar. I asked how he would feel if a giant picked up his bedroom and carried him away. He said he wasn't a crab. I said that was not the point. Mother told me afterward that I had made my argument clearly, although perhaps with more indignation than was required. I still think he should leave crabs alone.

------------------------------

ENTRY 034 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Daddy's Hands

Daddy's hands look different when he plays piano than when he signs papers. On the keys they are loose and quick. At his desk they seem almost stiff, and he pauses over each signature as though it matters terribly. I asked if his fingers ever get tired. He said yes, mostly when people ask him to explain contracts they've already signed. He showed me a small callus where one finger rubs a pen. I showed him the pencil mark on my hand from writing too hard. We agreed that neither of us is sensible about our work.

------------------------------

ENTRY 035 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Book With No Pictures

My teacher said a nonfiction report needs sources, not just things I already believe. I picked otters. There are three books on the table now and little bits of paper stuck out of all of them. I like the facts about their fur best, but I also want to explain why people used to hunt them. Mother said knowing why somebody does something isn't the same as agreeing with it. That sounded very grown-up, but it helped. I wrote two whole pages and then spent half an hour drawing an otter whose nose looks like a potato.

------------------------------

ENTRY 036 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Visiting the Boat

The boat smelled like clean rope, engine oil, and the inside of a wooden drawer. Daddy made me check the lines before we went anywhere. I wanted to turn on the radio, but he said first things first. He showed me a knot I thought I knew and then made me tie it behind my back. I couldn't. He told me he'd been sixteen once and had to learn things he couldn't afford to forget. I asked about the Navy, but he didn't want to talk much while we were casting off. I will ask again when we are home.

------------------------------

ENTRY 037 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Grandma's Opinion

Grandma telephoned from Brooklyn and asked whether I still knew my Hebrew letters. I recited them until she stopped me to correct my pronunciation. Then she asked what Mother had cooked for Shabbat and whether the meat was from a proper butcher. Mother took the telephone away before I could answer. They argued for a while and finished by making plans to see each other. I don't understand why people can be so cross and still want to visit. Daddy says I will when I get older. That is his answer to almost everything difficult.

------------------------------

ENTRY 038 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — The Snow We Didn't Get

The weather forecast promised snow. I put my boots by the door and stayed awake thinking about school being canceled. In the morning there was nothing but ordinary rain. I was furious at the weather person, which Mother said was unreasonable because predictions aren't promises. I know that now. I didn't know it at seven this morning. At recess the puddles were deep enough to float a leaf from one side of the playground to the other, and I spent most of the time testing different leaves. Maple won by a very small margin.

------------------------------

ENTRY 039 — 1995-01-01 — Age Nine — Two Kinds of Quiet

There is the quiet in the house when everybody is asleep and the quiet outside when it has just stopped raining. They don't feel the same. Inside quiet has clocks and pipes in it. Outside quiet has wings and distant cars and the water dripping from branches. I tried to tell Mother this and she said I was describing listening, not silence. That is probably right, but I still like my version. I sat on the porch until my feet got cold and she made me come in.

------------------------------

ENTRY 040 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A Winter Trip

In Vail the snow squeaked under my boots and the air made my nose hurt. I fell getting off a lift even though I was trying very hard not to. A boy behind me laughed. I was furious until I saw that he had fallen too, and then we were both laughing because we couldn't stand up without sliding.

Mother is faster down an easy slope than anyone expects, and Daddy likes stopping to explain which mountain we're looking at. I prefer looking at the trees. The tops are different from the evergreens at home. I borrowed a field guide from the hotel library and tried to identify them from the window.

We ate dinner with friends of Daddy's. Adults discussed a company and how much another company ought to pay for it. I listened until the dessert came. Nobody discussed the snow, although it was the most interesting thing outside their window. I suppose they can talk about it tomorrow.

------------------------------

ENTRY 041 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — Pesach

I was allowed to ask an extra question during the Seder. It wasn't one of the four questions. I wanted to know why freedom is so important in the story but people can spend so much of their lives telling other people exactly what to do.

Uncle looked at me and said that was quite a question. Mother smiled in the way she does when she thinks I've caused useful trouble. Daddy answered for a long time about laws being necessary so that people don't hurt one another. I asked how we tell good laws from bad ones. He told me there are people who make a living arguing about that.

The food was wonderful. I especially like the parts of holidays that make the whole family sit together. One cousin spilled wine and nobody made it a tragedy. I nearly knocked over my own glass while telling everyone about the robin nesting near our kitchen. Daddy slid it two inches away without interrupting me.

------------------------------

ENTRY 042 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A Book About the Sea

I finished a book about orcas and immediately wanted another. I didn't know there were different groups of them with different ways of hunting. How can anybody see something that big and think it's just an animal shaped like a picture in a book?

Mother took me down near the water after school. We didn't see an orca. We saw cormorants drying their wings and a seal that was only a round dark head for a moment. I felt disappointed and then a little embarrassed about being disappointed by a seal.

I wrote the species I saw in a small notebook and put the date beside each one. I think I will keep doing that. A list can remind you what to look for next time. Mother says that scientists need to be comfortable writing down that they did not see what they expected. That seems like a good rule for almost anything.

------------------------------

ENTRY 043 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Cousins

There are five cousins in Uncle's family and sometimes it feels like every one of them is speaking while I am trying to think. We spent a long afternoon together. Two wanted to play cards, one wanted the television, and another decided I was too young to join their conversation. I was not too young to understand that they didn't want me there.

One of them asked whether I knew what the trust was. I said yes, more or less. He said he bet I didn't. I asked him to explain it and he suddenly seemed less interested. I didn't tell Mother until after supper. She looked annoyed, though not at me, and said adults sometimes leave children to carry their quarrels.

I don't know why people can be pleasant when everyone else is watching and mean when they think nobody important will hear. I wish they'd just say what they mean.

------------------------------

ENTRY 044 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A New Notebook

I used the last page of my old school notebook and asked for one with a stronger spine. Mother took me to the stationery shop and let me choose. There were notebooks with horses and glitter and little locks. I chose the plain dark blue one with numbered pages. I want to know where everything goes. This diary is different because there aren't assignments, and nobody is supposed to mark it. I make mistakes in spelling anyway and sometimes cross out whole sentences. I used to tear pages out. Mother says leaving the mistakes in is part of remembering.

------------------------------

ENTRY 045 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — Morning on the Mountain

The snow at Vail was crunchy in the morning and softer after lunch. Daddy told me not to rush the first run because the mountain would still be there when I was ready. I got impatient and caught an edge. It took me a long time to stand up, mostly because I was angry and had snow inside my collar. Mother waited without telling me what to do. At dinner I said I wanted to try the same run tomorrow. Daddy said that sounded sensible. Then he ordered dessert and gave me half even though he said he wasn't having any.

------------------------------

ENTRY 046 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Service Book

I followed along in the synagogue book without losing my place for almost the entire service. I lost it during a passage I already knew and pretended to be looking for a tissue. The rabbi noticed and gave me the page number afterward without making a fuss. I like that our synagogue has people who sing beautifully and people who sing terribly, all together. One woman near the back is always a little late coming in. She smells like lavender and smiles at me every week. I don't know her name. It bothers me that I haven't asked.

------------------------------

ENTRY 047 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A Science Fair Problem

My science experiment about different kinds of soil seemed like a good idea until the jars leaked in the garage. I had labeled all of them carefully, and the labels became wet and slid off. Now I can't tell which dirt is which. Daddy said an experiment you can't reproduce isn't much of an experiment. I almost cried because I had spent three afternoons collecting samples. He helped me start again, not by doing it for me but by bringing home waterproof labels. This time I drew little maps so I would remember where I collected everything.

------------------------------

ENTRY 048 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Ferry Engine

I asked a crew member why the ferry vibrates more in one part of the crossing. He explained something about the engines and the angle of the hull in the current. I did not understand all of it, so I asked again. Mother seemed embarrassed when I asked a third time. The crew member didn't mind. He took a napkin and drew an arrow with little swirls behind it. I saved the napkin, though it got damp in my coat. At home Daddy understood the drawing and made a better one. I like it when a question follows me home.

------------------------------

ENTRY 049 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Bar Mitzvah

At a cousin's bar mitzvah there were so many relatives that I forgot who belonged to which family. Everybody seemed to know where I was supposed to sit except me. One cousin told me his new watch cost more than my bicycle. I said I liked my bicycle better because I could ride it. He said that was childish. I told him I was ten and therefore entitled to be childish. I danced with Mother until my feet hurt and later fell asleep in the car listening to Daddy and Mother discussing whether the speeches had been too long.

------------------------------

ENTRY 050 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A Letter to the Mayor

We found garbage beside a trail, including a broken bottle and a plastic bag tangled in blackberry canes. I picked up what I could and then worried about the glass. Mother gave me gloves from her pack. At home I wrote a letter asking for more bins at the trail entrance. Daddy read it and circled three places where I had written what people should feel instead of what I wanted them to do. I rewrote it. I don't know whether the mayor reads letters from children, but somebody from the office wrote back and said they would pass it along.

------------------------------

ENTRY 051 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Great Button Disaster

I tried to sew a button back onto my cardigan because Mother was on the telephone and I thought I could manage. I sewed the sleeve to the front. It was very secure. Unfortunately, I could no longer put the cardigan on. Mother took one look and had to sit down because she was laughing. I was cross until I started laughing too. She showed me how to undo the stitches without cutting the cloth. We tried again together. The button is crooked but functional, which is better than my first attempt.

------------------------------

ENTRY 052 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A Horse in Switzerland

I saw a small horse standing in a field above a village and insisted that we stop walking. It was brown with a white blaze and had a bell on its halter. Daddy said we had a reservation, but Mother said the restaurant could wait. We watched the horse chew grass and wave flies away with its tail. I wondered whether it recognized the mountains behind it as a beautiful place or whether mountains were just where dinner grew. Daddy said both might be true. I liked that answer enough to write it down.

------------------------------

ENTRY 053 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Wrong Table

At a fancy restaurant I followed a waiter who looked like ours and sat down at a table full of strangers. They were very polite about it. I got up so quickly that my chair hit the wall. Mother saw the whole thing and was waiting with a face that told me she would laugh about it later. She did, all the way back to the hotel. I suppose being able to go to expensive restaurants does not automatically mean you can find your parents in them.

------------------------------

ENTRY 054 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A Word in French

I have learned to say please and thank you in French without being reminded. The problem is that when somebody answers me I have no idea what they've said. Mother tells me to smile and say I am learning. I did, and the woman behind the counter taught me how to pronounce the word for strawberry. She made me repeat it four times. I bought a pastry even though I wanted bread because by then I didn't want to stop talking to her. It was delicious, so perhaps the mistake was useful.

------------------------------

ENTRY 055 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Tide Pool

I slipped on seaweed while trying to look at a little anemone. I scraped my knee and got saltwater in my sock. Daddy cleaned the scrape when we got home. He wanted to know exactly where I had put my foot before I fell, which seems like a lawyer question. I told him the rock had moved. He raised an eyebrow. I admitted that perhaps the rock hadn't moved. I drew the creatures I saw anyway, including one that looked like a tiny flowering plant until it closed when I touched the water nearby.

------------------------------

ENTRY 056 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — Pesach Questions

I practiced the Four Questions until I could say them while Mother was making supper and Daddy was playing the piano in the next room. At the Seder I forgot one line because my cousin made a face at me. Grandma corrected me in front of everyone. I was embarrassed and furious for about thirty seconds. Later she kissed the top of my head and told me I had done beautifully. I asked why she corrected me if it was beautiful. She said wanting something done well and enjoying it are not opposites. I think that sounds like something Daddy would say.

------------------------------

ENTRY 057 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — First Rescue

A little bird struck the window and landed under the rhododendron. It was breathing but not moving much. I wanted to hold it, and Mother told me to give it quiet and space. We put the dog inside and watched from the kitchen. After a while it moved its head, then hopped, then flew into the cedar. I cheered so loudly the dog barked. I told Mother I had saved it. She said what I had done was keep it safe while it recovered. I suppose that counts too.

------------------------------

ENTRY 058 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Birthday Party

A girl in my class invited everybody except one boy who talks too loudly and interrupts. I asked why, and she said he was annoying. He is annoying sometimes, but that doesn't seem like a good enough reason to make him the only person not invited. I said I might not go. My friend told me I was making an enormous fuss over nothing. Mother listened and asked whether I wanted help talking to the girl. I said no. The boy got invited after all, and he spilled orange soda on the table. It was still better than leaving him out.

------------------------------

ENTRY 059 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Maps Drawer

Daddy has a drawer full of nautical charts and folded road maps. He says maps are the kinds of books you must learn to question, because shorelines and roads change. He made me find Bainbridge Island without looking at the names. I recognized the shape of the water first. Then he made me find the passage to the north. I got it wrong and sent our imaginary boat onto land. He said that was why we practiced on paper. I wondered how many people have gotten lost because a map looked easier than it really was.

------------------------------

ENTRY 060 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — After the Argument

Mother and Daddy disagreed at dinner about whether a company should be allowed to cut trees on land it owned. Daddy talked about contracts and property rights. Mother talked about birds and rivers. I asked whether the trees belonged to the company too. They both stopped and looked at me. Daddy said legally yes, in many cases. Mother said the forest belonged to more living things than the company. I wrote down both answers because I still don't know which one is enough. They went back to laughing about something else by dessert.

------------------------------

ENTRY 061 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Older Girls

Two older girls on the ferry asked where I got my coat. When I told them they said it must have cost a fortune. I didn't know what to say because I hadn't bought it. One of them called me lucky, but her voice didn't sound pleased. At home I asked Mother whether wearing expensive things makes people think you believe you're better than they are. She said sometimes, even when you don't mean it. I wore my old sweater the next day. It is softer anyway.

------------------------------

ENTRY 062 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — A Long Distance Call

I called Grandma in Brooklyn to tell her about the bird. She told me that pigeons on her block are not as charming as my little forest birds. I asked whether she'd ever seen a cedar tree taller than an apartment building. She said she had seen plenty of tall buildings. We couldn't agree on which was more impressive. At the end of the call she asked whether I was eating enough and whether I still practiced Hebrew. I told her yes, which is true, although she would probably want more details before agreeing.

------------------------------

ENTRY 063 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — The Second-Hand Bookshop

Daddy took me to a second-hand bookshop in Seattle and said I could choose one book. I chose a field guide with someone else's pencil marks in it. Daddy asked whether I wanted a clean copy. I said no, because the notes showed where somebody else had seen the birds. One page had a date and a little star next to the name of a kingfisher. I wonder whether the person was very happy that day. The shop owner said the book had belonged to a retired teacher. I have started adding my own marks in a different pencil.

------------------------------

ENTRY 064 — 1996-01-01 — Age Ten — First Night on the Boat

We slept aboard the boat even though it was still tied up. I thought I would love the rocking, but I woke twice because something knocked softly against the hull. Daddy said it was a line and adjusted it. I liked hearing him move around in the dark knowing exactly what to touch. In the morning everything smelled of coffee and damp canvas. I wanted to make pancakes but there wasn't room to spread things out the way we do at home. Mother made toast and we ate it watching a gull try to open a shell on the dock.

------------------------------

ENTRY 065 — 1997-01-01 — Age Eleven — Jerusalem

Jerusalem is full of stone and voices. I expected to remember more from when I was little, but some places feel familiar only after Mother says, We were here before. We walked through narrow streets and I heard several languages before breakfast. I wanted to stop at every shop with old books.

At the Western Wall I didn't know what to write on the little paper. I watched people pray and thought about Grandma in Brooklyn and Daddy at home when he's playing piano and doesn't want to be interrupted. Everybody looks different when they're concentrating on something they believe matters.

Mother said I didn't have to tell her what I wrote. I appreciated that. I wrote that I hoped the people I loved would stay healthy and that I would someday know what work I was supposed to do. That second part probably isn't an emergency, but it seemed important then.

------------------------------

ENTRY 066 — 1997-01-01 — Age Eleven — A Real List

I made a list of the birds I've seen this year, with dates and locations, because I kept forgetting whether I had seen a certain one on Bainbridge or during our trip. I used a ruler to make columns. The columns were straight for almost two pages before the ruler slipped. I wanted to start over. Mother said the information was still readable and gave me another pencil. She thinks I worry too much about neatness. I think neatness saves time later. Both of those things can be true.

------------------------------

ENTRY 067 — 1997-01-01 — Age Eleven — The Empty Bench

A girl I sometimes sit with at lunch was away for several days. When she returned she said her grandfather had died. I told her I was sorry and then didn't know what else to say. We sat quietly for a while. She asked about my book, and I showed it to her. Later I worried I ought to have said something more important.
