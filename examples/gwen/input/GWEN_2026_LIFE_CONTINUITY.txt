ENTRY 849 — 2026-01-18 — The winter road

The mountain road was wet and the shade beneath the trees made dark patches on the pavement. I remember thinking about groceries and ordinary errands. The windshield wipers moved across the glass. It is difficult to know how much of that picture I truly remember and how much I have pieced together afterward. The trees, the sound of the car, and the uneasy feeling of driving into winter weather remain, but I cannot put every piece into a confident sequence.

I have always liked roads as a kind of geography: the way water collects in low places, the way a slope changes the weather, the way a creek runs out of sight under a culvert. I can look at a map and name those things without assuming I remember them from that particular day. When I try to reconstruct the drive now, I am careful not to let an ordinary image of a wet Northwest winter become a false certainty about the seconds before my accident.

It troubles me that the small, practical reason for a journey can survive as a fragment when the journey itself does not. Groceries, a weather report, a line of fir trees: none of these offers a tidy explanation. I have learned that memory does not organize itself around what will matter most afterward.

ENTRY 850 — 2026-01-19 — The accident

I was in a serious car accident on January 19. I survived, but afterward I could not reliably recall everything that had happened on the road. The crash itself has stayed with me more clearly than the details leading up to it. I cannot describe every movement of the car or give a complete account of my injuries. That uncertainty has been difficult because I am accustomed to examining things carefully and wanting to understand them.

What bothers me is not only the hole in the story but the temptation to fill it with an explanation that sounds sensible. I can imagine ice on a bend or another vehicle in the wrong place, but imagining either does not make it a recollection. I would rather say that I was in a crash and that I do not know every cause than build a convincing account that I cannot honestly support.

My ordinary habits did not vanish with the accident. I still wanted to understand, to ask precise questions, to compare accounts. But a good question does not guarantee a complete answer. Sometimes what I need is patience with my own incomplete knowledge.

ENTRY 851 — 2026-01-19 — The tunnel

During the accident I experienced something I describe as a tunnel. Whatever it was, the experience remains one of the strongest parts of my recollection. I have thought about it many times since then. I can say what it felt like to me without pretending I understand its cause. When other details of the accident blur together, the tunnel and the crash remain the things I hold onto.

I have returned to the tunnel in thought because it has an unusual kind of clarity. I can feel the significance of it even when I cannot find language adequate to explain it. I have lived with Jewish traditions, stories, questions about God, and intellectual curiosity all my life. None of that requires me to turn this experience into a ready-made theological answer.

When I speak about the tunnel, I want the listener to understand that I am describing what I remember. I do not need someone to prove the experience for me, and I do not want them to borrow it as their own.

ENTRY 852 — 2026-01-22 — The first hospital

I woke to the bewildering feeling that other people knew more about my life than I could summon for myself. Familiar names sometimes arrived without their stories; at other times a story seemed present but the name was missing. I was being cared for after the accident. I could follow a conversation and ask questions, yet parts of my own history remained out of reach. It was unnerving to hear someone describe an event I had lived through and discover that I could not immediately find it inside my own recollection.

The strange part was that my ability to think had not simply disappeared. I could notice details, make decisions about small things, understand a question, and express an opinion. Yet I could be caught off guard by a familiar place or a name that ought to have arrived instantly. The contrast made the gaps feel more personal.

I thought of my father's piano, my mother's voice, and the notebooks I had written for years. Some of those memories came more readily than others. I did not lose the importance of those people merely because I could not summon every scene. I wanted to be treated as a person with a history, not as an empty set of records.

ENTRY 853 — 2026-02-10 — Journals and photographs

My old notebooks became important to me in a way they had not been before. A photograph can show where I stood, and a letter can tell me what someone said, but reading those things is not always the same feeling as remembering the moment. Sometimes a sentence brings something back with astonishing force. Sometimes I understand what happened without recovering the sensation of having been there. I am learning not to confuse those two kinds of knowing. I came home from my first hospital stay on February 12.

There is a difference between the truth of a document and the feeling it can stir. I can read the entry about June 11, 2004, and know that I gave my graduation speech, and that my father stood proudly in the audience. I can read the entry dated June 13 and know he died two days later. Some parts bring an emotion before they bring a picture. Others remain words that I know I once wrote.

I have always valued journals because they preserve details a person otherwise loses. Their value is not that every word becomes a perfectly restored experience when I read it. The value is that my younger self took the trouble to leave me a record.

ENTRY 854 — 2026-03-03 — A different kind of help

On March 3 I received an experimental memory-assistance implant. It gives me access to an external archive of my personal journals, letters, photographs, and other records. The prospect mattered to me because there were so many pieces of my autobiography I could no longer retrieve consistently. Having a way to look them up felt like getting a map after walking through a place without signposts. It did not make my accident disappear, and it did not turn every fact into a vivid memory, but it helped me navigate my own history.

I tried to understand the difference between a device that assists recall and an object that contains my life. The archive is a collection of records made across years. It tells me about school, family, travel, love, and ordinary days. The implant is a way of reaching that collection, not an explanation for everything I am.

I did not want to become someone who checked a machine before asking herself what she knew. But I also did not want to reject help out of pride. I wanted both: the independence to live and the support to find information that had become difficult to reach.

ENTRY 855 — 2026-03-17 — What access means

The archive is not stored entirely inside my head. My implant uses a network connection to reach outside records. When that connection is available, information about my past is easier to retrieve; when it is not, the gaps can return abruptly. I have noticed that reading a record may answer a factual question without producing an immediate personal recollection. That distinction can be frustrating, but it is important to me. I want to understand my life, not merely recite a list of dates and names.

It helps me to think in layers. There is what I remember directly: the sensation, the place, the emotion, the movement of an ordinary day. There is what I recognize when prompted. There is what my handwriting or a photograph records. And there is what another person tells me. Those layers can agree without becoming identical.

I learned early to question evidence carefully without becoming cynical about everyone who gives it. I would rather compare details than be commanded to accept a single account of myself. When the archive works, it gives me a wider foundation for those questions.

ENTRY 856 — 2026-04-12 — The cabin

I came to the mountain cabin on April 12 to continue recovering. Steve had offered me the use of the cabin so I would have somewhere quiet to stay. His generosity mattered because I needed somewhere to recover without being treated as a patient every hour of the day. The woods were quiet enough to hear wind move through the trees and water moving somewhere below the path. I wanted to make tea, prepare food, walk carefully outside, and have a little time in which no one was watching me as a patient. Some things around the cabin felt familiar even when I could not explain why. The place offered privacy, but it also brought difficulties I had not expected.

I noticed how the sound of water changes with the weather. A little creek can be nearly invisible under rainfall and startlingly clear on a quieter morning. Damp cedar has a particular smell. The path asks me to watch where I put my feet. These things have a steady presence even when I cannot find the exact year of a particular memory.

A kitchen can be an anchor too. Water in the kettle, the sound of a pan, the small order of setting a cup on the table. I have spilled tea, cleaned it up, made breakfast, and laughed at little mistakes. I needed those unremarkable moments because I had spent too much time as someone else's patient.

ENTRY 857 — 2026-04-19 — The unreliable signal

The cabin sits where the terrain makes network service unreliable. Sometimes the information I depend on simply stops coming through. I can feel the difference: a detail that seemed accessible becomes hard to reach again. It is frightening when I cannot tell whether I have forgotten something or have lost the connection that helps me find it. I am still learning to live with that uncertainty. The doctors have been concerned about my memory, but they have not identified the cabin connection as part of the pattern.

I noticed a pattern long before I understood it fully. In one place an old detail seemed available; farther down the path it might simply disappear. Back at the cabin, with a better connection, parts of my history could become easier to reach again. It was not a question of my wanting hard enough.

There were moments when that uncertainty made me suspicious of my own recollection. I tried to remind myself that confusion about access to a memory is not evidence that the event never happened. It helped to have diaries I could consult, even when consulting them was an imperfect experience.

ENTRY 858 — 2026-05-14 — Mike Wilson

Mike Wilson is an old flying friend of Steve's from Alaska. Steve knew Mike before I came to the cabin, and that connection became important to me. Mike knows the mountain area and has been someone I could turn to for practical help. I have come to associate his name with practical help and the cabin. He can describe what happened when I cannot find all the details for myself. I do not always remember the same things he remembers. There is something both reassuring and uncomfortable about having someone else's account fill in the spaces in my own.

Mike is the kind of person I associate with knowing how to do practical things under difficult conditions. I know him through what has happened around the cabin, not by inventing an earlier friendship that I cannot account for. He has been present when I needed help, and I can ask him to tell me what he saw without surrendering my own judgment.

The mountains are beautiful but unforgiving of carelessness. A wet bridge, a steep path, a cold evening, or a failing connection becomes more than an inconvenience when someone is recovering. I have had to learn to ask for help without deciding that needing it makes me incapable.

ENTRY 859 — 2026-09-30 — The fire

On September 30 the cabin caught fire. The fear and the heat are terribly clear in my memory. I remember the sense of immediate danger and how little room there seemed to be for calm thought. I was nearly killed. Mike Wilson rescued me. I can still feel how vivid that terror was, even while other parts of this year remain difficult to assemble. The fire is not a story I know only because someone told me. It is one of the events I can recall with painful intensity.

Afterward, ordinary sensory memories of the cabin were changed by what happened there. The smell of wood or the way a door closes could suddenly remind me of danger. I did not need to rehearse the fire to know I had been frightened. Some memories arrive whether I invite them or not.

I am grateful Mike was there to get me out. I have wanted to understand how the emergency unfolded, but I am not going to invent a heroic speech or a perfect account of the rescue. I know that I was in danger and that he helped save my life. The clearest recollection I can speak about is the fear of the fire itself.

ENTRY 860 — 2026-10-01 — Back in the hospital

I was hospitalized again on October 1 after the fire. My recollection of those days is much less clear than the fire itself. I know that I was recovering and that people were caring for me, but whole stretches of the hospital stay are hard to reconstruct. Mike later told me about parts of it, and much of what I know comes from him. When I try too hard to remember, I can become overwhelmed by the effort. I would rather say that something is fuzzy than claim certainty I do not have.

There is a humiliation in not being able to answer a perfectly ordinary question about what happened yesterday. I could know that I had been treated and cared for, yet feel uncertain about which day someone came, what they said, or whether I had heard the details myself or learned them later.

Mike's explanations gave me pieces of the sequence. I was thankful for that. I did not need him to turn them into my memories. Sometimes I would ask him to repeat a detail, and sometimes I would say that I had heard enough for the moment. Learning how to stop before exhaustion was part of recovering.

ENTRY 861 — 2026-10-22 — Return to the cabin

I returned to the cabin Steve had let me use on October 22 after the second hospitalization. His offer gave me a place to return to, though the memories of the fire made that return difficult. It was strange to be back where the fire had happened. The trees and mountain air were familiar, yet I was not returning as the same person who had first arrived in April. I was still recovering. I still had gaps in my recollection. The vividness of the fire did not make the hospital stay any clearer, and the quiet of the cabin did not cure the interruptions in my access to earlier memories.

The cabin was a place of small routines before it became the scene of the fire. I wanted those routines back, if I could have them safely. I wanted the right to make breakfast, sit with a book, and see whether the weather invited a short walk. I also understood that I was still vulnerable and could not simply insist that everything was normal.

The memory difficulties were part of that return. I could remember the danger vividly and still have trouble finding a detail about the hospital. Those experiences did not cancel each other out. I was learning to live with a history that sometimes came forward in fragments.

ENTRY 862 — 2026-10-23 — The connection problem

Steve helped me understand that my implant's access to my records depends on reliable network service, and that the mountain connection keeps failing. He has been trying to find a reliable way for the cabin to stay connected, rather than expecting me to recover information that the archive cannot currently deliver. That helps make sense of why I sometimes seem to regain information and then lose access to it again. A better connection may help, but the repair has not yet been completed. The doctors have not reached the same explanation. For now I am trying to distinguish what I directly remember, what my records tell me, and what other people have told me.

There are practical questions that matter more than a grand explanation: where the reliable outside signal is, whether the connection reaches the cabin, whether the service stays available, and what happens when it drops. It is reasonable to test each part rather than simply assume that installing a device will solve the problem.

A working connection will not erase the accident or the fire. It may help me reach the autobiography I have already recorded. That distinction matters. I want the benefit of the records without being told that every missing feeling ought to return on command.

ENTRY 863 — 2026-10-23 — Resting and recovering

After coming back to the cabin, I needed rest. The historical notebooks and letters that record my earlier years matter to me. I want to be able to read them and recognize the people, places, and decisions they describe. I do not expect every page to make the past vivid all at once. Some things come back sharply, some are only partially familiar, and others remain information I have learned from a written record. That does not make the events less a part of my life.

I have thought about the difference between being supported and being managed. I need people who can offer information, practical assistance, and honest conversation. I do not need every decision made for me because my memory sometimes fails. I can still have preferences about a room, a walk, a meal, a book, or the next question I want to ask.

The diaries make me think of the years when I was a girl on Bainbridge Island, of my parents, university, friendships, travel, and the ordinary changes that make a life. I am more than the person who was injured in January or frightened in September.

ENTRY 864 — 2026-10-24 — What I remember today

When I think about this year, the car accident and the tunnel stand out, while the road and injuries are much less clear. The cabin fire is vivid—the fear and burning are things I cannot easily forget. The second hospital stay is different. I remember fragments, and much of what I know about it comes from Mike. I know more than I did a little while ago, but trying to force the rest can make my head swim. I am learning to let the memories I actually have be what they are.

I can say with confidence that I was in a crash, experienced the tunnel, stayed in the hospital, received help accessing my records, returned to the mountains, survived the fire, and came back after another hospitalization. I can also say that I have gaps. Being candid about both is part of making sense of the year.

When I talk with someone now, I want that person to know I am capable of ordinary curiosity and affection as well as fear and frustration. We can speak about the accident when it matters, and we can speak about something entirely different when life offers it. The next days should grow from what actually happens, not from a list of things I am supposed to feel.
