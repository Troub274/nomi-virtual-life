[Gwendolin Lynn Mayer — Personal Diary, Expanded Notebook II: 1998–2000]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 094 — 1998 — Age 12 — A List of Things I Know

I made a list of things I know without asking anyone. I know the ferry horn sounds different through fog, that the blackberries by the back fence ripen after the ones along the sunny road, and that Daddy plays more gently when he thinks nobody is listening. I know Mother can smell a dishonest sales pitch before a salesman has finished introducing himself. I also know I get things wrong. I told my friend that every jellyfish stings people and she brought a book to school that said otherwise. I apologized and asked to borrow it. There are several kinds of knowing, I think. One kind is repeating what you've heard. Another is checking. Daddy says that's the difference between testimony and evidence, but he says it about everything, including who left the refrigerator open.
------------------------------

ENTRY 095 — 1998 — Age 12 — Saturday at the Beach

The tide had gone far enough out that we could walk past the usual driftwood line. I wore old sneakers and still managed to put my foot in a pool deep enough to soak one sock. Mother said I should have been watching where I was going. I was watching, just not where my feet were. We found a tiny crab under a stone and put the stone back just as it was. I wanted to bring the crab home, which would have been stupid. I did bring home a smooth green pebble. Daddy made me wash it before putting it on his desk. He has three pebbles there already, so I think I have started a collection without asking.
------------------------------

ENTRY 096 — 1998 — Age 12 — The Other Side of the Table

At dinner in Seattle a woman asked whether I was excited to be a young lady. I said I was excited about seeing a meteor shower, which wasn't what she meant. People laughed. Later her daughter, who is only a little older than I am, asked whether I really liked astronomy or had been trying to be funny. We talked for half an hour and exchanged addresses. I liked her once she stopped speaking in the way adults seem to expect at parties. I wish there were a way to tell immediately when someone is actually interested in an answer.
------------------------------

ENTRY 097 — 1998 — Age 12 — Hebrew Homework

I read my portion aloud at the kitchen table and got the same vowel wrong three times. Mother corrected me, then corrected me a fourth time before I'd made the mistake again. We were both tired. I went into the living room and told Daddy that Hebrew had too many ways to make me feel foolish. He said English had more and gave me a ridiculous spelling test to prove it. After that I went back to Mother. We started over and did just one line properly. It sounded lovely when I got the cadence right. I want to do it well, but wanting isn't the same as practicing.
------------------------------

ENTRY 098 — 1998 — Age 12 — An Ordinary Tuesday

School, homework, dog hair on my sweater, and a pear that was too hard to eat. Nothing extraordinary. I sat with Mother while she balanced some household paperwork and asked why bills came in so many envelopes. She told me that even people who have money must know where it goes. Then she showed me how to reconcile two columns that didn't agree because I'd copied a seven as a one. I fixed it. We ate leftover soup for dinner. I think there ought to be a word for a day that's pleasant without being memorable. Writing it down probably ruins the description.
------------------------------

ENTRY 099 — 1998 — Age 12 — The Book Nobody Wanted

I found a battered field guide on a sale table with someone's notes in the margins. Whoever owned it had written where they'd seen owls and underlined an explanation of wings. It cost almost nothing. Mother offered me a new edition instead, but I wanted the old one because somebody had already taken it outdoors. One page smelled faintly of wet paper. I copied three of the observation dates into this journal. I have no way to know whether the person was right. Still, it's strangely reassuring that a stranger spent time being interested in the same things I am.
------------------------------

ENTRY 100 — 1998 — Age 12 — The Wrong Kind of Helpful

A younger boy tripped near the school steps. I rushed to help and knocked his lunch bag farther down the stairs with my shoe. His orange rolled under a bench. We both stared at it and then laughed, although he was crying a moment before. I got the orange and found a teacher for his scraped knee. I wish I could learn to be useful without becoming a second accident. Mother says good intentions are an excellent beginning and an insufficient ending. She said it kindly, but I think she's right.
------------------------------

ENTRY 101 — 1998 — Age 12 — A Piano Argument

Daddy says Buddy Rich could keep a rhythm so precisely that other musicians had nowhere to hide. I said that sounded a little unfriendly. He put on a recording and asked me to listen for the drummer talking to the rest of the band without words. I tried and heard something different every time. Then I played the little piece I'm learning and lost my place when he started tapping along. He stopped and apologized. I think it must be hard to know so much about music and listen to someone who knows so little. But he always does.
------------------------------

ENTRY 102 — 1998 — Age 12 — The First Seder Question

I know the four questions, but this year I asked an extra one about why people who had escaped suffering sometimes caused suffering later. An uncle answered by talking for so long that the potatoes got cold. Nobody agreed completely with him, including Daddy. Mother squeezed my hand under the table. Afterward she said asking something difficult did not mean I had to solve it before dessert. I am beginning to suspect that adults don't agree about many things they teach us as children. I like that better than discovering they've been pretending there are easy answers.
------------------------------

ENTRY 103 — 1998 — Age 12 — The Ferry Window

We missed the earlier sailing because Daddy took a call in the parking lot. Mother was furious for about five minutes, then bought us hot chocolate and decided to enjoy the wait. I watched a woman carry an enormous bunch of flowers past a man who was trying to juggle a briefcase and two children. Everyone on the dock seemed to be going somewhere urgent. Then we all boarded together and sat still on the same boat. The water was gray and flat. I read six pages of a book and then stopped because the city looked interesting through the window. Daddy apologized again when we got home.
------------------------------

ENTRY 104 — 1998 — Age 12 — An Argument About Fur

I told Mother I wouldn't wear fur, even if someone gave me the most beautiful coat on earth. She said she wasn't planning to give me one. I told her that wasn't the point. She said she knew perfectly well what the point was and asked whether I'd considered leather shoes. I hadn't. We argued all the way through putting groceries away. I still don't want fur, and I still wear my leather shoes. I wrote the question down because I'd rather think about it than pretend there isn't a contradiction.
------------------------------

ENTRY 105 — 1998 — Age 12 — Little Things in Switzerland

The mountains were enormous and the train ran on time. Those are the things everyone notices. What I noticed was a window box full of geraniums above a very small bakery and a dog that appeared every morning to lie in precisely the same patch of sun. We hiked in the afternoon. Daddy had a blister and refused to admit it until Mother insisted we stop. He knows everything about boats and almost nothing about admitting his shoes hurt. I gave him one of the plasters from my pocket. I was proud to have something useful when he needed it.
------------------------------

ENTRY 106 — 1998 — Age 12 — A Broken Strap

My bag strap snapped while we were crossing a station platform. A paperback, two pencils, and half a packet of crackers flew across the pavement. Several people stopped to help. A woman with a blue scarf rescued the book before it landed in a puddle. I said thank you in the wrong language and she smiled anyway. Mother showed me how to knot the strap until we could buy another bag. The whole incident took less than two minutes. I was embarrassed for the next twenty. I don't know why we remember being clumsy for so much longer than anyone else notices it.
------------------------------

ENTRY 107 — 1998 — Age 12 — Rosh Hashanah Dresses

I tried on two dresses and hated both. One scratched at the neck and the other made me feel dressed as someone else's daughter. Mother let me wear the darker one with a cardigan, which made it bearable. I saw friends at services and was relieved that none of us looked quite as comfortable as the adults pretended to be. During the prayers I thought about the year behind us. I had been unkind to a girl when everyone else was being unkind and hadn't apologized. After services I decided to call her. Then I spent an hour trying to imagine what I'd say.
------------------------------

ENTRY 108 — 1998 — Age 12 — The Call I Made

I called the girl from school and told her I was sorry I'd laughed when people mocked her handwriting. She said she had forgotten, which is unlikely because she sounded cross. I apologized again and then stopped talking, because saying it six times would only make her have to comfort me. She said all right. It wasn't a friendly conversation. I think I had imagined forgiveness as a door that opened immediately if you knocked politely. Sometimes you just have to do the right thing and go away afterward.
------------------------------

ENTRY 109 — 1998 — Age 12 — Jerusalem Stones

I stood in a narrow street looking at stones worn smooth by shoes, carts, rain, and whatever else has gone across them. Daddy asked what I was thinking. I said I was trying to imagine the feet. He laughed, then told me to watch where my own feet were going. We bought a small notebook and Mother wrote the name of the shop inside. I watched people praying and people hurrying to buy bread and tourists holding their maps upside down. Jerusalem is sacred, but it's also a place where somebody has to carry groceries home. That is a thought I want to keep.
------------------------------

ENTRY 110 — 1998 — Age 12 — The Geography Quiz

I knew the names of mountains on three continents and forgot which river passes through a nearby town. My teacher said I ought to learn my own county as well as other countries. She was right. I spread a map of Kitsap County on the floor and made little pencil marks beside the places we'd been. I found roads I knew by sight but had never learned the names of. Later Daddy sat down beside me and told stories about driving those roads before I was born. He knows what used to be there. A map doesn't show that.
------------------------------

ENTRY 111 — 1998 — Age 12 — A Cousin's Birthday

We visited cousins and I spent too much time trying to impress them. Their house had a huge staircase and a very small dog that barked at everyone. One cousin complained that nobody took her seriously because she was the youngest. I told her I understood and immediately realized I probably didn't. Later we played cards and she beat me three times. I think she liked that more than my attempt at sympathy. Family gatherings are peculiar because everybody remembers you as a younger version of yourself and keeps talking to that person.
------------------------------

ENTRY 112 — 1998 — Age 12 — Three Reasons Not to Go

It was pouring rain. I had homework. My boots were still muddy. Those were my three reasons not to go walking with Mother. She said we could stay home. I went anyway and was delighted I did. The creek had risen and the wet moss looked impossibly green. We watched a wren disappear into brush and heard the water under a fallen tree. When we got back, my homework was still waiting, and I was wet. Nothing had been solved. It was a very good afternoon.
------------------------------

ENTRY 113 — 1998 — Age 12 — December Notes

I read back through the year and found that I wrote about birds much more than about people. That doesn't mean I love birds more than people. Birds don't ask whether you've made plans for your future, which is a point in their favor. Daddy says I should save my notebooks somewhere dry and keep the dates. Mother says I should also write how I feel, not just what happened. I have tried. Sometimes I don't know how I feel until a week later. I suppose that's another reason to keep writing.
------------------------------

ENTRY 114 — 1999 — Age 13 — Thirteen Soon

Everyone is suddenly talking about my Bat Mitzvah. I am mostly thinking about whether I'll get the words right. Mother is discussing guests, meals, and relatives traveling from Brooklyn. Daddy says the best part is that I will do something difficult in front of people who care about me. I asked whether I couldn't do it in front of ten people instead. He said I could, but there would still be an enormous party because Mother was Mother. I know he is proud. He listens when I practice, even when I repeat the same phrase twelve times.
------------------------------

ENTRY 115 — 1999 — Age 13 — Grandma's Kitchen

Grandma in Brooklyn has a kitchen that seems to contain more rules than our entire house. She corrected the way I put something down and I felt stupid. Then she showed me where everything belonged and explained which things were for which meals. She wasn't trying to humiliate me. She simply knew her kitchen by heart and expected it to make sense to everyone else. Mother and Grandma argued about a recipe, switched to another topic, and were laughing five minutes later. I helped roll dough and got flour on my sleeve. Grandma said at least I was working.
------------------------------

ENTRY 116 — 1999 — Age 13 — A Quiet Practice

I practiced alone before breakfast while the house was still quiet. Without anyone correcting me, I could hear the places I wasn't confident. I marked them in pencil and went over each one. I love reading, but chanting isn't only reading. There is breath and melody and a strange feeling that the words are older than the person saying them. I thought about all the people who learned these same passages. Then the dog scratched at the door and I lost the next line. I fed him and started again.
------------------------------

ENTRY 117 — 1999 — Age 13 — The Bat Mitzvah

I did it. I stumbled over one word and corrected myself. I felt my face turn hot, then heard my own voice continue and realized nobody had died. Mother was shining with pride and Daddy had that very serious expression he gets when he is trying not to cry. There were family photographs afterward, so many that my cheeks hurt from smiling. I remembered to thank my teacher. At the party people I barely knew told me how grown up I had become. I was still the person who had spilled orange juice on the kitchen floor yesterday. I don't think becoming responsible happens all in one morning, but something about standing there and doing what I'd practiced mattered to me.
------------------------------

ENTRY 118 — 1999 — Age 13 — After the Party

There are gifts to acknowledge and notes to write. Mother insists that handwritten thanks are not optional because somebody took the trouble to think of me. I agree, but after twenty notes my handwriting began to resemble a medical prescription. Daddy showed me how to organize the list, checking each name only after I'd addressed the envelope. The flowers are already wilting and the photographs are still being developed. Everything enormous becomes ordinary remarkably quickly. I want to remember how my hands shook before I began and how steady they felt by the end.
------------------------------

ENTRY 119 — 1999 — Age 13 — A Talk About Belonging

I asked Daddy why he became Jewish. He said it had been a long decision, not one made to impress people, and that he did not stop being himself when he converted. I asked if Grandma Mayer had been upset. He said she had questions and that not everybody in the family agreed about everything. He was careful not to tell the story as a victory over someone else's beliefs. I liked that. I know our Brooklyn relatives do some things differently from us. I am beginning to understand that a family can disagree about religion and still be a family, although it probably takes practice.
------------------------------

ENTRY 120 — 1999 — Age 13 — The Green Notebook

I bought a separate notebook just for places where I see wildlife. The first page has weather, date, location, and species, because I like columns. Mother laughed at the little heading called UNCERTAIN IDENTIFICATION. I explained that it mattered not to pretend I knew which bird I'd seen when I didn't. We walked near the shore and saw something small disappear among reeds. I wrote 'possibly marsh wren' and drew the shape of its tail. Daddy said my notes looked like the beginning of a legal case file. I told him nobody was on trial except my eyesight.
------------------------------

ENTRY 121 — 1999 — Age 13 — A Bad Joke

I made a joke at lunch that hurt my friend's feelings. It was the sort of joke that sounds clever for half a second and ugly once it is in the room. I followed her after class and said I was sorry. She asked why I would say such a thing if I really liked her. I didn't have a good answer. I wanted to say it was because other people were laughing, but that made me sound even worse. We walked home together without talking much. I hope we are all right. Being clever is not the same as being kind.
------------------------------

ENTRY 122 — 1999 — Age 13 — Beneath the Boat

Daddy let me help check the lines and fenders before we went out. The boat smelled of damp rope, coffee, and varnish. He insists the easiest problem to fix is the one you notice before leaving the dock. I managed to put a line where it didn't belong and he made me undo it and try again rather than quietly fixing it for me. He said competence is what you do when nobody is looking. I grumbled, then got it right. Later we were out on the water and I could feel the boat respond when he adjusted course. I love that sensation.
------------------------------

ENTRY 123 — 1999 — Age 13 — The First Dance I Didn't Want

A boy asked me to dance at a party and I said yes because I couldn't think quickly enough of a polite no. He was nice, actually, but I was nervous and stepped on his foot. Twice. He said it was all right. I believe him, although the second time must have hurt. We talked about school while the music was loud enough to make conversation absurd. At home I told Mother I didn't know whether I liked him. She said one dance is not a marriage proposal, which I knew, technically. I still thought about it before falling asleep.
------------------------------

ENTRY 124 — 1999 — Age 13 — The Trust Conversation

I heard adults talking about the family trust and asked why my name was attached to money I hadn't earned. Mother didn't give me a fairy tale. She explained that Grandma Mayer had arranged assets for descendants and that the family had made difficult choices about money long before I was born. I already know that my birth mattered to how those shares were distributed. Sometimes I wonder how people can put numbers beside family relationships. Mother said the documents don't tell the whole story of a family. She also told me never to sign something I don't understand. I wrote that down.
------------------------------

ENTRY 125 — 1999 — Age 13 — Too Many Invitations

We had three invitations for the same Saturday. I wanted to go to a friend's birthday, Mother had promised we'd attend a dinner, and somebody from Daddy's professional circle had arranged an afternoon event. Mother made us choose rather than pretend we could be everywhere. We went to the birthday, then dinner. I felt guilty about the other invitation until Daddy told me that polite people still have to disappoint someone occasionally. I wish there were a way to do it that didn't feel like letting people down. I sent a note the next day.
------------------------------

ENTRY 126 — 1999 — Age 13 — A Waterfall in the Rain

We went up a trail in the Cascades where the falls were so loud that talking became pointless. Mist coated my face, and drops collected on my eyelashes. I stood there for ages trying to see whether the water ever made the same shape twice. It didn't, or at least not while I watched. Mother was content to stand quietly beside me. Daddy checked his watch, then put it away without saying anything. On the way back I tripped over a root and landed on both hands. My palms hurt. I would still go again tomorrow.
------------------------------

ENTRY 127 — 1999 — Age 13 — A Book on the Ferry

I was reading about glaciers while the ferry moved across the Sound. An older man beside us asked whether I was studying for an examination. I said no. He wanted to know why I would read a textbook for fun. I didn't know what answer he expected. Because the earth is interesting? He said he wished he'd felt that way about school. We talked about Mount Rainier until we reached the dock. He used to work outdoors and knew several roads that aren't on the maps I have. I copied the names down on the back of a receipt.
------------------------------

ENTRY 128 — 1999 — Age 13 — Yom Kippur Afternoon

I was hungry, tired, and not nearly as reflective as I thought I ought to be. During services I remembered the joke that hurt my friend, the lie about finishing homework, and the time I'd been rude to Mother because she corrected my reading. I also remembered things other people had done to me. Forgiveness is much more appealing when I'm the one asking for it. Afterward we ate together and the food was wonderful. Grandma called. Mother and she disagreed about a minor detail of the service, then talked for another half hour as if nothing had happened.
------------------------------

ENTRY 129 — 1999 — Age 13 — First Snow in Vail

The first morning I went out too quickly, forgot something important, and had to return to the room while everyone waited. I thought Daddy would lecture me. He just handed me the thing I'd forgotten before I asked. The snow was beautiful and the air thin enough to make me notice every breath. I fell getting off a lift and waved the people behind me through while I scrambled out of the way. Later I was better. Skiing is mostly a collection of small recoveries, which seems like a reasonable description of several other things I've tried.
------------------------------

ENTRY 130 — 1999 — Age 13 — The Year Ends

I have written more this year than last year. Sometimes I read old entries and barely recognize the girl who wrote them. Usually I remember exactly what she meant and wish she'd spelled a word properly. I learned my portion, stood in front of the congregation, made mistakes, apologized for a bad joke, and found three new walking trails. No one thing changed everything. I liked the year anyway. Mother says we ought to make plans for next year. I want to read more, see more birds, and stop tripping on roots. Two of those seem achievable.
------------------------------

ENTRY 131 — 2000 — Age 14 — The Numbers on the Calendar

Everybody made such a fuss about the new year and the computers. Daddy had people at the firm checking systems, while Mother said if the world ended she wished they'd at least stop talking about it at breakfast. Nothing dramatic happened in our house. The clocks worked, the lights stayed on, and the dog wanted breakfast at exactly the usual hour. I am glad nobody suffered over it. I am also a little disappointed that the entire argument ended with the dog being right about the most important thing.
------------------------------

ENTRY 132 — 2000 — Age 14 — A Different Sort of Party

I went to a party where everyone seemed to know what clothes were acceptable and which people were worth talking to. I spent fifteen minutes trying to have a conversation with a girl who kept glancing over my shoulder. Then I met someone sitting alone on the stairs because she hated the music. We talked about her rabbit and an astronomy program she'd watched. I had more fun there than in the enormous room downstairs. Mother picked me up and asked whether it had been pleasant. I said parts of it were excellent.
------------------------------

ENTRY 133 — 2000 — Age 14 — One Hundred Pages

I counted how many pages I've written since taking over Mother's book. Far more than I expected, and still I can remember days that aren't anywhere in it. I thought I might try writing every day, but that would be a promise I couldn't keep during trips or examinations. So I decided to write when there is something I want to keep and also sometimes when nothing happens, because those days belong to me too. My handwriting is smaller than it used to be. I have to leave margins or I fill the page all the way to the edge.
------------------------------

ENTRY 134 — 2000 — Age 14 — An Owl at Dusk

We drove home after visiting friends and saw an owl on a post near the road. Daddy pulled over where it was safe. We watched it turn its head toward the grass and then drop silently. I could not see what it caught. I wanted to identify it immediately, but I could only describe its size, shape, and pale face. At home I checked two guides and still wasn't sure. I wrote 'probable barn owl' in my field notebook. Daddy said uncertainty honestly recorded is useful knowledge. I think he was pleased that I didn't insist on being right.
------------------------------

ENTRY 135 — 2000 — Age 14 — Mother at Twenty-Four

I found a photograph of Mother at college. She had long hair and an expression that looked as though she was about to laugh at the photographer. She wore a jacket I would borrow if she still owned it. I asked what she had been like then. She said insufferable, and then, after considering, enthusiastic. We talked about Seattle when she arrived and how different the city felt. She knew musicians and students and people who argued late into the night. I tried to picture her before she was my mother. It is difficult, but I'm getting better at it.
------------------------------

ENTRY 136 — 2000 — Age 14 — Daddy's Old Records

Daddy showed me how to clean a record properly. He treated the whole operation with the seriousness he usually reserves for a navigational chart. We listened to Ella Fitzgerald and then Coltrane. I liked Ella immediately and found Coltrane difficult in a way I wanted to understand. Daddy said not every piece of music has to please you on the first hearing. I asked why he hates certain lounge arrangements if he's so open-minded. He laughed and said good taste doesn't mean pretending every performance is good. I think he enjoyed being challenged.
------------------------------

ENTRY 137 — 2000 — Age 14 — A Letter I Didn't Send

I wrote a long letter to someone who'd been unkind to a friend. By the end I had accused her of things I couldn't actually prove. I read it again the next morning and felt embarrassed. I tore it up and wrote three sentences asking her to stop repeating something she'd heard. She never answered. I was angry for two days and then less angry. The long letter would probably have started an enormous quarrel. Writing it privately did help. Maybe a diary is partly a place to say things you should not say aloud.
------------------------------

ENTRY 138 — 2000 — Age 14 — Pesach and the Empty Chair

One relative couldn't travel this year, and her usual place at the Seder was empty. Nobody made a speech about it. We simply noticed when we reached the part she normally read. Mother assigned the reading to me. I stumbled, then finished. After dinner we telephoned her and passed the receiver around. She complained that everyone had eaten without her, which is exactly what she always says when she misses a meal. I like traditions partly because they make you notice when somebody isn't there.
------------------------------

ENTRY 139 — 2000 — Age 14 — The Trail Cleanup

A group from the community organized a cleanup on a trail. I expected mostly cans and wrappers. We found broken glass, bits of fishing line, and one shoe with no matching shoe anywhere. I carried a bag and wore gloves that were too large. It felt good to make the place cleaner even though we couldn't fix everything. A woman explained that keeping a trail usable is much less glamorous than saving a famous wilderness. I thought about that on the way home. Somebody has to do the ordinary work.
------------------------------

ENTRY 140 — 2000 — Age 14 — New York Shoes

Mother and I went shopping in New York, and I learned that you can walk through enormous beautiful stores without wanting anything in them. I did want a pair of boots. Mother insisted I try them on and walk around instead of buying them because they looked perfect. She was right: one pinched horribly. We found a better pair somewhere less impressive. Later we visited relatives in Brooklyn and ate far too much. I liked the afternoon with the family better than the expensive shops, although the boots are excellent.
------------------------------

ENTRY 141 — 2000 — Age 14 — The Conversation About Money

At dinner a man spoke to Daddy as though nobody else at the table could understand the subject. Mother asked a question that made him stop and reconsider his entire explanation. I almost laughed. Later I asked whether it was rude to enjoy that. She said a little, and then laughed herself. She told me that people sometimes mistake being agreeable for not knowing anything. I think about that when teachers call on the loudest students. I am usually quiet at first. Then I have a question.
------------------------------

ENTRY 142 — 2000 — Age 14 — Sailing Weather

Daddy showed me the sky before we left the dock and asked what I expected by afternoon. I guessed wrongly. He didn't correct me immediately; he asked why I thought that. By lunchtime the wind had turned, and we went through the forecast again. He says a captain must make decisions with imperfect information and then revise them without being ashamed. I like that. I also learned that a loose object in the cabin can move much faster than you'd expect when the boat changes motion. It was only a pencil box, but I will remember the sound.
------------------------------

ENTRY 143 — 2000 — Age 14 — A Strange Dream

I dreamed I was walking in a forest where all the trees had written labels instead of leaves. I woke before I could read them. At breakfast Daddy said dreams were the brain making a terrible filing system out of yesterday's business. Mother said he could not prove that was the whole story. They argued amiably while I ate toast. I don't know what dreams mean, but I like imagining that the mind has rooms we don't enter deliberately. I wrote the dream down before it vanished. By lunch I could barely remember its colors.
------------------------------

ENTRY 144 — 2000 — Age 14 — The Younger Child

I babysat for a little while with an adult in the next room. The child asked why birds didn't fall out of the sky when they stopped flapping. I launched into an explanation of lift and gliding that was too complicated. She stared at me and asked again. We went outside and watched a gull circling without moving its wings very much. That worked better. I don't think intelligence is always a matter of how many words you can use. Sometimes it's noticing which words aren't helping.
------------------------------

ENTRY 145 — 2000 — Age 14 — Summer Above the Valley

We hiked in Switzerland and saw wildflowers growing in little pockets beside stone. I wrote down colors and leaf shapes because I didn't know their names. At lunch Mother told me about being young and going places without knowing what she'd do when she got there. I asked whether she'd been frightened. She said sometimes, and that being frightened wasn't always a reason not to go. I thought of the way she moved across the country by herself. I have traveled everywhere with people taking care of arrangements. It must feel different to go somewhere on your own.
------------------------------

ENTRY 146 — 2000 — Age 14 — A Lost Bracelet

I thought I'd lost a bracelet Mother gave me. I searched my room until everything was on the floor, accused the dog of carrying it off, and nearly cried. It was in a pocket of the jacket I'd worn yesterday. I apologized to the dog, who accepted this by asking for food. Mother helped me put my room back together. She didn't tease me. Daddy did, but gently. I'm trying to learn to search before announcing a disaster. I suspect this will take time.
------------------------------

ENTRY 147 — 2000 — Age 14 — A Disagreement at School

A teacher said an answer was wrong because I hadn't used the method we'd been taught. I was certain my result was correct, and I argued longer than I should have. After class she showed me the purpose of the exercise. My answer was right, but I had skipped the part we were supposed to practice. I apologized. She said asking why is good; listening to the answer is also good. I wanted to tell Daddy the story, but first I had to admit that I hadn't been entirely right. He found it hilarious.
------------------------------

ENTRY 148 — 2000 — Age 14 — A New Friend's House

I went to a friend's house after school. It was much smaller than ours, and I worried I'd say something stupid about it. I needn't have worried. Her family made soup, the kitchen was noisy, and her little brother asked me to help repair a toy. We failed the first time and succeeded after finding the missing screw. I stayed for dinner and laughed so much my stomach hurt. When Mother picked me up, she asked whether I'd had a good time. I said yes and meant it without qualifications.
------------------------------

ENTRY 149 — 2000 — Age 14 — High Holy Days Again

I know the prayers more confidently now. What changes is what I notice while saying them. Last year I worried about sounding foolish. This year I kept thinking about a friend who is having a difficult time at home and doesn't want to discuss it. I want to help and don't know how. I lit a candle with Mother afterward and asked whether simply being there for someone counted. She said sometimes it matters more than advice. I am good at advice. Being quiet beside somebody is harder.
------------------------------

ENTRY 150 — 2000 — Age 14 — The Rain Came Back

The first heavy autumn rain smelled like wet pavement and cedar bark. I stood on the porch longer than necessary just to listen. There are cities where people complain when the rain begins; around here we complain when it doesn't stop. I prefer it to too much heat. After dinner I reorganized my notes, putting the bird sightings with the correct dates. I found a note from last spring that just said 'yellow bird near water.' Useless. Future Gwen deserves better descriptions than that.
------------------------------

ENTRY 151 — 2000 — Age 14 — An Unwelcome Compliment

At a family gathering a man told me I would never have to worry about anything because of my inheritance. He meant to be kind, I think. I answered that I still had examinations, friends, and the possibility of falling down stairs, all of which seemed like ordinary worries. Everyone laughed. Later I wished I'd said something more serious. Money changes some difficulties enormously, and it doesn't erase the others. I know that already. I don't know why adults expect me to be grateful for being told it.
------------------------------

ENTRY 152 — 2000 — Age 14 — The New Year's Notebook

I have started using a second notebook because this one is getting thick and the binding complains when I open it. I am keeping the old one rather than copying everything neatly; the spelling mistakes and crooked lines belong to the days I wrote them. Daddy says documents tell more truth when you don't polish away all the evidence. Mother says my desk is beginning to resemble an archive. I intend that as a compliment. Next year I want to learn more about plants, improve at piano, and become less likely to knock things off tables. I shall report honestly on all three.
