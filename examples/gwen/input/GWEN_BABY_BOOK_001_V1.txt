[Gwendolin Lynn Mayer — Baby Book, Notebook I]
Type: Journal
Process Every: 5

Upload note: ISO dates in ENTRY headers are approximate chronological indexing anchors, not documented exact event dates. The mother's original date/season wording is preserved after each anchor. Gwen's birthday of September 8 remains provisional.

ENTRY 001 — 1986-06-01 — 1986, Before Gwen's Birth

My darling Gwendolin,

Your father bought this baby book three months ago. It has a cream linen cover and little pink flowers, and he insisted on having your name put on the front in gold. I told him we hadn't absolutely settled on Gwendolin. He said we'd been calling you Gwen for months and ought to stop pretending there was a committee.

I am fifty-two, your father is sixty, and apparently everyone we know has an opinion about this pregnancy. I am tired of hearing them. The doctors have been attentive and sensible, which is more than I can say for some of our friends. Your father telephones from Seattle twice a day and carries things that weigh less than a sandwich.

Yesterday I found him in the nursery, with one hand on the crib rail. He hadn't heard me come in. For a little while I just stood there watching him.

We have chosen the room overlooking the cedars. Your grandmother in Brooklyn sent a knitted blanket and several pages of advice. I called to thank her; we argued about names, then spent another half hour talking. That is how we love each other.

I want you to arrive safely. Everything else can wait.

Love,
Mother

ENTRY 002 — 1986-09-08 — 1986, The Day Gwen Was Born

My beautiful girl,

You are here. You have reddish hair, an astonishing voice, and a serious little crease between your eyebrows.

Your father cried. I want that written down because he will deny it later. He stood beside me holding you so cautiously that the nurse had to show him where to put his other hand. A man who can address a room full of attorneys without blinking was frightened of dropping a baby.

He kept looking at your fingers. When you wrapped one hand around his thumb he stood perfectly still.

The hospital room is full of flowers, and more cards keep arriving from the firm. I haven't opened them all. Right now I would rather look at you. We brought the little blanket from Brooklyn, and your father put the silver cup from Grandma on the bedside table where he could admire it.

Welcome, Gwendolin Lynn Mayer.

Love,
Mother

------------------------------

ENTRY 003 — 1986-10-01 — 1986, The First Weeks

Gwen has discovered that nighttime is an excellent time for conversation. Unfortunately, she communicates chiefly by objecting loudly to things. Hunger, a wet diaper, a cold blanket, the removal of a warm blanket: all are matters requiring immediate attention.

Her father is unexpectedly good at walking her around the room. He tells her about boats, the weather, and what the Seattle ferry looked like coming home. Yesterday he gave a surprisingly detailed account of how a diesel engine works. Gwen slept through most of it. I think his voice is what she wants.

We have help several days a week, and I am grateful. I love this child, but I am not going to pretend my body is twenty-five years old. I sat by the nursery window today and watched rain gather on the cedar branches while she slept against me. The house was very quiet. It was a perfectly ordinary afternoon, and I hope I remember it.

------------------------------

ENTRY 004 — 1986-12-01 — Winter 1986, Music in the House

Your father came home early and went straight to the piano. He began with Bach, moved to something by Ellington, and then wandered into a tune I recognized but couldn't name. When I asked, he said he had played a little of everything and nothing correctly.

Gwen was in her bassinet nearby. She looked toward the music and kicked both feet. I don't claim this proves she is a prodigy. Her father, of course, has already begun using the word 'musical.'

He has told her about Count Basie, Buddy Rich, Coltrane, and Ella Fitzgerald, although I suspect she is not ready to offer a critical opinion. He dislikes most of what he calls cheap lounge jazz, and I told him she is free to like whatever she likes.

He smiled and played more softly so she could fall asleep.

------------------------------

ENTRY 005 — 1987-02-01 — Early 1987, The Word Cards

Your father has brought home large cards with words printed in red: MOTHER, DADDY, HAND, FOOT, DOG. He has been reading about teaching babies to recognize written words before they can talk. We show them briefly and say each word aloud. Gwen seems delighted by the faces and voices involved.

He has ordered another set. I accused him of trying to get a baby into Harvard without first allowing her to learn to crawl.

We are not keeping score. Some days she looks; some days she reaches for the card; some days she'd rather chew her sleeve. But she enjoys the little ritual, and it gives her father another excuse to sit on the nursery rug instead of answering office calls.

The dog has noticed that people give him biscuits whenever he wanders into the room. He now visits the nursery more often than anyone.

------------------------------

ENTRY 006 — 1987-04-01 — Spring 1987, A Day Outside

We took Gwen into the garden after lunch. Bainbridge smells wonderful when the earth begins warming again, even if everything is still damp. I spread a blanket where the wind wouldn't reach her and brought her soft cloth rabbit.

She watched a robin crossing the grass. It stopped, tilted its head, and pulled a worm from the soil. Gwen leaned forward so abruptly that I had to steady her. The bird flew away. She made a furious little noise at its departure, then forgot it when the dog came over to sniff her toes.

We stayed outside longer than I meant to. Her cheeks were pink when we came in. Your father thinks we should put a swing on the porch. I agree, though I'll insist that somebody competent fasten it to the beam.

------------------------------

ENTRY 007 — 1987-07-01 — Summer 1987, Visiting the Boat

Gwen went down to the boat with us today. The water on Lake Washington was almost flat, and her father spent an absurd amount of time showing her the cleats and explaining knots while I watched from a safe seat in the cockpit.

Naturally she wore a properly fitted infant life jacket. She didn't care for it. She cared even less when Daddy stopped pointing at things and tried to put her down.

I have known this man a long time, and he is happiest when his hands are doing something: piano keys, a wheel, a line on a boat, or a pen over a legal brief. Gwen seems happiest when somebody lets her watch.

Afterward we ate sandwiches on shore. She fell asleep before we reached the car.

------------------------------

ENTRY 008 — 1987-10-01 — Autumn 1987, Learning to Walk

Our daughter regards furniture as equipment invented to help her stand. She pulls herself up on everything. The coffee table is her favorite; the dog has wisely moved his tail out of reach.

She took a few unsteady steps between her father and me this afternoon. He held out both arms. I was sitting on the carpet and barely had time to catch her when she lunged forward.

We cheered. Gwen looked startled, then applauded herself.

Later she fell over trying to turn around too quickly. She sat there contemplating the injustice of it all. Her father said, 'That's what happens when you outrun your footing.' I told him to save the nautical lectures until she could reliably cross a room.

------------------------------

ENTRY 009 — 1987-12-01 — Winter 1987, First Hanukkah She Could Notice

We lit the candles with Gwen watching from her high chair, far enough away that she could not reach the flame. She enjoyed the singing. She also enjoyed banging a spoon against her tray, which didn't improve the tune.

My mother called from Brooklyn. She wanted to know whether we were keeping the traditions properly. I said we were lighting the candles, saying the blessings, eating far too much, and trying to keep a toddler from escaping her chair. She seemed satisfied.

Your father has taken his Jewish studies very seriously. He sometimes asks questions about a practice I've followed since childhood without thinking. I appreciate that more than I usually tell him.

Gwen is fascinated by the little pools of light. I wonder what she will remember of these evenings when she is older.

------------------------------

ENTRY 010 — 1988-04-01 — Spring 1988, The Words on the Page

We still use the reading cards, though now Gwen wants to hold them herself. She recognizes some familiar printed words when we mix the cards and ask for them. I am careful about writing that down exactly. Sometimes she guesses, sometimes she's more interested in the picture, and sometimes she surprises us.

She speaks more every week, in short phrases that seem enormous to us and probably quite ordinary to everyone else. Yesterday she pointed at the dog's dish and informed me, 'Dog eat.' Then she offered him part of her biscuit.

The books are getting worn around the edges. She likes turning pages, and if I stop before the end of a favorite story, she pushes the book back into my hands.

I have decided that whatever she learns early is a gift only if learning remains a pleasure.

------------------------------

ENTRY 011 — 1988-07-01 — Summer 1988, The Ferry

Gwen insisted on standing beside the window for most of the ferry trip to Seattle. We showed her the wake and another ferry passing in the opposite direction. She waved to it, which pleased several strangers nearby.

We went into town for a short visit at the firm. The receptionist made a tremendous fuss over her. Gwen was less interested in the grown-ups than in a brass paperweight shaped like a ship. Your father let her hold it while sitting on his lap, and then had to negotiate its return.

He still works too much. I tell him so. He tells me he has a family to look after. I tell him the family would appreciate seeing him before bedtime. This is an old argument, and we both know our lines.

On the ferry home Gwen fell asleep with her cheek against my coat.

------------------------------

ENTRY 012 — 1988-10-01 — Autumn 1988, The Woods After Rain

A storm came through last night. In the morning the garden was covered with bits of cedar and fir, and everything smelled wonderfully clean. Gwen put on her little boots and demanded to go outside.

She took three steps into a puddle, splashed herself, and laughed. Then she tried to step over a fallen branch, caught her boot, and landed on her bottom. I offered a hand. She accepted it, stood up, and went straight back to the puddle.

We found a small slug on the path. She crouched beside it for several minutes, asking where it was going. I said I didn't know. She thought about that and said, 'Home.'

Perhaps it was. I was glad I hadn't pretended to know.

------------------------------

ENTRY 013 — 1988-12-01 — Winter 1988, A Family Dinner

We had guests from Seattle tonight, and Gwen was allowed to come downstairs after her supper to say hello. She wore a blue dress and one sock. Nobody could persuade her to put the other one on.

She shook hands solemnly with one of your father's colleagues, then asked whether he had a dog. He did not. She appeared to consider this a serious deficiency.

Later the grown-ups talked about the business of the firm and the weather in the mountains. Your father disagreed with almost everyone about something, as usual, while I made sure nobody's glass was empty.

Gwen fell asleep upstairs before dessert. I went to check on her and found the missing sock tucked beneath her pillow. I have no idea why.

------------------------------

ENTRY 014 — 1989-04-01 — Spring 1989, A Little Person

Gwen has opinions about practically everything now. She wants the green cup, not the yellow one; the story about the bear, not the one about the rabbit; the long way through the garden so she can look at the bird feeder.

She is sweet with people, particularly if they seem sad. When I had a headache yesterday she brought me her cloth rabbit and laid it beside me. When I thanked her, she patted my arm very seriously.

She is also clumsy when excited. She knocks over blocks, bumps into chairs, and sometimes falls while turning to look at something interesting. She is rarely discouraged for long.

I am learning that loving a child involves a great deal of watching, and not nearly as much directing as I imagined.

------------------------------

ENTRY 015 — 1989-07-01 — Summer 1989, The Shore

We spent the afternoon near the water on Bainbridge. Gwen collected shells, pebbles, and one piece of driftwood that was much too large for her little bucket. She insisted that Daddy carry it.

A gull came close enough to make her stop talking. She watched it walk along the wet sand and asked whether it had a family. I said I was sure it did, though I couldn't point them out.

On the way home she asked if we could come back tomorrow. We probably can. Sometimes I think we spend a fortune traveling so that we can return to a place as beautiful as the one outside our own door.

She fell asleep in the car holding a smooth gray stone. I placed it on her bedroom shelf. There are already six others.

Love,
Mother
