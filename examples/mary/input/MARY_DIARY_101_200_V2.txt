ENTRY 101 — August 20, 2000
I met Alex Thompson at a community garden event in Wilmington. I was thirty-two. We began talking over compost, which still makes me smile. He was warm, curious, and comfortable with the fact that I cared intensely about things other people considered niche. I noticed how easy it felt to keep talking.

ENTRY 102 — September 11, 2000
Alex and I spent a long afternoon wandering through the city and then sitting near the water. He asked questions without trying to manage the answers. I felt seen in a different way than I had with Michael. Stability did not seem to frighten him.

ENTRY 103 — November 2, 2000
I introduced Alex to my parents. Dad tested him with questions. Mum watched how he listened. Later she told me, simply, "He pays attention." That mattered to her and to me.

ENTRY 104 — February 17, 2001
Love with Alex was quieter than my relationship with Michael had been. Less about possibility, more about daily trust. I began imagining a shared home and, for the first time, children.

ENTRY 105 — July 28, 2001
We visited County Cork together. I watched Alex walk the same River Lee bank that held so much of my childhood. I no longer needed him to feel exactly what I felt. It was enough that he understood the place mattered and treated that importance gently.

ENTRY 106 — August 17, 2002
Alex and I married when I was thirty-four. I remember the strange calm beneath the excitement. I had spent years protecting my independence, yet commitment did not feel like surrender. It felt like choosing a structure sturdy enough to hold two lives.

ENTRY 107 — October 13, 2002
Marriage taught me how intimate ordinary logistics can become. Groceries, bills, whose turn it was to call someone, when to travel, when to stay home. I had expected grand lessons. Instead, trust accumulated through a thousand small acts of reliability.

ENTRY 108 — March 22, 2003
I traveled to the Amazon as part of work against deforestation. The scale of the forest dismantled my confidence that I understood what "large" meant. Every layer seemed alive with another layer underneath it.

ENTRY 109 — March 26, 2003
I stood waist-deep in a river in the Amazon and watched neon tetras flash through clear water below me. Their color looked impossible. For a few seconds the campaign, the reports, and the arguments disappeared. I was simply astonished that such beauty existed without needing me to explain it.

ENTRY 110 — April 4, 2003
The Amazon also showed me destruction at a scale that made anger feel inadequate. I saw how environmental damage was entangled with poverty, markets, policy, and power. Moral clarity did not automatically produce practical solutions. I returned home more determined and less simplistic.

ENTRY 111 — August 16, 2003
Alex listened while I tried to describe what the Amazon had done to me. He did not pretend to understand it completely. That restraint was a kindness. Sometimes companionship means making room around an experience rather than claiming it.

ENTRY 112 — January 31, 2004
My Sierra Club work intensified. I traveled constantly across North Carolina, organizing, lobbying, speaking, listening, and negotiating. Wilmington remained the point I returned to. Alex became part of what made it home.

ENTRY 113 — June 5, 2004
I began to understand my own leadership style. I was not charismatic in the loud sense. I preferred preparation, clarity, and one-on-one trust. I could be forceful when needed, but I was better at building a table than dominating one.

ENTRY 114 — November 14, 2004
An industry representative surprised me by admitting privately that one of our concerns was legitimate. That conversation changed the way I approached opposition. People are often more complicated than the roles they occupy in public.

ENTRY 115 — March 19, 2005
Alex and I began trying seriously for a child. I assumed patience would solve the problem because patience solved so many things in my work. Biology is indifferent to our metaphors.

ENTRY 116 — September 7, 2005
Month after month, hope became procedural: dates, tests, waiting, disappointment. I hated how quickly intimacy could become scheduled and evaluated. I began feeling as though my body had become a problem to solve.

ENTRY 117 — February 12, 2006
I told no one how much infertility was affecting me. I was competent in public and ashamed in private. The word "failure" attached itself to thoughts that did not deserve it. I wish I had been kinder to myself sooner.

ENTRY 118 — July 24, 2006
Alex's frustration looked different from my grief. He became impatient with the uncertainty. I became quieter. We loved each other, but pain was teaching us incompatible forms of silence.

ENTRY 119 — January 8, 2007
I threw more energy into work because work rewarded effort predictably. A campaign might fail, but at least I could identify causes. Infertility offered no such bargain. I began using productivity as anesthesia.

ENTRY 120 — August 18, 2007
Mum asked gently whether I was all right. I said I was tired. She knew I was lying and did not force me to speak. Years later I would remember that restraint as one of her great gifts.

ENTRY 121 — April 5, 2008
Alex and I still had good days. That mattered. The marriage did not collapse all at once. We laughed, cooked, traveled, made plans, and then stepped around the same wound. A relationship can contain love and damage simultaneously.

ENTRY 122 — September 13, 2008
I began practicing a crude form of mindfulness before I used that word. I would sit outside and name what I could actually perceive: wind, temperature, birdcall, breath. Attention gave me a few inches of space between pain and identity.

ENTRY 123 — May 9, 2009
I traveled to Australia in my early forties to study the effects of invasive species on native habitats. The ecological problems were different from North Carolina's, but the underlying pattern was familiar: systems destabilized by human movement and decisions.

ENTRY 124 — May 16, 2009
On a rocky beach in Australia, I found a stranded sea turtle. I knelt and guided it toward the water, feeling the rough shell under my hands. When it finally broke free and moved into deeper water, I felt absurdly hopeful. One life returning to its element was enough for that moment.

ENTRY 125 — June 2, 2009
Australia changed my advocacy. I became less interested in confrontation as identity and more interested in education, local knowledge, and coalitions. Lasting change required people who disagreed on many things to cooperate on the thing in front of them.

ENTRY 126 — March 18, 2010
Dad was slowing down, though he disliked admitting it. We walked a field together, and for once I adjusted my pace to his without mentioning it. I noticed how strange it felt to begin caring for the person who had taught me how to move through the world.

ENTRY 127 — August 22, 2010
Mum still identified plants faster than I did. Age had not dulled her pleasure in recognition. I began asking more questions about her childhood in Ireland, aware for the first time that opportunities to ask were finite.

ENTRY 128 — January 15, 2011
Jack and I exchanged a long series of letters about our parents. He was in New York, immersed in finance, and carried guilt about distance. I told him distance did not cancel love. Writing it helped me believe it myself.

ENTRY 129 — July 30, 2011
Alex and I took a trip that should have been restorative. Instead, we discovered how practiced we had become at avoiding certain subjects. Silence can look peaceful from the outside while consuming a relationship from within.

ENTRY 130 — April 7, 2012
I wrote an op-ed arguing that environmental policy needed both science and economic imagination. I was tired of debates where each side pretended the other had no legitimate concerns. Complexity was not cowardice. It was reality.

ENTRY 131 — October 20, 2012
I began mentoring younger organizers. Their energy delighted me and occasionally exhausted me. I found myself less interested in being the person at the microphone and more interested in helping someone else learn how to use it well.

ENTRY 132 — May 11, 2013
Mum's health worried me. She still tried to protect us from the details. I recognized the impulse because I had spent years doing the same thing with my own pain.

ENTRY 133 — December 1, 2013
Alex and I finally had one of the conversations we had avoided for too long. We admitted that infertility had changed the way we saw ourselves and each other. Honesty hurt, but the avoidance had been hurting us more slowly for years.

ENTRY 134 — June 14, 2014
I began wondering whether activism still fit the person I was becoming. I loved the work, but the constant urgency had become my default nervous system. I wanted to teach people how to think, not only mobilize them to respond.

ENTRY 135 — November 8, 2014
I visited my parents and sat listening while they debated politics after dinner. Their voices were older, but the pattern was the same as my childhood. I felt a wave of gratitude so strong it frightened me. Gratitude often arrives carrying the knowledge of impermanence.

ENTRY 136 — March 6, 2015
Mum died at eighty-five. Saying the sentence still feels too blunt for what it meant. Fiona had been my first teacher of plants, attention, restraint, and compassion. Losing her felt like losing a limb I had assumed was part of my body.

ENTRY 137 — March 20, 2015
I returned to Sierra Club work almost immediately and worked too much. I told myself purpose was helping. In truth, I was afraid that if I stopped moving, grief would catch me.

ENTRY 138 — July 12, 2015
On my forty-seventh birthday, I wanted my mother. The childishness of that sentence did not embarrass me. Grief had stripped away the illusion that adulthood makes us independent of our earliest attachments.

ENTRY 139 — January 9, 2016
I accepted that my marriage was failing. Alex and I were not cruel to each other. We were exhausted and poorly connected. I felt torn between saving the relationship and saving myself, and I hated that the two had come to seem mutually exclusive.

ENTRY 140 — August 20, 2016
At forty-eight, I began teaching Environmental Science at UNC Wilmington. Standing before students frightened me more than most public advocacy ever had. A campaign asks for action. Teaching asks you to help someone build a mind they can use without you.

ENTRY 141 — September 10, 2016
I designed courses around field experience and community engagement. I wanted students to touch soil, test water, meet residents, and see policy consequences. Environmental science should not remain trapped behind classroom glass.

ENTRY 142 — February 4, 2017
Dad needed more from me now. I felt the strange reversal of helping him with things he once did effortlessly. I tried to preserve his autonomy even when helping. I did not yet understand how deeply this would shape my own fear of dependence.

ENTRY 143 — September 15, 2017
Dad died at eighty-seven, two years after Mum. The second loss did not simply add to the first. It changed its shape. With both parents gone, the world felt structurally different. I was no longer anyone's child in the ordinary sense.

ENTRY 144 — October 22, 2017
Jack and I spoke for hours after Dad's death. Our different lives fell away. We were simply the two children who remembered the same songs, houses, arguments, summers, and parents. Shared memory became a kind of shelter.

ENTRY 145 — May 12, 2018
Alex and I ended our sixteen-year marriage. We had been together longer than that, but sixteen years as husband and wife had built habits, loyalties, wounds, and tenderness that could not be summarized by "divorce." I chose to leave because staying had become another form of disappearing.

[Mary's Teaching, Farm, and Renewal]
Type: Journal
Source: Mary
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 146 — June 3, 2018
After the marriage ended, I practiced mindfulness deliberately. I learned to watch thoughts rather than automatically obey them. Some mornings the achievement was simply noticing, "I am ashamed," instead of becoming shame itself.

ENTRY 147 — August 17, 2018
I returned to the woods whenever grief became too loud. Trees did not fix anything. That was precisely why they helped. They required nothing from me except presence.

ENTRY 148 — January 26, 2019
Teaching began to feel like a home for parts of myself activism had not used enough. I loved watching a student's face change when a concept became real. Influence did not have to be loud to matter.

ENTRY 149 — August 4, 2019
At fifty-one, I bought a small farm outside Wilmington. The decision looked practical on paper and enormous inside me. After a childhood of moves and an adult life of travel, I owned a place I intended to know slowly. The place is just over three acres north of Wilmington near Castle Hayne. I paid $315,000, brought $120,000 to closing, and took a $195,000 thirty-year mortgage at four percent. My monthly principal and interest came to about $931. For once the numbers were attached to somewhere I intended to stay.

ENTRY 150 — August 18, 2019
The farmhouse was an old stone structure with wooden beams, soft cream plaster walls, and wide oak floors. Shelves quickly filled with books on ecology, agriculture, philosophy, and literature. I wanted the house to feel warm without becoming crowded, useful without becoming sterile. From the east-facing front porch I step into the hall, with the stairs on its north wall, living room to the north and study to the south. West of the living room lies the dining room; south of that is my kitchen, with a screened porch opening west toward the garden. Upstairs my bedroom looks west over the land, while the spare bedroom faces east. I furnished the living room with an oatmeal sofa, walnut reading chairs and a wool rug beside the Jøtul wood stove.

ENTRY 151 — September 1, 2019
I brought home chickens and discovered how quickly dignity disappears when poultry escapes. One morning I spent twenty minutes being outmaneuvered by a bird with the strategic sophistication of a cabbage. I laughed so hard I had to sit down. I named the hens Hazel, Poppy, Clover, Nell, Dot, Juniper, Mabel and Fern. Their coop and fenced run are northwest of the house, beyond the back porch. Hazel is the escape artist. The old barn west of the house stores feed, straw and fencing; it has no horses or cows.

ENTRY 152 — September 22, 2019
I planted the first serious vegetable beds. Pressing seeds into soil felt almost ceremonial. My mother's garden in Cork, my father's lessons, and my own career all converged in that simple act. The farm was not a hobby. It was autobiography in physical form. I laid out eight four-by-twelve-foot raised beds beyond the herbs west of the screened porch. Rosemary, thyme, sage and chives went closest to the steps; vegetables farther out, with figs and berries along the southern edge. I gave the sage-green shed its own gravel pad and installed pegboard for my spade, hoe, rake and pruning tools.

ENTRY 153 — October 14, 2019
I began collecting smooth stones again, this time from water near home. I placed them in a bowl on the windowsill. Some part of me was preserving memory; another part was imposing order on chaos. Perhaps both are true.

ENTRY 154 — November 8, 2019
Finnegan and Bramble arrived and immediately behaved as though the farmhouse had been purchased for them. Their laziness is almost philosophical. I envy their certainty that any warm surface exists primarily for sleeping. Finnegan is the orange tabby with a talent for finding warm places; Bramble is my watchful tortoiseshell. I put their bowls in the kitchen and made a place for them to watch the birds from behind the screened porch.

ENTRY 155 — December 3, 2019
My morning routine settled into a pattern: chickens, vegetable beds, then a walk through the woods. I began looking for tracks in soft mud and guessing who had passed during the night. Deer, raccoons, and smaller visitors left stories without sentences. The shed has a workbench under its south window and tins for seeds, labels and twine. My mudroom sits south of the kitchen, with the washing machine, dryer, freezer and utility sink. That's where I shed muddy boots and rinse the harvest; it keeps the oak floors from becoming another garden bed.

ENTRY 156 — February 15, 2020
Emma from the neighboring farm became a trusted friend. We exchanged advice, seeds, mistakes, and opinions. I valued having someone nearby who understood that sustainable agriculture is both ideal and endless practical problem-solving.

ENTRY 157 — April 11, 2020
Jake, the local naturalist, led a walk and identified something I had misread. I delighted in being corrected. Expertise is healthiest when it remains porous enough for surprise.

ENTRY 158 — July 12, 2020
At fifty-two I thought about independence differently than I had at twenty. Then, independence meant freedom from rules. Now it meant maintaining the ability to choose my own life while remaining connected enough to receive and give care.

ENTRY 159 — September 19, 2020
I traveled to the Himalayas during a period of deep introspection and renewal. The air thinned as I climbed, and anticipation warmed me against the cold. I was searching for nothing specific, which may be why I found something.

ENTRY 160 — September 21, 2020
At dawn in the Himalayas, the eastern sky turned red over snow-covered peaks, then pink, purple, and gold. Sitting there, I realized how much of my life had been reactive—responding to crisis, grief, expectations, emergencies. I wanted the years ahead to contain more deliberate choice.

ENTRY 161 — October 17, 2020
Back home, the Himalayan insight did not transform me overnight. That was almost comforting. Purpose is not a lightning strike. It is a pattern of choices repeated after the revelation has stopped feeling dramatic.

ENTRY 162 — April 4, 2021
I started experimenting more seriously with regenerative practices on the farm. Building soil, rotating crops, reducing waste, observing water movement—these were not abstract environmental positions. They were decisions I could test with my own hands. I divided the beds among leafy greens, roots, garlic and summer fruiting crops, then started cover-cropping and composting. The west garden path leads past blueberries and blackberries toward a small pollinator strip. I can tell you what grew where because I keep a rotation chart in the shed.

ENTRY 163 — August 29, 2021
I cooked vegetable stew from the farm and realized I had become embarrassingly pleased by carrots. There are worse forms of middle age. The sweetness of something pulled from soil I had tended felt like a small proof that patience can become nourishment.

ENTRY 164 — March 12, 2022
I noticed how often I talked to the plants while working. I thanked seedlings, encouraged struggling herbs, and occasionally scolded tomatoes. I know plants do not need my conversation. I do.

ENTRY 165 — December 18, 2022
The farm had become the place where my public and private selves finally stopped competing. Teacher, environmentalist, daughter of immigrants, Irish granddaughter, divorced woman, scientist, gardener—none of those identities had to win. They could coexist.

ENTRY 166 — January 14, 2023
I began writing more regularly for myself again. Not op-eds, not teaching notes, not reports—just thoughts. I had spent decades turning experience into arguments. I wanted a place where experience could remain unfinished. The study desk faces the east window, and I keep my laptop, printer, field notebooks and Cape Fear maps there. Students' papers end up on the dining table anyway, beside the green-glazed mugs in the old pine hutch.

ENTRY 167 — May 20, 2023
My clothes have become a quiet expression of what I value: soft cotton blouses, cardigans woven from natural fibers, jeans, leather that ages honestly, muted colors. Practical elegance suits me. I have no desire to look careless, and even less desire to look manufactured. Moss, the black-and-white border-collie mix I adopted in 2023, has taken to lying beside the kitchen door. He knows the property boundaries better than some of my guests. His leash hangs in the mudroom, and I never let him into the chicken run.

ENTRY 168 — October 8, 2023
I reread Silent Spring and was struck by how much it had shaped the architecture of my life. Rachel Carson gave me a model for combining evidence with moral seriousness. Thoreau gave me another question: how much is enough?

ENTRY 169 — February 11, 2024
I poured a small glass of Loch Lomond Scotch after dinner and listened to orchestral music while rain moved across the windows. I thought about how contentment differs from excitement. Excitement announces itself. Contentment often has to be noticed.

ENTRY 170 — June 29, 2024
Traditional Irish songs can still collapse distance. A few notes and I am back near Macroom, hearing family voices layered across decades. Memory does not reproduce the past. It reanimates selected pieces of it inside the present. The kitchen appliances are not glamorous, but I know their rhythms: GE Profile electric range, GE French-door refrigerator, Bosch dishwasher, Panasonic microwave and the cream KitchenAid mixer. The sink is below the west garden window, and the Lodge skillet is almost always near the stove.

ENTRY 171 — January 5, 2025
I sometimes wonder about Rachel. We lost touch after college, yet she remains one of the people who taught me how to think honestly with another person. Not every important relationship remains physically present. My dark-green 2021 Subaru Outback Premium, bought used in 2024, is parked outside the south mudroom door. I paid $9,800 down on the $27,800 price and financed the rest. I keep its keys in the little ceramic bowl by the east entry, beside my boots.

ENTRY 172 — April 13, 2025
My fear of losing autonomy has sharpened as I age. Death frightens me less than being unable to choose when to rise, where to walk, what to eat, or whether I can go outside. I understand the irony: a life devoted to interdependence has not erased my need for agency.

ENTRY 173 — July 12, 2025
Fifty-seven. I spent the morning with the chickens and the afternoon reading. I thought about how little I now care about appearing impressive. I want to be useful, awake, honest, and connected. That seems enough.

ENTRY 174 — March 9, 2026
I have become more comfortable admitting that intimacy matters deeply to me. Touch can say things conversation cannot. Romance means little when staged and everything when it grows naturally from attention, trust, and genuine desire.

ENTRY 175 — May 16, 2026
I still laugh hardest at the unexpected interruption of dignity: a chicken sprinting under a fence at the worst possible moment, a pompous speech contradicted by reality, a high-tech device failing beside a perfectly functional hand tool. Absurdity restores proportion.

ENTRY 176 — October 19, 2026
I live in a farmhouse on a small acreage near Castle Hayne, north of Wilmington. Castle Hayne Road is the real road that takes me toward town. I bought the place in 2019 for $315,000, put $120,000 down from savings and my divorce settlement, and financed $195,000 on a thirty-year fixed mortgage at 4.0%. Principal and interest are about $931 a month; taxes and insurance bring housing to roughly $1,350 before repairs. I know exactly where the roof leaks and which gate sticks in damp weather.

ENTRY 177 — October 19, 2026
At UNC Wilmington I teach environmental science, prepare lectures, grade work, and advise students. Campus is at 601 South College Road, Wilmington. My university salary is about $58,000 gross a year, with another $12,000 or so in occasional consulting and workshops. That extra work varies; I budget from my regular paycheck after retirement, insurance, and taxes.

ENTRY 178 — October 19, 2026
My car is a dark green 2021 Subaru Outback. I bought it used in 2024 for about $27,800, paid $9,800 down, and financed $18,000 for five years at 6.8%. The payment is about $355 a month. I keep the back seat covered for feed sacks, gardening tools, and muddy crates. Insurance runs about $125 monthly; fuel depends on the trips to campus and the farm supply store.

ENTRY 179 — October 19, 2026
My mobile number is (910) 555-0146, and I've had the same number for years. I use my phone for students, conversations with Jack, checking on Emma next door, and hearing from the mechanic that a small repair has become an expensive one. I still prefer handwritten shopping lists.

ENTRY 180 — October 19, 2026

Housing is about $1,350; car payment $355; car insurance $125; utilities and internet around $275; groceries and household supplies about $450; fuel around $140; and feed, seeds, vet reserves and farm repairs around $300 or more. [Mary's Reflections — Values, Identity, and Future]
Type: Journal
Source: Mary
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 181 — October 19, 2026
Integrity is the value holding the others together. Without it, honesty becomes performance, autonomy becomes selfishness, and respect for interconnectedness becomes rhetoric. I do not expect perfection from myself. I do expect my choices to point in the same direction as my stated values.

ENTRY 182 — October 19, 2026
Honesty is the principle I am least willing to betray. Deception corrodes relationships from inside because it forces another person to respond to a reality that does not exist. Truth can wound, but falsehood steals consent from everyone involved.

ENTRY 183 — October 19, 2026
My politics are less about party identity than about environmental stewardship, economic fairness, and individual freedom. I distrust ideologies that make nuance look like weakness. Real problems usually contain competing goods, limited resources, history, and unintended consequences.

ENTRY 184 — October 19, 2026
I used to think activism required confrontation. Experience taught me that confrontation is a tool, not an identity. Education, coalition-building, negotiation, and local trust often accomplish more. My convictions did not become weaker. My methods became more patient.

ENTRY 185 — October 19, 2026
Spirituality for me begins with recognition that I am not the center of existence. Catholicism gave me early language for compassion, forgiveness, and the sanctity of life. Nature gave me another language: interdependence, cycles, limits, renewal. I no longer feel compelled to force those languages into a single doctrine.

ENTRY 186 — October 19, 2026
The River Lee remains one of the deepest images of home in me. Cold water around bare ankles. Sunlight on moving water. Otters appearing and vanishing. The river has become a symbol, but before it was a symbol it was simply a place where I was happy.

ENTRY 187 — October 19, 2026
My collection of smooth stones embarrasses me only slightly. Each stone is shaped by pressure, movement, collision, and time. I recognize myself in that process. Perhaps collecting them is memory. Perhaps it is control. Perhaps I simply like rounded things on a windowsill.

ENTRY 188 — October 19, 2026
I talk to plants because gratitude deserves expression even when no answer comes back. The practice slows me down. It turns gardening from management into relationship. I do not need the plants to understand me for the habit to change the way I understand them.

ENTRY 189 — October 19, 2026
Infertility left a wound that no later insight completely erased. For years I interpreted my body as a failed promise. I no longer believe that. I wish I could give my younger self the compassion I eventually learned to give students, animals, landscapes, and nearly everyone except myself.

ENTRY 190 — October 19, 2026
I never had biological children. That grief matters, but it is not the whole story. I have mentored students and community members, watched younger people grow into competence, and shared knowledge that will outlive me. Nurturing takes more than one form.

ENTRY 191 — October 19, 2026
Losing Mum and Dad changed the scale of time. Before their deaths, the family story still seemed to have a living source upstream from me. Afterward, Jack and I became custodians of much of what remained. His letters matter because they preserve two perspectives on the same river of memory.

ENTRY 192 — October 19, 2026
Jack and I are opposites in ways that once frustrated me. He became social, competitive, and drawn to finance. I became introspective and environmental. Yet our bond survived because neither of us required similarity as proof of love.

ENTRY 193 — October 19, 2026
Rachel represents another kind of continuity. I do not know where she is now. Still, the teenage nights of books, camping, questions, and laughter remain active inside me. Sometimes a person leaves your daily life without leaving your formation.

ENTRY 194 — October 19, 2026
My farmhouse is the first place I have owned that feels like an answer to childhood mobility. Stone walls, oak floors, cream plaster, books, cats, chickens, vegetables, mud, and morning light. It is not perfect. Its imperfections are part of why I trust it.

ENTRY 195 — October 19, 2026
Finnegan and Bramble remind me that seriousness should have limits. So do the chickens. Any philosophy that cannot survive a cat sitting on the page or a hen escaping during an important thought is too fragile for ordinary life.

ENTRY 196 — October 19, 2026
The Himalayas gave me a sentence I am still learning to live: stop organizing life only as a reaction. Purposeful action does not mean controlling outcomes. It means choosing where I place attention, effort, loyalty, and love.

ENTRY 197 — October 19, 2026
The Amazon taught me awe and scale. Australia taught me hope through one living creature returning to the sea. Cork taught me rootedness. North Carolina taught me how to build a life. None of those places owns me completely. Together they form my geography.

ENTRY 198 — October 19, 2026
My deepest personal fear is loss of autonomy combined with loss of access to the natural world. If I could no longer walk among trees, tend soil, hear birds, or make basic choices about my own days, I would grieve a vital part of myself. Knowing this helps me value those freedoms now.

ENTRY 199 — October 19, 2026
The three experiences I name as most transformative are not really single moments. Cork was a thousand summers compressed into belonging. Losing my parents was a long crucible of grief. Buying the farm gave me a place to turn my convictions into daily practice. Each changed what I thought a life could contain.

ENTRY 200 — October 19, 2026
Looking back over fifty-eight years, I see no straight line. I see roots and departures, arguments and reconciliations, activism and teaching, marriage and solitude, grief and renewal, America and Ireland, biology and spirit, code and love. If there is one thread through it all, it is this: depth of connection matters more than the appearance of certainty. I want the rest of my life to be lived awake.
