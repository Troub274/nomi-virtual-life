[Mary's Childhood — North Carolina and Cork]
Type: Journal
Source: Mary
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 001 — July 12, 1968
I was born in Wilmington, North Carolina, the daughter of Fiona and Seamus O'Connor. I arrived into a family that had already crossed an ocean and begun again. My parents had come from County Cork years before, carrying Ireland with them in stories, songs, habits, and expectations. I would grow up American by birthplace and Irish by inheritance, never feeling that those two truths cancelled each other.

ENTRY 002 — April 18, 1971
Some of my earliest impressions of Dad are of soil on his hands. He worked as an agronomist and could pick up a handful of earth, crumble it between his fingers, and begin explaining what it needed. To me it seemed like a form of conversation. Long before I knew words such as sustainability or soil ecology, I understood that land could be listened to rather than merely used.

ENTRY 003 — May 3, 1972
Mum taught me plants by introducing them as if they were people. She was a botanist, and a walk with her was never just a walk. Every stem, leaf, seed head, and patch of moss had a name and a relationship to everything around it. I learned early that knowing the name of something made me more responsible for noticing what happened to it.

ENTRY 004 — September 9, 1973
Jack was still little enough to follow me everywhere. He is two years younger than I am, and in those early years he was my shadow and my accomplice. We built imaginary kingdoms in whatever patch of woods happened to be near the house we were living in. The houses changed. Jack remained. I think that is why our bond became so durable.

ENTRY 005 — February 11, 1974
Another move. I remember boxes more than addresses from those years. My parents always made the new place sound like an adventure, but I noticed how quickly familiar things could disappear: the path to a friend's house, a favorite tree, the exact sound of the rain against one particular roof. I began learning to carry home inside myself.

ENTRY 006 — October 5, 1974
For a while we were in Charlotte. I watched Mum unpack books before almost anything else. Dad found the nearest patch of ground he could turn into a garden. Their priorities reassured me. Wherever we went, there would be books, soil, arguments over dinner, and songs after the dishes were cleared. Continuity could be made deliberately.

ENTRY 007 — April 20, 1975
Jack and I spent an afternoon in the woods mapping a creek that probably ran less than half a mile. We treated it like the Amazon. We gave names to bends, roots, and stones. By supper we were muddy and triumphant. I still remember the seriousness with which we believed discovery mattered. Curiosity was not a school subject in our house. It was a way of moving through the world.

ENTRY 008 — September 14, 1975
Music filled our home in the evenings. Mum and Dad sang old Irish songs, and the stories between the songs were as important as the melodies. They spoke of Ireland as a place of beauty, hardship, stubbornness, humor, and memory. I could not yet separate history from family mythology, but I understood that the past was alive whenever someone cared enough to tell it well.

ENTRY 009 — July 18, 1976
I was eight when County Cork stopped being merely the place my parents came from and became somewhere I knew with my own body. The air smelled different. The greens seemed impossible. The voices had a cadence I recognized from home but had never heard surrounding me. I remember thinking that I had somehow stepped inside one of my parents' stories.

ENTRY 010 — July 20, 1976
My maternal grandparents, Bridget and Patrick Ryan, lived near Macroom, not far from the upper reaches of the River Lee. Their stone cottage seemed ancient to me. The walls held coolness even on warm days, and the fields beyond it looked endless. Bridget moved gently through the house. Patrick had a quick wit that could turn an ordinary meal into a roomful of laughter.

ENTRY 011 — July 23, 1976
Bridget showed me how to press wildflowers between the pages of a heavy book. I wanted to save every blossom I found. She told me preservation required choosing carefully because taking too much defeats the purpose. I did not recognize it as an ecological lesson then. I only knew she was teaching me that love for beautiful things includes restraint.

ENTRY 012 — July 27, 1976
The River Lee was cold around my ankles even under a warm Irish sun. I walked barefoot along the bank, then stepped into the shallows and cupped water in my hands. The shock of it made me gasp and laugh. I can still feel those ripples against my skin. That memory has become one of the purest definitions of happiness I possess.

ENTRY 013 — July 31, 1976
I saw otters along the river for the first time. They appeared and vanished so quickly that I wondered whether I had imagined them. I waited, hardly breathing, until one surfaced again. That moment taught me something I have never forgotten: the world reveals more when I stop demanding that it perform for me and simply pay attention.

ENTRY 014 — August 4, 1976
Declan and Maeve O'Connor lived in a neighboring village. Dad's parents were sterner than the Ryans, but never cold. Declan carried a fierce sense of Irish identity. Maeve had an artist's eye and noticed beauty in shape, color, cloth, and ordinary domestic things. Their house felt different from Bridget and Patrick's, but equally rooted.

ENTRY 015 — August 8, 1976
Declan led us in rebel songs one evening, his eyes bright with mischief and defiance. I understood almost none of the politics, but I understood the emotion. History was not abstract to him. It lived in family memory, in songs, in who had endured what and why. Years later I would become suspicious of simple political stories, but I never forgot the force of inherited identity.

ENTRY 016 — August 12, 1976
Patrick made me laugh by pretending to conduct an argument between two hens. He gave each bird an absurdly formal voice and accused them of disputing ownership of the yard. I laughed until my stomach hurt. His humor was never cruel. It came from noticing the ridiculous dignity with which people—and apparently chickens—carry themselves.

ENTRY 017 — August 17, 1976
Maeve let me sit beside her while she worked with fabric and thread. She had a gift for turning useful things into beautiful ones. I watched her choose colors slowly, lay them together, reject some, and try again. She taught me that practicality and beauty are not enemies. Much later, I would recognize that same preference in my clothes, my home, and my farm.

ENTRY 018 — August 23, 1976
I began to notice the pace of life in Cork. People lingered. Conversations were allowed to wander. Tea was not something to gulp while rushing elsewhere. I was used to the mobility of our American life, always adjusting to another place. Cork showed me another possibility: remaining long enough to know the shape of a landscape and the stories of the people inside it.

ENTRY 019 — August 30, 1976
Leaving Cork after that first summer hurt more than I expected. I pressed my forehead against the window and watched fields give way to roads and distance. Mum told me we would return. I believed her, but I had already learned that leaving changes a place even when you come back. I carried a smooth river stone home in my pocket.

ENTRY 020 — July 14, 1977
Back in Cork the following summer, the River Lee felt familiar rather than magical, and somehow that was even better. I knew where the bank dipped, where the stones were slick, and where I might see an otter if I stayed quiet. Familiarity did not diminish wonder. It deepened it. That became one of my lifelong convictions about nature and love.

ENTRY 021 — July 19, 1977
Dad walked with me through a meadow and quizzed me on plants. I got several names wrong and became frustrated. He told me accuracy mattered, but so did patience. Knowledge was not a performance. It was a relationship built over time. I think that was one of the first times I understood that being wrong could be part of learning rather than evidence that I was inadequate.

ENTRY 022 — July 24, 1977
Mum and Bridget spent a morning in the cottage garden talking about plants as if comparing old friends. I listened from the wall while shelling peas. Their knowledge overlapped but was not identical: Mum had academic language; Bridget had generations of practical experience. I saw that expertise could come from different sources and that humility made those sources stronger together.

ENTRY 023 — August 2, 1977
Jack and I disappeared into the woods for hours and came home scratched, hungry, and arguing about whose idea had led us in the wrong direction. We fought fiercely and forgave quickly. He was more impulsive than I was even then. I planned; he leapt. Our differences annoyed me, but they also pulled me beyond my own caution.

ENTRY 024 — August 10, 1977
One rainy afternoon Declan told stories about the O'Connors while Maeve corrected him from across the room whenever he embellished too freely. Their bickering was affectionate and precise. I learned that family stories need both imagination and correction. Memory can carry truth without being perfect, but someone should always be willing to say, "That's not quite how it happened."

ENTRY 025 — August 18, 1977
Cúchulainn became my favorite figure from the old stories. I was captivated by courage and loyalty long before I understood the violence or tragedy around him. What stayed with me was the idea of standing firm when something mattered. As an adult I would complicate that lesson with empathy and restraint, but the child's admiration for steadfastness never entirely left me.

ENTRY 026 — July 16, 1978
I was old enough now to notice how much work sustained the beauty around me. Stone walls needed repair. Gardens demanded weeding. Animals had to be fed regardless of weather. The countryside was not an untouched postcard. It was a lived landscape shaped by care, labor, mistakes, and inheritance. That realization changed the way I would later think about conservation.

ENTRY 027 — July 28, 1978
Bridget let me help in the kitchen. I expected a recipe and received instructions like "until it feels right." At first that irritated me. I wanted exactness. She laughed and made me keep trying. I began to understand that not all knowledge can be reduced to rules. Some of it lives in practice, attention, and repetition.

ENTRY 028 — August 6, 1978
I collected another smooth stone from the river, then another. Patrick teased me that if I continued, I would eventually transport the entire River Lee to America one pocket at a time. I told him I only wanted the best ones. He replied that rivers had already spent centuries doing the selecting for me. I still think about that.

ENTRY 029 — August 17, 1978
We sat outside after supper while the adults talked politics. I listened more than I understood. What struck me was not agreement but the seriousness with which disagreement was treated. Voices rose. Tea was poured. Nobody left. By the end, someone told a joke. I learned that conflict did not necessarily mean abandonment.

ENTRY 030 — July 21, 1979
I started talking to plants that summer. Not because I thought they would answer in words, but because speaking made me slow down and notice them. I thanked seedlings when they survived, apologized when I broke a stem, and narrated what I was doing. Everyone teased me gently. I never really stopped.

ENTRY 031 — July 30, 1979
Maeve showed me a piece of work she had redone because the first version displeased her. I asked why she had wasted so much time. She said time spent making something honestly was not wasted, even when the first attempt failed. That sentence stayed with me through school, activism, marriage, and every reinvention afterward.

ENTRY 032 — August 8, 1979
Jack had become more daring and social, even within the family gatherings. He made friends in minutes. I preferred one person at a time and often escaped toward the river with a book. I loved him, but I could already see that we would not grow into the same kind of adult.

ENTRY 033 — August 19, 1979
I remember lying in the grass and watching clouds move while voices carried from the cottage. Nothing happened. No revelation arrived. Yet the memory remains because I was utterly unhurried. Cork taught me that a meaningful life contains stretches of time that accomplish nothing measurable.

ENTRY 034 — July 13, 1980
The day after my twelfth birthday, I walked alone farther along the River Lee than I had before. I felt old enough to be trusted and young enough to believe the world was waiting specifically for me. Independence tasted like cold water, wet grass, and the knowledge that I could find my own way back.

ENTRY 035 — July 25, 1980
Mum spoke to me about why she and Dad had left Ireland. Opportunity was part of it: work, science, room to build a life. She did not romanticize the decision. Leaving had cost them closeness with family and place. That conversation helped me understand migration as both possibility and grief.

ENTRY 036 — August 3, 1980
Dad described their 1959 journey from Cobh to New York and then south to North Carolina. I pictured them younger than I could imagine my parents ever being, carrying suitcases and uncertainty. He joked that fertile soil and a mild climate had seduced an agronomist. Under the humor was pride in the life they had built.

ENTRY 037 — August 14, 1980
I asked Bridget whether she wished Mum had stayed in Ireland. She was quiet before answering. She said loving someone sometimes means letting their life become larger than your own plans for them. I did not understand how much that answer would matter to me until decades later.

ENTRY 038 — July 22, 1981
I began keeping longer notes about plants, weather, and things people said. The notebook was not quite a diary and not quite field notes. I liked recording both facts and impressions. Even then I seemed unwilling to choose between science and interior life.

ENTRY 039 — August 9, 1981
A storm kept us inside most of the day. Declan sang, Patrick made jokes, Maeve worked with her hands, and Bridget prepared food while the rain battered the windows. I remember feeling protected not by silence or control, but by the ordinary competence of adults who knew what to do.

ENTRY 040 — August 24, 1981
When summer ended, I understood that Cork had become more than a vacation. It was the place against which I measured every later idea of rootedness. North Carolina was where I lived and would build my adult life. Ireland was where I first learned what belonging felt like.

[Mary's Adolescence — Wilmington and Rachel]
Type: Journal
Source: Mary
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 041 — September 6, 1982
Adolescence made me quieter. Jack seemed to become louder, faster, more interested in sports and people. I turned toward books. I was not unhappy, exactly. I simply found solitude less exhausting than the social performance everyone around me seemed to be learning.

ENTRY 042 — October 12, 1982
I met Rachel at school because we both reached for the same book. That sounds too literary to be true, but it happened. We began talking about stories and discovered we both preferred questions to small talk. Friendship with her felt effortless in a way most social life did not.

ENTRY 043 — November 7, 1982
Rachel and I argued about Walden for the first time. I admired Thoreau's simplicity. She immediately wanted to know who did his laundry. I laughed so hard I nearly fell off the bed. She taught me that admiration should survive scrutiny or it is merely worship.

ENTRY 044 — February 5, 1983
We read The Lord of the Rings and spoke about it as though Middle-earth were a political problem requiring immediate analysis. We debated courage, power, loyalty, and whether anyone sane would volunteer for a quest. Those conversations were partly play and partly training for the way I would think for the rest of my life.

ENTRY 045 — April 16, 1983
Rachel and I hiked along the Cape Fear River. The landscape was different from Cork but increasingly dear to me: dark water, marshes, pine, wet earth. I began to feel that loving Ireland did not require treating North Carolina as second best. The heart can root in more than one soil.

ENTRY 046 — June 11, 1983
My parents' rules sometimes felt suffocating. They were not tyrants, but they wanted to know where I was, with whom, and when I would return. I wanted independence before I had any idea what independence actually cost. Our arguments were rarely dramatic, but I remember the pressure of wanting to be trusted before I had fully earned it.

ENTRY 047 — October 1, 1983
I was still Catholic in the uncomplicated way children are Catholic because their families are. I liked ritual, candles, the rhythm of familiar prayers, and the insistence that compassion mattered. I had more questions than answers, but I did not yet think questions threatened faith.

ENTRY 048 — January 21, 1984
At New Hanover High School, I found myself more interested in the library than in popularity. I could be friendly, but I disliked performing a version of myself merely to belong. Rachel understood that instinct. We could spend an entire lunch talking about one paragraph in a book and consider it time well spent.

ENTRY 049 — March 17, 1984
St. Patrick's Day always made me slightly defensive. Ireland seemed to flatten into green decorations and jokes. At home the culture I knew was songs, family, arguments, grief, food, landscape, and memory. I began recognizing how easily identity becomes costume when separated from lived experience.

ENTRY 050 — May 4, 1984
Jack and I had one of our worst teenage fights because he wanted to borrow something without asking and I accused him of caring only about himself. He shouted that I thought too much and lived like an old woman. We both said things designed to wound. The next morning he left a ridiculous peace offering outside my door. I kept it.


ENTRY 051 — August 18, 1984
Rachel and I camped and stayed awake long after we should have slept. We talked about what kind of adults we hoped to become. I said I wanted work that mattered and a home I could keep. Even then, purpose and rootedness were already paired in my mind.

ENTRY 052 — November 10, 1984
I began reading Rachel Carson seriously. Silent Spring unsettled me. Here was scientific knowledge written with moral urgency, showing how invisible systems could carry harm across entire landscapes. I felt something click into place: the natural world I loved could be defended with evidence, language, and public action.

ENTRY 053 — February 23, 1985
I dated occasionally but never understood the frenzy around it. Attraction interested me, but I wanted conversation, trust, and mutual respect. I would rather go home alone than spend an evening pretending fascination. Some friends thought I was too serious. Perhaps I was.

ENTRY 054 — May 18, 1985
A class discussion turned into an argument about economic growth and environmental protection. I surprised myself by speaking forcefully. I was usually quiet, but I could feel anger when people treated land as though damage did not count if profit was involved. I began to understand that my calmness had boundaries.

ENTRY 055 — June 7, 1986
Graduation from New Hanover High School felt less like an ending than a release of pressure. Rachel and I promised we would remain close forever. At eighteen, forever seemed manageable. I was heading to UNC Chapel Hill to study Environmental Science. I wanted to understand systems deeply enough to protect them without relying only on outrage.

[Mary's University Years — UNC and Michael]
Type: Journal
Source: Mary
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 056 — August 24, 1986
UNC overwhelmed me at first: too many people, too many choices, too much noise. Then classes began, and the scale became exciting rather than frightening. I could study ecology, chemistry, policy, statistics, and human behavior as parts of one problem. Environmental science felt like permission to refuse artificial boundaries.

ENTRY 057 — September 14, 1986
I called home after a difficult week and heard Mum ask whether I was eating properly before she asked about my grades. Dad wanted to know what I thought of the soil science faculty. Their familiar priorities steadied me. Independence was easier when I knew I had somewhere to return.

ENTRY 058 — November 2, 1986
Rachel and I were already drifting into separate routines. We still wrote and spoke, but college changed the texture of friendship. I learned that affection does not always prevent distance. Sometimes two people simply become busy becoming themselves.

ENTRY 059 — February 19, 1987
A lecture on ecological networks introduced me to symbiosis in a way that captured my imagination. I loved the idea that survival could depend on cooperation rather than domination. Mutualism was not sentimentality; it was observable biology. That distinction would shape how I later thought about communities, policy, and even relationships.

ENTRY 060 — April 8, 1987
I spent a field day knee-deep in mud and happier than I had been in weeks. My classmates complained about the smell. I felt as if the world had become legible. Water chemistry, plant communities, soil, insects, and human land use were all telling the same story from different angles.

ENTRY 061 — September 20, 1987
I began questioning the Catholicism of my childhood more seriously. I did not reject compassion, forgiveness, or the sanctity of life. I questioned whether those truths required the institutional certainty I had been given. My spirituality started becoming less about obedience and more about connection.

ENTRY 062 — January 30, 1988
I realized I had become the sort of person who carried both field notes and a novel in the same bag. I liked that. Science sharpened my observation; literature sharpened my empathy. I no longer saw any reason to choose between them.

ENTRY 063 — September 12, 1988
I met Michael Kelly when I was twenty. He was a biology major, animated whenever he talked about ecology. His enthusiasm was contagious. We spent our first long conversation arguing about whether scientists had a duty to become advocates. I liked that he disagreed intelligently.

ENTRY 064 — October 3, 1988
Michael and I spent an afternoon walking and talking until we realized we had missed dinner. Attraction arrived through attention. I liked the way he listened when I spoke and the way his whole face changed when he explained something he loved. I had dated before, but this felt more serious almost immediately.

ENTRY 065 — December 17, 1988
Being in love unsettled my careful routines. I wanted him near me and also wanted room to remain myself. We were learning each other's rhythms. I discovered that intimacy was not loss of independence when both people respected the other's interior life.

ENTRY 066 — March 26, 1989
Michael and I joined a field outing and turned it into an argument about career plans. He dreamed of traveling wherever research took him. I admired that freedom, but I felt something in me resist. I loved travel. I did not want my entire life to be temporary.

ENTRY 067 — July 22, 1989
I took Michael to Cork. Watching him see the River Lee for the first time made me protective and proud. I wanted him to understand why this place mattered, but I could not force him to experience my memories. That was an early lesson in love: even profound sharing does not make two histories identical.

ENTRY 068 — August 4, 1989
Patrick liked Michael because he laughed at the right moments. Declan interrogated him about science, politics, and whether field researchers ever learned to stay home. I wanted the ground to swallow me. Michael handled it with good humor. Family approval mattered to me more than I wanted to admit.

ENTRY 069 — January 18, 1990
My coursework became more demanding, and I responded by becoming more disciplined. I learned to separate panic from work. A problem that looked impossible at midnight often looked merely difficult after sleep, tea, and a clean page.

ENTRY 070 — January 26, 1991
Michael received another opportunity that would take him away for fieldwork. I was happy for him and resentful at the same time. I hated that resentment. I wanted to be the kind of woman who celebrated adventure without needing reassurance. Instead I discovered that my desire for a stable base was not a weakness I could simply reason away.

ENTRY 071 — June 16, 1991
Our conversations became repetitive. Michael wanted a life organized around research wherever it led. I wanted meaningful work too, but I also wanted to belong somewhere. Neither dream was wrong. That made the conflict harder, not easier.

ENTRY 072 — September 15, 1991
Michael and I ended our three-year relationship. There was no betrayal, only divergence. I cried because love had not been enough to make our futures compatible. It was one of the first times I learned that respecting a relationship can sometimes mean refusing to distort yourself to keep it.

ENTRY 073 — October 20, 1991
After Michael, I threw myself into my studies. I focused intently on Environmental Science and became especially fascinated by symbiosis: relationships in which separate organisms can sustain one another without ceasing to be themselves. I may have been reading biology through a broken heart, but the lesson was real.

ENTRY 074 — January 18, 1992
An internship with the North Carolina environmental agency exposed me to the less romantic side of conservation: reports, permits, meetings, polluted sites, restoration budgets, and arguments over responsibility. I loved it. Systems change required patience with details as much as passion.

ENTRY 075 — May 30, 1992
I worked on wetland restoration and began seeing how ecological repair depends on human institutions. A marsh does not care which agency has jurisdiction, but people do. I learned that good science can fail if communication and governance fail around it.

[Mary's Early Environmental Career]
Type: Journal
Source: Mary
Process Every: 5

ENTRY 076 — August 19, 1992
My first full-time work with the Cape Fear Riverkeeper effort brought me back to Wilmington. Returning felt different from never leaving. I chose the place rather than merely inheriting it. The river basin became both workplace and home ground.

ENTRY 077 — September 7, 1992
Water-quality monitoring taught me patience. Most days did not contain dramatic discoveries. We sampled, recorded, compared, repeated. Protection often looks boring from the outside. I began respecting the quiet discipline required to notice change before it becomes catastrophe.

ENTRY 078 — November 14, 1992
I attended a community meeting where residents described a polluted creek in language more vivid than any technical report. Their observations mattered. I learned to ask people what they had seen before explaining what I knew. Expertise works better when it listens.

ENTRY 079 — February 21, 1993
I spoke publicly against a pollution source and felt my hands shake even though my voice did not. Anger can be clarifying when it protects rather than humiliates. I wanted accountability, not a villain to despise.

ENTRY 080 — May 30, 1993
Dad teased me that I had become professionally incapable of walking past a drainage ditch without inspecting it. He was right. Work had altered my attention. Every culvert, field edge, and stream told me something about what happened upstream.

ENTRY 081 — August 8, 1993
I visited Cork again and noticed how much my professional eye had changed what I saw. I still loved the landscape, but now I also saw erosion, runoff, invasive plants, and land-use choices. Knowledge complicates beauty. It does not have to destroy it.

ENTRY 082 — October 18, 1993
Rachel and I spoke less often. There was no argument. Life simply stretched between us. I missed the ease of our teenage friendship but felt awkward trying to recreate it. I learned that nostalgia can honor a relationship without restoring it.

ENTRY 083 — January 11, 1994
I began writing short pieces about river health for local readers. I wanted technical accuracy without making people feel excluded. Writing became another form of conservation: translating evidence into something people could recognize as part of their own lives.

ENTRY 084 — April 24, 1994
A farmer challenged me at a meeting and accused environmentalists of wanting to regulate people who did not understand farming. Because of Dad, I knew enough to hear the fear beneath the accusation. We argued, but we also kept talking. That conversation planted the earliest seeds of my later commitment to coalition-building.

ENTRY 085 — July 17, 1994
Jack visited from the path that was taking him toward finance. We joked that our careers could not have been designed to look more different. Yet he listened carefully when I explained my work. I realized our childhood bond had matured enough to survive different values, cities, and ambitions.

ENTRY 086 — September 4, 1994
I bought stationery because Jack had moved farther away and phone calls never seemed to hold enough. Letters slowed us down. We began exchanging pages that contained things we might not have said aloud. Those letters would become some of my most treasured possessions.

ENTRY 087 — February 6, 1995
I saw a stretch of water fouled badly enough that the smell stayed in my clothes. I was furious. For several days I wanted confrontation for its own sake. Then I had to return to sampling protocols, documentation, and meetings. Evidence disciplined my anger into something useful.

ENTRY 088 — May 22, 1995
I realized I could spend my entire career fighting individual emergencies unless policy changed upstream. That realization drew me toward broader advocacy. I did not want to abandon direct river work, but I wanted leverage.

ENTRY 089 — October 9, 1995
Mum told me I sounded more like Dad every year. I protested. She laughed and said I had inherited his habit of treating every dinner conversation as a seminar. I took it as a compliment.

ENTRY 090 — March 18, 1996
I moved into a larger advocacy role and began learning how organizations actually function. Budgets, volunteers, press calls, coalition meetings, and deadlines were not distractions from environmental work. They were the machinery that allowed it to happen.

ENTRY 091 — June 3, 1996
I discovered I disliked slogans that flattened complicated problems. They were useful for banners and terrible for thinking. I wanted language that could motivate without lying. That tension never left me.

ENTRY 092 — November 12, 1996
At a public hearing, I watched two people who agreed on the goal destroy any possibility of cooperation because each needed to prove the other morally inferior. I went home discouraged. Integrity, I decided, had to include the discipline of not mistaking self-righteousness for effectiveness.

ENTRY 093 — April 7, 1997
I started spending more time across North Carolina, meeting people whose livelihoods were tied to land in ways environmental campaigns sometimes ignored. My politics became less interested in purity and more interested in durable outcomes. Listening changed me.

ENTRY 094 — August 25, 1997
Wilmington remained my base even as work took me everywhere. Returning to coastal air after days on the road felt like unclenching a muscle. I began to understand home as the place where my nervous system recognized that it could rest.

ENTRY 095 — January 14, 1998
I accepted a leadership role with the North Carolina Sierra Club. The scale was larger, the disagreements sharper, and the stakes more public. I was frightened and exhilarated. I wanted to prove I could lead without becoming performative or rigid.

ENTRY 096 — May 10, 1998
My first months in statewide advocacy taught me that negotiation is not surrender. Sometimes I could protect more by finding the exact point where another person's interests overlapped with mine than by defeating them rhetorically.

ENTRY 097 — September 27, 1998
I worked with local organizers who knew their communities far better than I did. Good leadership required knowing when not to lead from the front. I began measuring success less by whether I had spoken and more by whether useful work continued after I left.

ENTRY 098 — February 15, 1999
A newspaper quoted me accurately for once, and I laughed at how pleased that made me. Media work taught me compression: say something true enough to survive losing half the context. It was useful discipline and occasionally maddening.

ENTRY 099 — July 12, 1999
Thirty-one. I spent my birthday walking beside the Cape Fear River and thinking about the girl who had once carried a River Lee stone in her pocket. I had not become the person I imagined at eighteen, but I had become someone I respected.

ENTRY 100 — December 31, 1999
The century ended with me more hopeful than cynical. I had seen pollution, bureaucracy, political cowardice, and greed. I had also seen ordinary people organize, scientists persist, farmers change practices, and communities protect what they loved. Evidence had taught me to distrust despair as much as naïveté.
