ENTRY 0821 — 2024-07-19 — Dad

Dad died today. I cannot make the sentence bigger than that.

He died today. Mom and I were at the hospital. Jason and Mike got there before the end, but time is broken and I cannot put the order together properly.

There were machines. Nurses.

Somebody asking whether we understood. Dad's hand felt warm until it didn't. I kept looking at his fingernails.

Those hands built things. Burned things.

Fixed my bike. Showed me how not to cross-thread a bolt. Held a welding torch for decades.

Pointed at the Barracuda engine while he made me explain what I thought was wrong before he would tell me. Then there was just his hand inside mine and nothing left for either of us to fix.

Mom made a sound I have never heard from her before. I do not know what happens next. I do not know how the world gets to have tomorrow in it.

ENTRY 0822 — 2024-07-21 — The Phone Calls

Two days since Dad died.

The house keeps filling with phone calls. People who loved him. People who worked with him twenty years ago. People Mom barely remembers. Every call begins with the same careful voice, and every time I hear it I brace myself.

Jason is handling some of the family calls. Mike is dealing with the funeral home. I somehow became the person with the folder: insurance, hospital papers, Dad's ID, medication list, the notes from the doctor, names and numbers written on envelopes because nobody had a clean piece of paper when somebody called.

Mom keeps asking where something is and then apologizing for asking. I told her to stop apologizing. This is exactly what I am good at.

That sentence made me cry in the bathroom.

Being good at the paperwork does not mean I am good at Dad being dead.

ENTRY 0823 — 2024-07-22 — His Things

I went into the garage and immediately walked back out.

I know he signed the car over to me months ago.

It still feels like his car.

Maybe it always will.

ENTRY 0824 — 2024-07-24 — Walnut Hill

We buried Dad at Walnut Hill Cemetery today. 1101 Mascoutah Avenue, Belleville. Section 15, Lot 248, Grave 3.

I memorized it because apparently my brain needs grief to have coordinates.

The grass was too green. The sky was offensively beautiful. Somebody's phone rang during the service and I wanted to kill them, then felt guilty because Dad would have thought that was funny.

Mom held my hand on one side. Jason on the other. Mike stood close enough that our shoulders touched.

After people left, I stayed.

I told Dad I still have the Barracuda. I told him I am going to take care of Mom. Then I stopped because I could almost hear him telling me not to turn taking care of everybody into an excuse for never having a life of my own.

So I told him something else.

I told him I am still going to go.

I do not know whether I was promising him or myself.

ENTRY 0825 — 2024-07-25 — After the Burial

I wanted the entire world to understand that Dad was buried yesterday and therefore printers should work without complaint for at least a week.

Mom ate half a piece of toast and claimed she was not hungry. I made her eat soup later.

I went back to Walnut Hill near sunset. I sat beside the grave and talked to Dad more than I usually talked when he was alive. That is probably because he cannot interrupt me with practical advice now.

I told him I was angry.

I did not explain what at.

Everything, probably.

ENTRY 0826 — 2024-07-27 — Mom Knows

Mom knows I am still thinking about leaving Illinois.

I had not planned to talk about it this week. It felt obscene somehow, like discussing my future too close to Dad's funeral meant I was already walking away from him.

Mom brought it up.

She said, "Your father didn't give you that car so you could park it here forever."

I told her I was not leaving her right now.

She said that was not what she said.

Then we had one of our quiet fights where neither of us raises a voice and both of us become extremely interested in wiping already-clean counters.

Finally she said, "Tammy, I lost my husband. I don't need to lose my daughter because she decides grief is a job."

That shut me up.

I still do not know when I am going.

But Mom knows I intend to.

ENTRY 0827 — 2024-07-28 — The Quiet After

The house was full of people for days and now suddenly it is not.

That might be worse.

There are leftovers in the refrigerator from women at church. A stack of cards on the table. Flowers beginning to droop. Mom is sitting in Dad's chair even though she never used to sit there.

I went to the diner for a few hours because Lisa told me I could come in, leave, wash dishes, scream in the freezer, or do absolutely nothing and she would count all of those as reasonable behavior.

It helped.

Not because I forgot Dad.

Because for four hours nobody needed me to understand a form, answer a condolence, or decide what happens to a dead man's account.

Sometimes ordinary work is mercy.

ENTRY 0828 — 2024-07-30 — Paperwork

Death has an administrative department.

Nobody mentions this at funerals.

Copies of the death certificate. Insurance. Hospital billing. Social Security. Bank accounts. Vehicle records even though the Barracuda title is already mine. Mom's beneficiary paperwork. A welding pension question. Every office wants a number from another office.

I made a checklist.

Of course I did.

Jason offered to take half of it. I gave him the pension calls because he has a lower tolerance for hold music but a higher tolerance for arguing with institutions. Mike took the bank.

I kept the medical and insurance paperwork because I already know the history.

For years being the available daughter meant I knew everything.

For once that knowledge is useful to all of us.

I hate the reason.

ENTRY 0829 — 2024-07-31 — Grief Sneaks Up

I made it through almost the whole day without crying.

Then I found one of Dad's pencils in the Barracuda glove box.

Not a special pencil. Short, flat carpenter pencil, sharpened with a knife instead of a pencil sharpener because apparently normal pencils were too civilized for him.

I sat in the driver's seat holding it and lost my mind.

This is what grief does. The funeral did not break me the way the pencil did.

Later Shannon called and let me talk for almost an hour. I told her I feel guilty whenever I laugh. She said that sounds exactly like something my brain would invent to make grief harder than it already is.

She is probably right.

Dad liked making me laugh.

I am trying to remember that without turning it into another rule.

ENTRY 0830 — 2024-08-02 — Insurance

Then it turned green, because traffic remains offensively committed to continuing.

ENTRY 0831 — 2024-08-03 — The Empty Chair

ENTRY 0832 — 2024-08-05 — Mom's Anger

Mom is angry today.

Not at me exactly. At insurance, the hospital, Dad, God, the broken latch on the kitchen cabinet, and probably every person who has ever said "he's in a better place" without being invited to.

She snapped at me for moving an envelope.

I snapped back.

Then we stood in the kitchen staring at each other because both of us knew the envelope was not the problem.

She said, "I don't know how to be a widow."

I said, "I don't know how to be the daughter of a dead father."

That sounds melodramatic written down.

It did not feel melodramatic in the kitchen.

It felt like two women admitting we have no idea what we are doing.

ENTRY 0833 — 2024-08-06 — Grief Sneaks Up Again

A man at the diner had Dad's hands.

Not his face. Not his voice. Just thick fingers, scarred knuckles, dark half-moons of grease near the nails.

I love her for knowing the difference.

ENTRY 0834 — 2024-08-08 — Charleston

I looked at Charleston apartments online again tonight.

Then I looked at tuition.

Then jobs.

Then I closed every tab because the numbers made the whole idea feel stupid.

An hour later I opened them again.

Dad knew I wanted out. Not out of the family. Out of the shape my life had taken because I was always the available one.

I keep hearing something he said before he got really sick: "You can love people from another zip code."

At the time I accused him of trying to get rid of me.

He said, "No. I'm trying to get you to quit hiding behind us."

That was Dad. Tenderness delivered with all the cushioning of a socket wrench.

Charleston still scares me.

The fact that it scares me is becoming less persuasive.

ENTRY 0835 — 2024-08-09 — The Barracuda

I cleaned the Barracuda today.

Actually cleaned it. Vacuumed under the seats, wiped the dash, sorted the glove box, checked fluids, tightened the battery connection, and found three things Dad had left in it that I could not throw away.

The carpenter pencil from last week.

A faded gas receipt.

A tiny folded paper with a part number written in his handwriting.

None of those things are valuable.

All of them are now apparently sacred relics because grief is ridiculous.

I played it while I worked.

For the first time since Dad died, the car felt a little less like a coffin-shaped memory and a little more like the machine he meant me to have.

ENTRY 0836 — 2024-08-11 — Dad's Tools

Jason, Mike, and I went through Dad's tools.

That sentence sounds simple.

It was not.

Every drawer had a history. The good torque wrench. The cheap sockets Dad kept for loaning to people he did not trust. Welding clamps. Electrical testers. Files. Hammers polished smooth where his hands held them.

Jason wanted the big rolling chest because he has the garage space. Mike wanted Dad's old welding hood even though none of us thinks he should actually use it.

I wanted the multimeter and the small socket set Dad taught me on.

Nobody fought.

That made it harder somehow.

We kept asking, "You want this?" in voices that were too careful.

Dad would have hated how solemn we were being about wrenches.

ENTRY 0837 — 2024-08-12 — Three Weeks

Three weeks and a few days.

I keep expecting grief to become more organized.

It does not.

Mom is sleeping badly. I know because her light is on when I get up for water. I am sleeping badly too, but we have both apparently agreed not to discuss this because then somebody might suggest doing something sensible.

Church people still bring food occasionally. The frequency is dropping, which is natural and also feels like a second abandonment even though that is unfair.

I went to work, came home, checked on Mom, and read twenty pages of a book about trauma before realizing I had absorbed none of it.

So I started again.

That is basically my strategy for everything right now.

Start again.

ENTRY 0838 — 2024-08-13 — Mom and Me

Mom and I ate Chinese takeout at the kitchen table tonight.

No television.

No paperwork.

No one visiting.

We talked about Dad before he was sick.

She told me about a date they had in 1983 when his truck broke down and he tried to fix it in dress shoes because he had been attempting to impress her. I have heard pieces of the story before, but not the part where Mom sat on the hood laughing while he swore at the starter.

I asked whether she knew she would marry him.

She said, "Not that night. That night I thought he was an idiot."

I laughed so hard I nearly choked on rice.

It felt good.

Then it hurt.

Then it felt good again.

Apparently those feelings can share a table.

ENTRY 0839 — 2024-08-15 — Charleston Again

ENTRY 0840 — 2024-08-16 — Mom Knows How Serious I Am

Smooth.

ENTRY 0841 — 2024-08-18 — His Work Shirts

Mom asked me to help sort Dad's clothes.

I lasted twelve minutes.

His work shirts were the worst. The old heavy ones with tiny burns, frayed cuffs, and the faint smell that no amount of laundry ever completely removed. Welding fumes, metal dust, sweat, machine shop, Dad.

Mom picked up one blue shirt and pressed it against her face.

I left the room because seeing that felt too private even though she is my mother and he was my father.

We kept some.

Donated some.

Threw away almost nothing today.

There is no law saying grief has to be efficient.

ENTRY 0842 — 2024-08-19 — Brothers

Jason, Mike, and I had dinner without Mom tonight.

That almost never happens.

We talked about Dad. Then baseball. Then Dad again. Then money. Then whether Mom should stay in the trailer long term.

Jason thinks she eventually needs something easier to maintain. Mike thinks bringing it up now would be cruel. I think both things can be true.

At one point Jason apologized for how much baseball swallowed the family when we were kids.

I stared at him.

He said, "I know you noticed."

I told him of course I noticed.

Then I told him I never blamed him for being good at something people celebrated.

That is mostly true now.

It was less true when I was thirteen.

We are getting old enough to forgive each other for childhood things nobody actually designed.

ENTRY 0843 — 2024-08-21 — The Hospital Bill

A hospital bill came today.

There is something grotesque about a bill arriving after the patient is dead.

I know that is how systems work. Services happened. Codes exist. Insurance has to process them. Understanding the mechanism does not make the envelope less ugly.

I called because one line item did not match the explanation of benefits.

The person on the phone corrected it.

A small victory.

Dad would have been proud that I checked instead of paying whatever number they printed.

That thought made me smile.

Then I cried because I cannot call and tell him I caught it.

This is grief too, apparently: having information with nowhere to send it.
