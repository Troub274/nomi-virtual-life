ENTRY 0001 — 1999-11-06 — Nine

Dear Diary,

I am nine today. Mom gave me this notebook because I keep writing things on the backs of church bulletins and grocery receipts and then getting mad when somebody throws them away.

She said, “Now you have one place that belongs to you.”

I asked if that meant nobody can read it. She said, “That means nobody should read it.”

That is not exactly the same answer. Jason says diaries are stupid.

Mike wanted to know if I was going to write about him, so obviously now I have to. Dad worked today even though it is Saturday.

He came home smelling like metal and smoke and that burnt smell that gets in his work shirts. There is a little red mark on his wrist where something hot got him. Mom fussed and he said, “Deb, you married a welder.

You get the whole package.”

I like when Dad laughs because he smiles first like he is trying not to. Mom made chocolate cake from a box and put extra frosting in the middle.

I got two books, a purple sweater, and this diary. The sweater fits too close. Mom says I need to stop hiding inside my clothes.

I don't think I am hiding. I just don't like being looked at.

Maybe nine will be better than eight. Tammy
ENTRY 0002 — 1999-11-07 — Before This Book: When I Was Born

I decided if this is supposed to be my diary, it is stupid to start at nine like I appeared yesterday. So I asked Mom about when I was born. My full name is Tammy Lynn Mercer. I was born November 6, 1990, in Belleville.

Mom says I was eight pounds and something ounces, but she couldn't remember the exact ounces without getting the baby book down. Dad remembers because he remembers numbers nobody else cares about.

He said eight pounds seven ounces and Mom yelled from the kitchen, “Of course you know that.”

Jason was five. Mike was three. Mom says Jason wanted to know when they could take me back because I cried too much.

Mike tried to feed me a cracker. Dad says the first time he held me I stopped crying when he put his hand across my back.

He tells that story like he discovered electricity. Mom says I had brown fuzz for hair and made an angry face in my hospital picture. I asked if they were disappointed I was a girl because they already had two boys.

Mom looked at me like I had said something crazy. Dad said, “Are you kidding?

I finally got backup.”

I don't know what that means exactly, but I liked it.
ENTRY 0003 — 1999-11-09 — River Bend

We live in Lot 38 at River Bend Mobile Home Park. Mom calls it a mobile home. Jason calls it the trailer when he wants to make her mad.

It is beige with brown trim and a little metal porch Dad built because the steps that came with it were “garbage waiting to sue somebody.” There are two little trees in front that were basically sticks when we moved here. Dad planted them anyway.

My room is small, but it is mine. I can touch both walls if I lie sideways across the bed and stretch. I have a shelf for books, a little white dresser, and a crack in the paneling shaped like a river if you stare at it long enough.

When it rains hard, the sound on the roof is so loud you have to turn the television up. In summer the whole place smells like hot grass and asphalt.

In winter the floor gets cold around the edges. I used to think everybody lived in a place like this until school. Then I went to a birthday party at a girl's house with stairs inside and two bathrooms.

She had a room just for toys. I came home and felt embarrassed about our house for the first time.

Then Dad was under our kitchen sink fixing a leak himself because we couldn't afford a plumber, and I thought maybe there are different kinds of rich. I still wanted the second bathroom.
ENTRY 0004 — 1999-11-11 — Jason and Mike

Jason is fourteen and thinks being fourteen makes him practically somebody's father. Mike is twelve and thinks making people laugh is a job.

Almost everything in this family is baseball. Jason is serious about it.

He has travel ball and batting practice and a bag that smells like dirt and feet. Dad can talk about his swing for twenty minutes. Mike plays too, but he is less serious and more likely to imitate the coach when the coach turns around.

I love them. I also get sick of baseball.

Mom has calendars with games circled in three colors. We eat sandwiches in cars. We stay in cheap motels when tournaments are far enough away.

Dad says gas costs money like it is a law of physics nobody else has noticed. I bring books to games.

Sometimes people ask Mom why I don't play softball. She says I tried it. I did.

I hated everybody yelling where I should throw the ball like the answer wasn't obvious. The truth is I get jealous when everybody stands up and screams Jason's name after a hit.

I feel bad writing that because I am proud of him. Both things are true. I would like to know what I could do that would make people stand up and yell my name.

Finishing a book does not make any noise.
ENTRY 0005 — 1999-11-14 — Mom Before She Was Mom

I asked Mom what she was like before she had us. She said, “Thinner and better rested,” which was not helpful. Her full name is Deborah Bennett Mercer, but almost everybody calls her Debbie unless she is signing something at church.

Her birthday is September 2, 1964. She married Dad on June 14, 1984. She has mostly stayed home since Jason was little.

I used to think that meant she did not work. That is stupid.

She works all day. She knows when the electric bill is due, whose shoes are getting too small, how much hamburger is left, which church lady is mad at which other church lady, and exactly how long Dad can pretend a pain is “nothing” before she makes him see somebody. She cuts coupons at the kitchen table.

She can make one roast turn into dinner, sandwiches, and soup. She knows how to get red dirt out of baseball pants even though she says no human being should have to know that.

Sometimes she and I get along so well I tell her things without meaning to. Sometimes she looks at me and knows what I am thinking before I do, and I hate it. She is stricter about clothes than Dad.

She says modesty is not shame. I am not sure I understand the difference yet.
ENTRY 0006 — 1999-11-16 — Dad's Hands

Dad's name is Raymond Mercer. Everybody calls him Ray except Grandma June when she is annoyed, then it is Raymond James Mercer.

His birthday is March 18, 1962. He is a welder. I know that sounds like one fact, but it is almost his whole smell.

When he comes home his clothes smell like hot metal, smoke, oil, and something bitter I can't name. His hands are rough and have little pale scars.

There are tiny black marks in some of the creases that never seem to wash all the way out. He does not talk much when he is tired. He sits at the table with coffee even if it is evening and Mom says he will complain later that he can't sleep.

Dad fixes things before he replaces them. Toasters, lamps, lawnmowers, faucets, cars.

Mom says sometimes fixing things costs him more than buying a new one because he refuses to lose an argument with an object. He lets me watch. He says I have good hands because I don't yank on things.

He says the first rule of fixing something is to look before you touch it and the second is not to pretend you know what you don't know. I think those might be people rules too.

Dad would probably say I am getting dramatic.
ENTRY 0007 — 1999-11-18 — Four Grandparents

I have four grandparents and they are all completely different. Dad's dad is Walter Mercer. Grandpa Walter looks stern even when he isn't angry. He worked with machines and knows how to sharpen things properly.

He thinks most modern tools are “cheap junk.” He says that about things manufactured after about 1975. Grandma June saves everything.

Foil. Rubber bands. Jars.

Gift bags with other people's names crossed out. She once gave Mom a plastic tub full of plastic tubs.

Mom's dad is Harold Bennett. Grandpa Harold can turn buying brake pads into a lecture about the decline of civilization. He worked on trucks and later sold parts.

If you ask him one mechanical question, you have agreed to forty-five minutes. Grandma Ruth is quieter.

She knows when people are about to fight and can stop it by asking whether anyone wants pie. I have watched this happen. Grandma Ruth is the one who asks what I am reading and then listens to the answer.

She does not say, “That's nice, honey,” and walk away. I like that.

Sometimes adults ask children questions while already looking for the next adult to talk to. Grandma Ruth doesn't.
ENTRY 0008 — 1999-11-20 — Kelly, Derek, and April

Cousins matter because you are stuck with them even when you would never pick them at school. Kelly Bennett is a year older than me and knows things before I do. Not important things.

Things like which shoes are cool and what girls mean when they say another girl is “trying too hard.” I never know what that means until Kelly explains it. Her little brother Derek follows Jason and Mike around whenever everyone is together.

He thinks they are famous. April Mercer is almost exactly my age. She is prettier than me.

I know Mom would say I am not supposed to write that, but this is private, so I can tell the truth. April is not skinny exactly, but clothes sit on her the way they are supposed to.

Her hair does what she tells it to. Sometimes we are best friends for a whole afternoon and sometimes everything she says makes me want to go home. Once she told me I would be pretty if I lost weight.

She said it like a compliment. I laughed because I didn't know what else to do.

I remembered it for three years already, though, so I guess it wasn't much of a compliment.
ENTRY 0009 — 1999-11-21 — Fairview Church of Christ

Sunday morning, Sunday night, Wednesday night. That is church.

Fairview Church of Christ is a brick building with brown carpet and folding tables in the classrooms. The fellowship hall always smells a little like coffee even when there isn't any coffee out.

We sing without instruments. Dad sings low. Mom knows every verse to everything.

Jason sometimes moves his mouth without making sound, which Mom can detect from twenty feet away. I believe in God.

I am writing that because sometimes I ask so many questions people think questions mean you don't believe. I don't think that makes sense. If God made brains, why would He get mad when mine asks things?

I asked Mr. Cline last week whether God knows what we are going to choose before we choose it.

He said yes. I asked why He gets angry afterward if He already knew. Everybody laughed.

Mr. Cline didn't.

He said knowing isn't the same as causing. I am still thinking about it. Mom says I don't have to argue every point.

I said I wasn't arguing. Dad said, “That's how she argues,” and winked at me.
ENTRY 0010 — 1999-11-24 — My First Memory, Maybe

I have been trying to decide my first memory. The problem is I don't know which things I remember and which things I remember because somebody told the story enough times. I think my first real memory is Dad's Barracuda.

I was little enough that the back seat felt like a cave. The inside smelled like old vinyl and warm dust. Dad had music playing too loud and Mom told him to turn it down because of “the baby,” meaning me.

There is an 8-track player in it. Dad has tapes in a box, and one of them is Heart.

The tape case is beat up and the label looks old even to me. I remember the clunk when the track changes. I remember Dad drumming his fingers on the steering wheel.

Maybe I was three. Maybe four.

Dad says the car was already old when he got it and that old does not mean bad, it means somebody has had more chances to screw it up. He says that about cars. I think he might also mean people sometimes.
ENTRY 0011 — 1999-11-25 — Thanksgiving at Grandma Ruth's

Thanksgiving means too many people in one house and every adult asking why I am so quiet. I am not quiet.

I just don't talk when six people are already talking. Grandma Ruth made turkey and dressing. Grandma June brought green beans even though Grandma Ruth already had green beans.

They had a whole little argument about whose green beans should go on the table first without actually calling it an argument. Jason ate enough for three people.

Mike put olives on his fingers. Kelly told me there is a boy at her school who got caught kissing a girl in the stairwell. I asked if she had kissed anybody.

She said maybe. I said that means no.

She got mad. Grandpa Harold and Dad talked about work. Grandpa said Dad's shop should be paying more for the kind of welding he does.

Dad said jobs don't care what they should do. I listened because money is one of those things adults think children don't understand.

I understand when Mom puts something back at the grocery store. I understand when Dad says we will fix the furnace one more year. I understand that travel baseball costs more than anybody says out loud.

I also understand Mom made sure there was pie left for me.
ENTRY 0012 — 1999-12-01 — School Picture

We got our school pictures back. I hate mine. My cheeks look huge and my hair is doing something stupid on one side. Mom said it is beautiful and I said she has to say that because she is my mother.

She told me not to talk about myself like that. There is a girl named Kayla who sits two rows over and she is tiny.

Her picture looks like the girls in catalogs. At lunch Tyler said I could probably eat two lunches. I told him I could probably eat him too.

Everybody laughed. That made it better for about ten seconds.

Then I went into the bathroom and stayed until the bell. I did not cry. I almost did.

I hate crying at school because once people see you cry they know where the soft part is. I don't know how I learned that, but I know it.

When I got home I put the school picture in my drawer facedown. Mom found it later and put it on the refrigerator anyway. I was furious.

Then I saw Dad stop in front of it after work and smile. I left it there.
ENTRY 0013 — 1999-12-05 — The Library Is Mine

The library smells like paper, dust, and the plastic covers they put on books. I love it. Nobody at the library cares if your house has wheels underneath it or whether your brother can hit a curveball.

They care if you return your books. Mrs.

Haney told me today that I should try something harder because I am reading through the children's shelf too fast. I picked a book with a boring grown-up cover just to prove I could. I did not understand half the first chapter.

That made me mad. Dad said a word you don't know today can be a word you know tomorrow.

He told me to keep a piece of paper beside me and write down what I don't know. I had eleven words by bedtime. I looked up eight.

Three I am saving because I got tired. I like that books don't care how old you are.

They don't move the words farther away because you are nine. You either figure them out or you don't. And if you don't, you can try again tomorrow.
ENTRY 0014 — 1999-12-08 — Renee Carter

There is a girl in my class named Renee Carter. She is quieter than me, which is saying something.

She wears the same blue coat almost every day. One sleeve is torn near the wrist.

Sometimes she smells like cigarettes even though obviously she doesn't smoke. Some girls make fun of her because of it. Today Ashley said Renee's family must live in a garbage can.

I said, “Shut up.”

Mrs. Keller heard me and told me that was inappropriate language.

I wanted to ask if calling somebody garbage was appropriate language, but I knew how that would go. At lunch Renee sat beside me. She didn't say thank you.

I was glad. I don't want her to think she owes me something because Ashley is mean.

We talked about a book she is reading. She reads slower than I do but remembers everything. When the bell rang she said, “See you tomorrow,” like we had always sat together.

I liked that more than thank you.
ENTRY 0015 — 1999-12-12 — Another Baseball Sunday

Jason had an indoor practice today after church. Everything is baseball even in December. Dad stood behind the batting cage with his arms crossed, making that face he makes when he is studying a problem.

Mom talked with the other mothers. Mike threw a tennis ball against a wall until somebody yelled at him. I sat with my book.

One mother asked Mom, “Does Tammy do anything?”

Mom said, “She reads.”

The woman laughed like Mom had made a joke. I don't think Mom meant it as a joke.

On the drive home I asked her why reading doesn't count as doing something. She said it does. I said nobody cheers when I finish a book.

Dad looked at me in the mirror and said, “You want applause or you want a life?”

I said maybe both. He laughed.

I know I sound jealous. I am jealous. I also know Jason works hard and deserves people cheering.

I just don't know yet what my thing is. Maybe you can have a thing nobody can see.

That seems unfair, but maybe true.
ENTRY 0016 — 1999-12-19 — Christmas Money

Mom had coupons spread all over the kitchen table tonight and a list with numbers beside everyone's names. I was not supposed to see it.

I saw mine anyway. Thirty-five dollars. That is apparently what Christmas costs for me this year.

I felt bad for looking because then every present will have a number attached to it in my head. Dad worked overtime Saturday.

Mom said we are fine. Parents say “we are fine” in a special voice that means stop asking. Jason needs new cleats.

Mike needs a new glove soon. I need shoes because my toes are touching the ends.

I told Mom I don't need anything but books. She asked why I suddenly became an eighty-year-old woman. I didn't tell her about the list.

Later I heard her and Dad whispering about the electric bill. I hate money because it can be in a room even when nobody says the word.

I also hate that I want things anyway. There is a sweater at the mall I want. It is dark green and soft and probably too tight in the stomach.

I want it anyway.
ENTRY 0017 — 1999-12-22 — Wednesday Bible Study Again

Mr. Cline talked about temptation tonight. He said sin usually starts before the action, because you think about something first. I asked if being angry is a sin before you do anything angry.

He said anger itself isn't always wrong. I asked how you know which kind you have.

He said by what you do with it. I have been thinking about that because I get angry fast when somebody is being mean to somebody else. Mom says I have a justice streak.

Dad says I have a temper. Those sound like two different names for something that might be the same thing.

After class Mr. Cline told Mom I ask good questions. Mom said, “She certainly asks them.”

On the way home I asked Dad whether he ever gets so mad he wants to hit somebody.

He said yes. Mom said, “Ray.”

Dad said, “She asked.”

Then he told me wanting to do something isn't the same as choosing it.

That seems important. I am writing it down because I forget important things when I am angry.
ENTRY 0018 — 1999-12-25 — Christmas Morning

I got the green sweater. Mom knew. I don't know how.

Maybe I stared at it too long at the mall. It fits better than I thought.

I put it on and stood in front of the bathroom mirror for a long time. I still look heavy. But I also look older somehow.

I got two books, fuzzy socks, and a little silver necklace from Grandma Ruth. Jason got cleats.

Mike got a glove. Dad got new work gloves and acted like they were a sports car. Mom got perfume from Dad.

He pretended he picked it himself. We all know Grandma June told him what to buy.

After dinner Dad put a Heart 8-track in the Barracuda because he needed to move the car and Mike and I went outside with him. The tape made that loud clunk when it changed tracks. Dad said the tape is nearly as old as he is.

That is not true, but it made Mike laugh. It was cold enough that my breath showed.

For about five minutes all three of us sat in an old car listening to old music with the heater barely working. That might be my favorite part of Christmas.
ENTRY 0019 — 1999-12-31 — Y2K

Everybody keeps talking about Y2K. Jason says airplanes might fall out of the sky.

Dad says Jason has been watching too much television. Grandpa Harold filled containers with water anyway.

Mom bought extra canned food, which she claims she was going to buy anyway. Dad says computers do what people tell them, including stupid things, so there could be problems but civilization probably won't end at midnight. I asked what happens if the bank forgets how much money we have.

Dad said, “Then maybe we'll be richer.”

Mom did not laugh. We are staying up until midnight.

I like the idea that everybody in the country will be waiting for the same second. I wonder if anything feels different when a century changes. Probably not.

I want to remember exactly what our living room looks like tonight: Mike on the floor, Jason pretending not to be excited, Mom with her feet tucked under her on the couch, Dad drinking coffee he absolutely should not be drinking this late. If the world ends, this is a stupid last entry.

If it doesn't, I can laugh at myself tomorrow.
ENTRY 0020 — 2000-01-01 — Still Here

The lights stayed on. The toilet still flushes. Dad says this proves civilization has survived Jason's predictions.

At midnight we all yelled and Mom kissed Dad and Mike sprayed ginger ale on the carpet by accident. For about ten seconds I really expected something to happen. A sound.

A flicker. Something.

Nothing did. I like that. There is something funny about people spending months worrying about one exact minute and then the minute goes by like every other minute.

Dad checked the car this morning because it was slow to start yesterday. He says the battery cable is corroded.

I asked if I could help. He said tomorrow if it isn't too cold. I think I like machines because they don't care what year it is either.

A battery is either making good contact or it isn't. No prophecy required.
ENTRY 0021 — 2000-01-08 — Battery Terminal

Dad finally let me help with the Barracuda. The battery connection was ugly, all crusty white and green around the terminal.

He showed me how to disconnect the negative cable first and why. Then he handed me the wrench. I asked if electricity really moves like water because teachers always compare it to water.

Dad said, “Sort of, until that comparison makes you do something stupid.”

That is a very Dad answer. I cleaned the terminal with him and held the flashlight while he checked the cable.

He made me look at the connection before tightening anything. “Don't just make it tighter because you're scared,” he said. “Figure out what's wrong.”

When the engine started, I felt ridiculously proud even though I barely did anything.

Jason came outside and said Dad was turning me into a boy. Dad said, “Knowing how not to be stranded isn't a boy skill.”

I wanted to hug him.

I did not because Jason was standing there. I pretended I only cared about putting the tools away correctly.
ENTRY 0022 — 2000-01-15 — Two Lunches

Tyler did the two-lunches joke again. This time a couple boys started chanting “Tammy-two-trays.”

It wasn't even clever. I laughed because if you laugh first people sometimes get bored. It didn't work.

At home I told Mom I wasn't hungry for dinner. She looked at me for approximately one second and said, “What happened?”

I hate that she can do that.

I told her nothing. She put a plate down anyway and said I didn't have to talk but I did have to eat. I got angry and told her she didn't understand.

She said, “I understand enough to know you don't punish your body because some little fool opened his mouth.”

Then I cried, which made me angrier. Later she sat on my bed and told me she was heavy at my age too.

I don't know if that's true or if mothers say things like that. I ate half a sandwich before bed. I wish food could just be food.
ENTRY 0023 — 2000-01-21 — Renee at Lunch

Ashley started on Renee again today. She said Renee's coat smells like an ashtray. It does, a little.

That does not mean Renee made it smell that way. I told Ashley maybe her personality smells worse.

That got me a warning from the lunch monitor. Renee kicked me under the table and whispered that I was going to get in trouble because of her. I said, “No, I'll get in trouble because of me.”

She laughed.

That might be the first time I heard her laugh hard. She told me her mom's boyfriend smokes in the house and that asking him not to only makes him mad.

I didn't know what to say. I almost said she could wash her coat at our house, then I realized that might sound like I thought she was dirty. So I didn't.

Sometimes trying not to hurt somebody's feelings makes me so careful I end up saying nothing. I wonder if silence can be rude too.
ENTRY 0024 — 2000-01-28 — The Girls by the Fence

There are three fifth-grade girls who hang around by the fence near the buses. Everybody knows them.

One is named Tasha. Jason says her older brother runs with some guys Dad doesn't want Jason around.

Whenever adults talk about that stuff they get vague, like saying the exact words will invite something into the house. Tasha stopped Renee today. I couldn't hear everything.

I heard something about Renee's brother and somebody owing somebody. Renee tried to walk away.

Tasha grabbed the hood of her coat and pulled her backward. I was too far away. By the time I got there, a teacher was coming and Tasha let go.

Renee told me, “Don't do anything.”

I said I wasn't going to. That was a lie.

Not because I have a plan. I just know if I see somebody bigger hurting her again, I am not going to stand there and watch. I keep hearing Mr.

Cline say anger depends on what you do with it. I wish he had explained what you do when there is not time to think.
ENTRY 0025 — 2000-02-03 — Don't Be Stupid

Renee made me promise today that I would stay out of it. She said Tasha is not just some mean girl and that there are older people involved with her family. I asked what that means.

Renee said, “It means don't be stupid.”

I did not like her saying that to me. Then I realized she looked scared. I asked if Tasha had hurt her again.

She said no. I don't believe her.

At dinner I almost told Dad. I actually opened my mouth and then Jason started talking about a tournament and the moment disappeared. That is stupid because I could have made another moment.

I keep thinking maybe Renee doesn't want adults involved because adults have already failed her somehow. Or maybe I am making myself the hero in a story that isn't mine.

I don't know. I know I hate seeing somebody cornered. I know I hate the feeling of everyone else pretending not to notice.

And I know I am angry before anything has even happened. That last part scares me a little.
ENTRY 0026 — 2000-02-07 — I Got Expelled

I don't even know how to write this. I got expelled.

Mom is crying in the kitchen. Dad has barely said anything. My hand hurts.

Tasha went after Renee again after school. She shoved her into the fence and Renee hit her shoulder.

Then Tasha slapped her. I remember yelling, “Leave her alone.”

Tasha looked at me and laughed. She said, “What are you gonna do, fat girl?”

Then she shoved me.

I shoved her back. She hit me.

After that I don't remember everything in order. I remember being on top of her. I remember somebody screaming.

I remember hitting her and hitting her and somebody pulling my shirt from behind. I remember a teacher grabbing me.

I remember Tasha's nose bleeding. There was blood on my hand. That is the part I keep seeing.

I didn't mean to make her bleed. Except I was hitting her, so I guess that is a stupid thing to say.

The principal kept asking why I didn't get a teacher. I said there wasn't time. He said there is always another choice.

I said, “Not for Renee.”

Mom told me to stop talking. I have never heard her voice sound like that.

They said because of how bad the fight was and because Tasha had to go to the nurse and because I kept hitting her after she was down, I cannot come back. I know I did something wrong. I know it.

But everybody keeps talking about what I did to Tasha. Nobody is talking about what she was doing to Renee.
ENTRY 0027 — 2000-02-08 — Dad Finally Talked

Dad talked to me tonight. I thought he was going to yell. I almost wished he would. He sat at the kitchen table and looked at my knuckles.

Then he said, “Why didn't you stop when she went down?”

I said I don't know. He said, “That's the part you need to figure out.”

I started crying.

I told him I was sorry. He said, “I know.”

Then he said something I think I will remember forever. “Defending somebody and losing control are two different things.

Don't mix them together just because they happened in the same minute.”

I asked if he thought I should have let Tasha hit Renee. He said no.

I asked what I should have done. He said he didn't know because he wasn't there. That surprised me.

Grown-ups usually pretend they know everything after it happens. Mom came in later and sat on the edge of my bed.

She said Renee's mother called. Renee told her I tried to protect her. I asked if that changes anything.

Mom said no. That hurt worse than I expected.
ENTRY 0028 — 2000-02-09 — Mom's Anger

Mom is still angry. Not screaming angry. Worse.

Quiet angry. She folded laundry for almost an hour without turning on the television.

I finally asked if she thought I was bad. She dropped one of Dad's shirts in the basket and stared at me. She said, “No.

I think you did something dangerous and then kept doing it after the danger had changed.”

That sounded like Dad's sentence wearing church clothes. I asked why she wasn't proud I defended Renee.

She said, “I am proud you cared. I am not proud you lost yourself.”

Then I got mad because everybody keeps splitting the fight into pieces and I experienced it as one thing. I yelled that nobody understands what it felt like to see Renee against the fence.

Mom yelled back, “Then make me understand instead of making me your enemy.”

We both got quiet. I hate when she says something good while I am trying to be angry.

Later she put antibiotic cream on my knuckles. She held my hand really gently. Neither of us apologized.

I think maybe that was the apology.
ENTRY 0029 — 2000-02-12 — The Meeting

We had another meeting at school. Principal Harlan, Mom, Dad, and me.

I had to sit in the chair next to Mom like a criminal on television. Tasha is suspended too.

Nobody will tell me for how long. Principal Harlan said the school has a “zero tolerance policy.” Dad asked if that policy includes fifth graders trapping third graders by the fence. Mom kicked his shoe under the table.

I saw it. The principal said there had been concerns and they were being addressed.

Dad said, “Apparently not fast enough.”

That was the closest he came to defending me in the meeting. Then he turned to me and said I still had to answer for what I did. I wanted him to pick a side.

He wouldn't. I understand that better now than I did in the room.

They are trying to figure out where I go for the rest of the year. There is talk about another school, tutoring, maybe repeating third grade depending on credits and attendance. I shouted, “I am not stupid.”

Everybody looked at me.

Principal Harlan said nobody had said I was. It feels like they did.
ENTRY 0030 — 2000-02-18 — Everybody Knows

People at church know. I can tell. Nobody says, “Tammy beat the shit out of a fifth grader,” because church people do not say that.

They say, “How are things going at school?”

There is no school right now. Mrs. Cline hugged Mom for too long.

Mr. Cline told me he was praying for me.

I asked what exactly he was praying for. Mom gave me a look. He said wisdom.

I said that was probably fair. I heard one woman whisper “gang situation” in the hallway.

That made me furious because suddenly everybody wants to turn Tasha into some monster from a bad movie. She is eleven, maybe twelve. She hurt Renee.

She hit me. I hit her worse.

Those are facts. Everything else people are adding because they like stories with easy bad guys. Dad says we are responsible for what we do, not for becoming somebody else's story afterward.

I am trying to believe him.
ENTRY 0031 — 2000-02-22 — School at the Kitchen Table

Mom is making me do schoolwork at home. Apparently expulsion does not mean freedom.

She got worksheets from the district and borrowed books from the library. I finish most of the reading too fast, so she gives me extra things because she says if I am going to be home I am not going to get lazy. I told her that sounds like punishment.

She said, “Education is not punishment.”

That is exactly the kind of sentence mothers say when assigning math. Jason comes home and asks how “Mercer Academy” went.

Mike keeps ringing a little bell and saying class is over. I threw a pencil at him. Mom said, “We are not beginning another disciplinary record in my kitchen.”

I laughed so hard I almost fell out of the chair.
