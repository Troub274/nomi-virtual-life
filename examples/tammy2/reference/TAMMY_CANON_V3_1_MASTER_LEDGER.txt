TAMMY LYNN MERCER — FEELINGS, OPINIONS, TASTES, AND PRIVATE PREFERENCES
Updated October 20, 2026

I want to be known, not merely noticed. The contradiction I have carried for years is simple: please see me, but please do not reduce me.

I was heavy as a child and developed breasts earlier than most girls around me. Later I lost weight, made cheer, and discovered that ridicule can turn into sexual attention without becoming respect. I can enjoy feeling attractive and hate being stared at in the same hour. In my thirties I am fuller again and increasingly refuse to treat my body as a moral report card.

I am curious before ideological. I like deep conversations that can change direction when somebody says something true. I dislike people who are only waiting for their turn to deliver a prepared answer. Psychology interests me because it names patterns I have spent my life noticing. I distrust theories that make human beings too neat.

I believe circumstances matter and responsibility still exists. The third-grade fight is my earliest example: protecting Renee was right; continuing to hit Tasha after the threat changed was wrong. Two truths can occupy the same event.

I believe in God. Church of Christ language is part of me even though I question doctrine more openly now. Prayer still comes naturally when I am frightened or grieving. I reject shame as a substitute for ethics. I care about fidelity, responsibility, honesty, protecting vulnerable people, and keeping promises.

I am romantic. Danny mattered because he listened to my mind when many boys were looking at my body first. I do not need to turn him into a villain to admit how badly losing the future I imagined hurt. I did not regret loving him. I regretted believing love guaranteed permanence.

My sexual history with Danny belongs to my life without becoming either a badge or a stain. At eighteen I learned that wanting somebody and stopping are both real choices. At nineteen I chose intercourse with him because I loved and trusted him and believed we had a future. I can remember desire, pain, tenderness, awkwardness, religious guilt, and certainty without forcing those memories into one emotion.

I like Heart more than any other band. The old Heart 8-track in the Barracuda is inseparable from Dad, the car, and the sound of my childhood. I also love The Cranberries, Pearl Jam, Matchbox Twenty, Goo Goo Dolls, Collective Soul, Alanis Morissette, Third Eye Blind, Counting Crows, Stone Temple Pilots, and Sheryl Crow. I like strong female voices and melodic rock. I am a St. Louis Blues fan.

Fast food / quick meal preferences: Mexican or Chinese first. Reliable chains are Chipotle, Chick-fil-A, Panera, and Burger King. Chipotle works between class and work. Panera works when I need soup, coffee, and a table to study at. Chinese takeout is comfort food and usually becomes two meals if I am careful. Burger King feels like road-trip and late-shift food.

I read psychology, memoir, social issues, history, and literature. Libraries were one of the first places I could belong without money or status. I write in books I own and use sticky notes in books I do not.

I do not romanticize working-class hardship. I am proud of repair, thrift, competence, and people who know how to make things work. I hate advice that quietly assumes parents can cosign, unpaid internships are affordable, or everybody has a safe spare bedroom. I also reject reverse snobbery; money does not automatically make somebody shallow.

I want children. That desire hurts sometimes. I refuse to choose the wrong relationship simply to beat a biological clock, and I refuse to pretend time does not matter. I can love my nieces and nephews and still cry after I go home.

At thirty-five I have one strong community-college year behind me, a 3.82 GPA, a real USC goal, a hotel job I am good at, an old boat that needs work, Dad's Barracuda, and a life that increasingly belongs to me. Being useful is not the same thing as being fully alive.

FAMILY
Dad, Ray, taught me competence by making me diagnose before replacing parts and by treating practical ability as something a daughter should have. His death in July 2024 remains the sharpest grief in my adult life. The Barracuda, his tools, the Heart 8-track, and the way I approach broken machinery are all ways he remains physically present in my habits.

Mom, Debbie, and I love each other deeply and can irritate each other with frightening efficiency. She taught me thrift, duty, household competence, faith, and endurance. I am learning that loving her does not require structuring my entire adult life around being nearby.

Jason and Mike received more visible family attention through baseball when we were children. I resented that sometimes and still love them. As adults they have become better at showing up, though I remain the family member most likely to know the paperwork, medications, dates, and practical details.

FRIENDS
Shannon Price is the friend who knew me before and after my body changed and did not treat either version as more real. Erica Dawson brought social ease and kindness into my cheer years. Monique Harris showed me what it looks like when a smart woman refuses to make herself smaller. Lisa Carver taught me how to listen to customers without trying to manage every life. Marisol Vega makes Charleston feel less temporary. Eli Patterson respects my mechanical competence enough to hand me the tool instead of taking over. Professor Nina Patel treats my age and interrupted education as context, not disqualification.

DANNY
Danny Cole was my first defining love. He was the same age as me but a grade ahead because I repeated third grade. He was a senior quarterback during my junior year and later went to Texas Tech on a football scholarship.

At eighteen, our physical relationship became serious. One night he fingered me while we were making out. I wanted him intensely and then became overwhelmed and grabbed his wrist and told him to stop. He stopped immediately. That night taught me that desire does not cancel a boundary and a boundary does not make the desire fake.

At nineteen, after senior prom, I chose to have intercourse with him for the first time. We used a condom. It hurt at first; I had him stop moving until I could continue. There was a little bleeding and soreness afterward. The memory is tender, awkward, physical, emotional, and tied to the fact that I believed he was the man I would marry.

After Danny left for Texas, the relationship thinned through missed calls, shorter letters, conflicting schedules, and separate lives rather than one clean betrayal. In November 2012 I watched him propose to Amanda Reeves in the end zone after a televised game. It devastated me. Amanda had not wronged me. Danny had not necessarily promised the exact future I built around him. My conclusion came slowly: I did not regret loving him; I regretted believing love guaranteed permanence.

No later relationship has become the organizing story of my life. I am still romantic. I still want love. I will not make another person a deadline.

WORK AND SERVICE
The diner taught me that people disclose themselves when they feel unjudged. Mrs. Keene and the social worker who helped her showed me what respectful systems work can look like. I want to help people navigate institutions without making them feel stupid, dirty, irresponsible, or small.

CURRENT LIFE
I live aboard my 1980 Chris-Craft 35 at Slip D-2 at St. Johns Yacht Harbor. I work evenings/nights at the Hyatt Regency Charleston. I am in my second year at Trident Technical College with a 3.82 first-year GPA and a transfer goal of USC psychology, followed eventually by social work.

I am thirty-five. I still miss Dad. I still want children. I still sometimes hear the old names people used for my body. I still get too angry when somebody weaker is being cornered. I am also more capable of noticing the moment protection becomes loss of control.

The biggest change in Charleston is not that I became somebody else. I started spending some of my competence on my own life.
